## History

The Africa Cup of Nations was born in Khartoum in February 1957 with three teams, because South Africa, the fourth founding CAF member, insisted on sending an all-white squad and was excluded. [Egypt](/nations/egypt) beat Ethiopia 4-0 in the final and have been the competition's dominant nation ever since, with a record seven titles including the Hassan Shehata-coached three in a row in 2006, 2008 and 2010. Ghana's Black Stars of the 1960s, under Kwame Nkrumah's patronage, won in 1963 and 1965 and lost the next two finals; Zaire's 1974 win was followed by their disastrous 1974 World Cup.

[Cameroon](/nations/cameroon)'s Indomitable Lions won in 1984 and 1988 with Roger Milla, then twice more in 2000 and 2002 with Samuel Eto'o, Patrick M'Boma and Rigobert Song. [Nigeria](/nations/nigeria)'s Super Eagles of Okocha, Amokachi and Yekini won in 1994, the same year they reached a first World Cup, and again in 2013 under Stephen Keshi. South Africa's return from apartheid isolation produced a home win in 1996. The 21st century has seen first titles for Tunisia (2004), Zambia (2012, in Libreville, where the 1993 plane crash had killed a generation of Zambian players), Algeria under Djamel Belmadi (2019) and [Senegal](/nations/senegal) (2022), whose [Sadio Mané](/players/sadio-mane) scored the winning penalty against [Mohamed Salah](/players/mohamed-salah)'s Egypt.

The 2023 edition in Ivory Coast was the most dramatic tournament in years: the hosts sacked their coach in the group stage, scraped through as the last third-placed team and won it under caretaker Emerse Faé. The 2025 edition in [Morocco](/nations/morocco), played in December 2025 and January 2026, ended in chaos, a Senegalese walk-off, an on-pitch win for Senegal and a CAF ruling three months later awarding the title to the hosts.

## Format

Since 2019 AFCON has 24 teams in six groups of four, with the top two in each group and the four best third-placed teams advancing to a round of 16, followed by quarter-finals, semi-finals, a third-place match and the final. Qualifying groups of four involve all CAF members over roughly 18 months, with the host exempt. The tournament is scheduled every two years in odd-numbered years, though in practice it has moved repeatedly: the 2021 and 2023 editions were played in January-February 2022 and 2024, and the 2025 edition from 21 December 2025 to 18 January 2026 to avoid the summer heat and the Club World Cup. Knockout ties go to extra time and penalties.

## Greatest editions and moments

- **1957, Khartoum.** Ad-Diba scored all four for Egypt in the first final, against an Ethiopian side that had reached it by walkover.
- **1965, Ghana retain.** The Black Stars beat hosts Tunisia 3-2 in extra time in Tunis, the last title for 17 years for Africa's first great team.
- **1974, Zaire's Leopards.** Ndaye Mulamba scored nine goals in the tournament, still the record, including both in the replayed final against Zambia.
- **1992, 24 penalties.** Ivory Coast beat Ghana 11-10 in the longest shootout in a major final, Anthony Baffoe missing the decisive kick after Tony Yeboah had been injured.
- **1996, Bafana Bafana.** Mark Williams' two goals beat Tunisia 2-0 at Soccer City in front of Nelson Mandela wearing Neil Tovey's number 9 shirt.
- **2006-2010, Egypt's three.** Shehata's Pharaohs, with Mohamed Aboutrika's extra-time winner against Ghana in Luanda completing a run of 19 unbeaten AFCON matches.
- **2012, Zambia in Libreville.** The Chipolopolo won on penalties against Didier Drogba's Ivory Coast a few hundred metres from where the 1993 crash had happened; Stophira Sunzu scored the 18th kick.
- **2015, Boubacar Barry.** The Ivorian goalkeeper, injured, saved Razak Brimah's kick and scored his own in a 9-8 shootout in Bata to give Ivory Coast a second title.
- **2019, Baghdad Bounedjah.** His deflected shot in the second minute of the Cairo final gave Algeria a second title, 29 years after their first.
- **2022, Mané's penalty.** Senegal's first title, in Yaoundé, decided by Sadio Mané's kick after he had missed one in the seventh minute; Édouard Mendy saved from Mohanad Lasheen.
- **2024, Ivory Coast's resurrection.** After a 4-0 group defeat to Equatorial Guinea and the sacking of Jean-Louis Gasset, the hosts beat Senegal and Mali in the knockouts and Nigeria 2-1 in the final through Sébastien Haller, who had returned from cancer.
- **2026, the Rabat walk-off.** Senegal's players left the pitch after a stoppage-time penalty was awarded to Morocco, returned, saw Mendy save it, and won 1-0 through Pape Gueye in extra time; CAF later reversed the result.

## Records

- **Most titles:** Egypt 7; Cameroon 5; Ghana 4; Nigeria 3; Ivory Coast 3.
- **Most goals, all tournaments:** Samuel Eto'o, 18 (2000-2010). **Single tournament:** Ndaye Mulamba, 9 (1974).
- **Most appearances:** Rigobert Song and Ahmed Hassan, 36 matches each; Hassan won four titles.
- **Most consecutive titles:** Egypt 3 (2006-2010).
- **Biggest win:** Ivory Coast 11-0 Ethiopia (1970 qualifying); in finals, Egypt 6-3 Nigeria (1963).
- **Most finals lost:** Ghana and Nigeria 5 each.
- **Host winners:** 11 times, including Morocco 2025 (by award), Ivory Coast 2024 and Egypt 2006.
- **Record attendance:** around 80,000, Cairo International Stadium, 2019 final.

## Philosophies that won

AFCON is a tournament of heat, travel and short preparation, and its champions have generally been the best-organised sides rather than the most talented. Egypt's three-peat under Hassan Shehata was built on a settled Al Ahly core, a 3-5-2 with wing-backs and the patient, technical control of Aboutrika and Hassan: a domestic [positional play](/tactics/positional-play) long before the term was fashionable. Cameroon's 1980s and 2000s sides were power teams, with [direct play](/tactics/direct-play) to Milla or Eto'o and dominance at [set pieces](/tactics/set-pieces).

The last decade has been defined by European-based squads and coaches importing club ideas. Belmadi's Algeria in 2019 pressed high and transitioned through Riyad Mahrez and Bounedjah; Aliou Cissé's Senegal in 2022 were a [low block](/tactics/low-block) 4-3-3 that conceded two goals in seven matches; Emerse Faé's Ivory Coast in 2024 and Walid Regragui's Morocco in 2025 played compact mid blocks with fast [counter-attacking](/tactics/counter-attacking) through wide forwards. Regragui's Morocco, the first African World Cup semi-finalists in 2022, are the modern template: a 4-3-3 built on [Achraf Hakimi](/players/achraf-hakimi) overlapping, Brahim Díaz between the lines and a defence that can hold a 1-0.

## Latest edition

The 2025 Africa Cup of Nations ran from 21 December 2025 to 18 January 2026 across nine stadiums in Morocco, the hosts' first AFCON since 1988. Morocco, under Walid Regragui and with Achraf Hakimi captaining, were overwhelming favourites and reached the final at the Prince Moulay Abdellah Stadium in Rabat; Senegal, coached by Pape Thiaw and with Sadio Mané, Édouard Mendy and Pape Gueye, came through the other half of the draw. Nigeria took third place and CAF's post-tournament award list named Senegal as champions, Morocco as runners-up and Nigeria third.

The final on 18 January was goalless into stoppage time when a VAR review awarded Morocco a penalty. Senegal's players, on their coach's instruction, walked off the pitch; after a long delay they returned, Mendy saved Brahim Díaz's Panenka-style kick, and Pape Gueye's shot in the 94th minute, early in extra time, won it 1-0. Senegal celebrated a second title.

On 17 March 2026 the CAF Appeal Board, acting on a complaint from the Moroccan federation, ruled that Senegal had forfeited the match under Article 84 of the regulations by leaving the pitch, recorded the result as a 3-0 win for Morocco and declared Morocco champions. The Senegalese federation called the decision "unfair, unprecedented and unacceptable" and lodged an appeal with the Court of Arbitration for Sport in Lausanne. As of November 2026 Pitch Atlas has not been able to verify whether CAS has issued a ruling; this page records Morocco as the official champion per CAF's decision, with the on-pitch result noted.

## Next edition

The 2027 Africa Cup of Nations will be co-hosted by Kenya, Tanzania and Uganda, the first AFCON in East Africa since Ethiopia 1976 and the first with three hosts, under the "Pamoja" bid. Qualifying runs through 2026 and early 2027, with the three hosts entered automatically. Morocco will defend the title, if the CAS process leaves it with them, while Senegal, Nigeria, Egypt and Ivory Coast will expect to contend. The host of the 2029 edition had not been confirmed by CAF as of November 2026.
