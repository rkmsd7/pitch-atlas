## History

CONCACAF had run a championship since 1963, mostly in Central America and usually ignored by [Mexico](/nations/mexico)'s full-strength team, before the confederation relaunched it as the Gold Cup in 1991 with a Los Angeles final and a clear commercial purpose: build American interest before the 1994 World Cup. The [United States](/nations/united-states) won that first edition on penalties against Honduras, and the competition has been held in the United States ever since, with occasional matches in Mexico, Canada, Costa Rica and Jamaica.

Mexico dominated the 1990s, winning in 1993, 1996 and 1998 with Luis García, Cuauhtémoc Blanco and Luis Hernández, and beating invited Brazilian sides in two finals. Canada's 2000 win under Holger Osieck, with Carlo Corazzin and Craig Forrest, remains the only title outside the big two. The 2000s swung towards the USA under Bruce Arena and Bob Bradley, who won four of five editions between 2002 and 2007, before Mexico's 5-0 rout of the Americans in the 2009 final at Giants Stadium and the 4-2 comeback from 2-0 down in Pasadena in 2011, Giovani dos Santos' chip over Tim Howard the signature image.

Since 2013 the trophy has alternated: the USA under Jürgen Klinsmann in 2013 and Arena in 2017, Mexico under Tata Martino in 2019 and Jaime Lozano in 2023, Gregg Berhalter's USA in 2021 with a squad of MLS players, and Mexico again under Javier Aguirre in 2025. Jamaica's two finals (2015, 2017) and Panama's three (2005, 2013, 2023) are the best results from the region's other nations.

## Format

The Gold Cup is held every two years in odd-numbered years, with 16 teams in four groups of four and the top two in each group advancing to quarter-finals, semi-finals and a final; there is no third-place match. Qualification has used the [Nations League](/tournaments/nations-league)-style CONCACAF Nations League since 2019, with the top-ranked sides qualifying directly and a preliminary play-off round deciding the last places. Guest nations from CONMEBOL and elsewhere were invited between 1991 and 2005 and again in 2021 (Qatar) and 2023 (Qatar); Saudi Arabia was the guest in 2025. Knockout ties go to extra time and penalties. The tournament is staged in even years only when combined with other events; in World Cup years it is not played.

## Greatest editions and moments

- **1991, the first.** The USA beat Mexico 2-0 in the semi-final and Honduras on penalties at the Coliseum, with Tony Meola saving from Juan Castro.
- **1993, Mexico 4-0 USA.** Zague scored twice at the Azteca in the first of 10 Mexican titles.
- **1996, Brazil's under-23s.** Mexico beat a Brazilian side containing Alex and Zé Elias 2-0 in the final; Ronaldo had played for Brazil the previous round.
- **2000, Canada.** Carlo Corazzin's goals and a 2-0 final win over Colombia in Los Angeles, with Jason de Vos heading the opener.
- **2002, Donovan's arrival.** A 20-year-old Landon Donovan was tournament MVP as the USA beat Costa Rica 2-0 in Pasadena.
- **2007, Chicago.** Benny Feilhaber's volley beat Mexico 2-1 in front of 60,000 at Soldier Field, the USA coming from behind.
- **2009, 5-0.** Mexico's heaviest Gold Cup final win, over a largely second-string American side, with Giovani dos Santos and Carlos Vela scoring.
- **2011, the Rose Bowl.** The USA led 2-0 after 23 minutes; Pablo Barrera scored twice, Andrés Guardado once and Dos Santos chipped the fourth from the byline.
- **2015, Panama's protests.** Mexico beat Panama in the semi-final with two contested penalties and Panamanian players staged a sit-down protest; Mexico then beat Jamaica 3-1.
- **2021, Miles Robinson.** A 117th-minute header from Kellyn Acosta's free-kick beat Mexico 1-0 in Las Vegas; Matt Turner saved everything.
- **2023, Giménez.** Santiago Giménez's 88th-minute goal beat Panama 1-0 at SoFi Stadium.
- **2025, Edson Álvarez.** Chris Richards' fourth-minute header, Raúl Jiménez's equaliser and Álvarez's 77th-minute winner, initially ruled offside and awarded by VAR, in front of 70,925 in Houston.

## Records

- **Most titles:** Mexico 10; United States 7; Canada 1.
- **Finals between Mexico and the USA:** 8, Mexico leading 6-2.
- **Most goals, all tournaments:** Landon Donovan, 18 (2002-2013). **Single tournament:** Luis Roberto Alves "Zague", 11 (1993).
- **Most appearances:** Andrés Guardado, 35 matches across eight tournaments; Donovan and Guardado share the most tournament MVP awards.
- **Biggest win:** Mexico 9-0 Martinique (1993); USA 8-1 Cuba (2008 qualifying era); Mexico 8-0 Cuba (2015).
- **Best result by a Caribbean nation:** Jamaica, runners-up 2015 and 2017.
- **Record attendance:** 93,420, Rose Bowl, 2011 final.
- **Most finals lost:** United States 6; Panama 3.

## Philosophies that won

The Gold Cup has been a battle of two footballing cultures. Mexico's titles have come from a possession-based 4-3-3 or 4-2-3-1 rooted in Liga MX's technical tradition and the Argentine coaching school, from Ricardo La Volpe's [positional play](/tactics/positional-play) principles in the 2000s to Tata Martino's structured [tiki-taka](/tactics/tiki-taka) in 2019 and Javier Aguirre's pragmatic [low block](/tactics/low-block) and transition game in 2025, built on Edson Álvarez as a destroyer and Raúl Jiménez holding the ball up.

The American approach has been more physical and vertical: Bruce Arena's 4-4-2 with wide runners and dominance at [set pieces](/tactics/set-pieces) won in 2002, 2005 and 2007; Klinsmann's 2013 team pressed higher and played faster; Berhalter's 2021 side combined possession ideas from his Columbus Crew days with [counter-attacking](/tactics/counter-attacking) through Paul Arriola and Gyasi Zardes. Mauricio Pochettino's 2025 USA played a front-foot 4-2-3-1 with [pressing traps](/tactics/pressing-traps) that reached the final with a young, largely MLS-based squad before losing to Mexico's superior game management. The lesson of 34 years is that Mexico wins when its midfield controls tempo and the USA wins when it turns the match into a transitional sprint.

## Latest edition

The 2025 Gold Cup ran from 14 June to 6 July, overlapping with the Club World Cup and played across 14 venues in the United States and Canada, with 16 teams including guest Saudi Arabia. Both finalists arrived under scrutiny. Mexico, under Javier Aguirre in his third spell, had won the 2024-25 Nations League in March; the United States, under Mauricio Pochettino, were without Christian Pulisic, Weston McKennie and Antonee Robinson and had lost four friendlies in a row before the tournament.

Mexico topped their group with wins over the Dominican Republic and Suriname and a draw with Costa Rica, then beat Saudi Arabia 2-0 in the quarter-final and Honduras 1-0 in the semi-final through Raúl Jiménez's goal at Levi's Stadium. The USA won their group with three wins, beat Costa Rica on penalties in the quarter-final (Matt Freese saving three kicks) and Guatemala 2-1 in the semi-final with two early goals from 19-year-old Diego Luna.

The final at NRG Stadium in Houston on 6 July was Mexico's by the end. Chris Richards headed the USA in front from Sebastian Berhalter's free-kick in the fourth minute, Jiménez equalised in the 27th after Marcel Ruiz's pass split the defence, and Edson Álvarez headed Johan Vásquez's flick-on past Freese in the 77th minute; the assistant flagged offside, but VAR reversed the call. Mexico retained the trophy for the first time since 2011, their 10th title, and Álvarez was named player of the tournament. The sellout crowd of 70,925 was overwhelmingly Mexican. Both teams went on to the 2026 World Cup as co-hosts.

## Next edition

The 2027 Gold Cup is expected in June and July 2027 in the United States, with the possibility of matches in Canada and Mexico, though CONCACAF had not formally announced host venues as of November 2026. Qualification runs through the 2026-27 CONCACAF Nations League. Mexico will defend a record 10th title; the United States, after a home World Cup, will have a new cycle to build towards the 2028 Olympics and the 2030 World Cup. Canada, who won the 2000 edition and were quarter-finalists in 2025, remain the most credible third force under Jesse Marsch.
