## History

The Copa Libertadores de América was created in 1960, five years after the European Cup, partly because UEFA wanted a South American champion to play in the Intercontinental Cup. Seven league champions entered and Peñarol of Montevideo won the first two editions, before [Pelé](/players/pele)'s Santos won in 1962 and 1963, beating Peñarol in a play-off in Buenos Aires and [Boca Juniors](/clubs/boca-juniors) at the Maracanã. The 1960s and 1970s were Argentine: Independiente's *Rey de Copas* won seven titles, four in a row from 1972 to 1975, Estudiantes de La Plata three in a row under Osvaldo Zubeldía with the ruthless, cynical *anti-fútbol* that also produced the infamous 1969 Intercontinental Cup against AC Milan, and Boca won back-to-back in 1977 and 1978.

Brazilian clubs, who had sometimes refused to enter in protest at the calendar and the violence, became a force in the 1980s: [Flamengo](/clubs/flamengo) with Zico in 1981, Grêmio in 1983 and then São Paulo's back-to-back under Telê Santana in 1992 and 1993. Colombia's Atlético Nacional in 1989, Chile's Colo-Colo in 1991, Ecuador's LDU Quito in 2008 and Colombia's Once Caldas in 2004 (beating Boca on penalties) spread the trophy. Boca under Carlos Bianchi, with Juan Román Riquelme and Martín Palermo, won in 2000, 2001, 2003 and 2007, and [River Plate](/clubs/river-plate) under Marcelo Gallardo in 2015 and 2018, the latter a *Superclásico* final moved to Madrid after the Boca bus was attacked outside the Monumental.

Since the switch to a single-match final in 2019 the competition has been Brazilian: Flamengo under Jorge Jesus in 2019 (two Gabriel Barbosa goals in the last three minutes against River in Lima), [Palmeiras](/clubs/palmeiras) under Abel Ferreira in 2020 and 2021, Flamengo again in 2022, Fluminense in 2023 with Marcelo and Germán Cano, [Botafogo](/clubs/botafogo) in 2024 with 10 men after a first-minute red card, and Flamengo in 2025 under Filipe Luís, a fourth title that made them Brazil's most successful club in the competition. The 2026 final in Montevideo breaks the pattern of all-Brazilian finals: Palmeiras face Estudiantes, back in a final after 17 years.

## Format

The Libertadores has 47 clubs from the 10 CONMEBOL nations. Four qualifying rounds of two-legged ties reduce the field to 32, who join the 28 direct entrants (four or five from Brazil and Argentina, three or fewer from the others, plus the holders and the Copa Sudamericana winner) in eight groups of four. Group winners and runners-up go to a two-legged round of 16, quarter-finals and semi-finals, seeded by group performance and with the away-goals rule abolished in 2022; third-placed clubs drop into the Copa Sudamericana. Since 2019 the final has been a single match at a pre-selected neutral venue, played in late November. Prize money has grown sharply, with the 2025 winners receiving over $30m in total.

## Greatest editions and moments

- **1962, Santos v Peñarol.** The second leg in Montevideo was abandoned amid crowd trouble and awarded; Pelé scored twice in the 3-0 play-off in Buenos Aires.
- **1963, Pelé at the Bombonera.** Santos won 2-1 in Boca's stadium to complete a 5-3 aggregate, the only time a Brazilian club has won a final leg there.
- **1968, Estudiantes.** A provincial club, Zubeldía's *Pincharratas* beat Palmeiras in a play-off in Montevideo, the first of three straight titles.
- **1975, Independiente's four in a row.** Ricardo Bochini's 3-1 second leg against Unión Española at the Doble Visera before a 2-0 play-off in Asunción.
- **1981, Zico in Santiago.** Flamengo beat Cobreloa 2-0 in the play-off, Zico scoring both, a week before the famous 3-0 over Liverpool in Tokyo.
- **1989, Higuita's saves.** René Higuita saved four penalties as Atlético Nacional beat Olimpia 5-4 on penalties in Bogotá, Colombia's first title.
- **2000, Riquelme's nutmeg.** Boca beat Palmeiras on penalties in São Paulo; Riquelme's crossed-ball nutmeg of Zé Roberto is folklore.
- **2004, Once Caldas.** The Manizales club beat Boca on penalties with four saves from Juan Carlos Henao after a 1-1 aggregate.
- **2008, LDU Quito.** Ecuador's first title, winning the shootout at the Maracanã after Fluminense had won the second leg 3-1.
- **2018, Madrid.** River 3-1 Boca after extra time at the Bernabéu, Juan Fernando Quintero's extra-time strike and Gonzalo Martínez's goal into an empty net.
- **2019, Lima.** River led 1-0 until the 89th minute; Gabriel Barbosa scored twice, in the 89th and 92nd, and was sent off celebrating.
- **2024, Botafogo with 10.** Gregore was sent off after 30 seconds; Luiz Henrique and Alex Telles scored in a 3-1 win over Atlético Mineiro in Buenos Aires.
- **2025, Danilo's header.** Flamengo's 1-0 over Palmeiras in Lima, the former Juventus defender heading in a corner in the 67th minute.

## Records

- **Most titles:** Independiente 7; Boca Juniors 6; Peñarol 5; Estudiantes, River Plate and Flamengo 4; Olimpia, Nacional, São Paulo, Grêmio, Santos, Palmeiras, Internacional and Cruzeiro among the multiple winners.
- **Most titles by country:** Brazil 24, Argentina 25 (Argentina leads, Brazil has won seven of the last eight).
- **Most consecutive titles:** Independiente 4 (1972-75); Estudiantes 3 (1968-70).
- **Most goals, all-time:** Alberto Spencer (Peñarol, Barcelona SC), 54.
- **Most goals in one edition:** Daniel Onega, 17 (1966).
- **Most titles as a coach:** Carlos Bianchi, 4 (Vélez 1994, Boca 2000, 2001, 2003).
- **Biggest final margin:** Boca 5-0 Grêmio on aggregate (2007).
- **Most finals lost:** Boca Juniors 6; Peñarol 5.
- **Only unbeaten champions in the modern format:** Flamengo 2019, Palmeiras 2021.

## Philosophies that won

The Libertadores has a reputation for cynicism that comes from the 1960s: Zubeldía's Estudiantes won with man-marking, provocation, meticulous set-piece planning and the counter-attack, an early, ugly cousin of [catenaccio](/tactics/catenaccio). Independiente's four in a row were more technical, built on Bochini's playmaking. The great Brazilian sides, Santos with Pelé and Telê Santana's São Paulo, were the artistic exception, the ancestors of modern [relationism](/tactics/relationism): short passing, close support and improvisation between Raí, Cafu and Müller.

Carlos Bianchi's Boca sides played a disciplined 4-3-1-2 with Riquelme as the *enganche* behind two strikers and a [low block](/tactics/low-block) that was happy to concede possession, the template that most Argentine champions followed. Marcelo Gallardo's River were the aggressive alternative, pressing high and playing [counter-attacking](/tactics/counter-attacking) football from a 4-3-1-2 or 4-4-2 with constant rotation. The modern Brazilian winners have imported European ideas on European budgets: Jorge Jesus's Flamengo in 2019 played a Portuguese-style [positional play](/tactics/positional-play) 4-2-3-1 with a [high line](/tactics/high-line); Abel Ferreira's Palmeiras were pragmatic, transition-based, built on a strong goalkeeper (Weverton) and [set pieces](/tactics/set-pieces); Filipe Luís's 2025 Flamengo, with Jorginho, Giorgian de Arrascaeta and a 4-2-3-1 that dominated possession, were the most complete team on the continent.

## Latest edition

The 2025 Copa Libertadores was the seventh with a single-match final and the sixth consecutive won by a Brazilian club. Flamengo, who had sacked Tite in late 2024 and promoted club legend Filipe Luís from the under-20s, won the Brazilian title in 2025 and reached the final by beating Internacional in the round of 16, Estudiantes on penalties in the quarter-finals after a 2-2 aggregate, and Racing 1-0 on aggregate in a tense, physical semi-final decided at the Maracanã. Palmeiras, under Abel Ferreira in his sixth season, beat Universitario, River Plate 5-2 on aggregate and LDU Quito in the semi-final, overturning a 3-0 first-leg defeat with a 4-0 win at Allianz Parque, one of the great Libertadores comebacks.

The final at the Estadio Monumental in Lima on 29 November 2025 was the second all-Brazilian final between the two clubs after Palmeiras's 2-1 extra-time win in Montevideo in 2021. It was cagey and tactical, with Jorginho and Arrascaeta controlling Flamengo's possession and Palmeiras threatening through Vitor Roque and Felipe Anderson. In the 67th minute Arrascaeta's corner was met by Danilo, the former Juventus and Manchester City full-back playing as a centre-back, whose header beat Carlos Miguel. Palmeiras pressed late but could not find an equaliser. Flamengo's fourth title took them past Palmeiras, Grêmio, Santos and São Paulo as the most successful Brazilian club in the competition, and earned them a place at the 2029 Club World Cup and the FIFA Intercontinental Cup in December 2025.

## Next edition

The 2026 Copa Libertadores final will be played on 28 November 2026 at the Estadio Centenario in Montevideo, the venue of the first World Cup final, between Palmeiras and Estudiantes de La Plata. Palmeiras, seeking a fourth title after 1999, 2020 and 2021, are in their third final in six years; Estudiantes, four-time champions, are in their first final since Juan Sebastián Verón's winning side of 2009, and the first Argentine finalist since Boca in 2023. It ends a run of all-Brazilian finals and revives the oldest rivalry in the competition's history: the two clubs met in the 1968 final, which Estudiantes won in a play-off. The 2027 final venue had not been announced by CONMEBOL as of November 2026.
