## History

Women's football joined the Olympic programme in Atlanta in 1996, five years after the first Women's World Cup, and the [United States](/nations/united-states-women) won it in front of 76,481 at Sanford Stadium in Athens, Georgia, the largest crowd ever to watch a women's sporting event at the time. Because the women's tournament has no age restriction, it is a genuine second world championship, and for the USA it has often meant more: the 1999 World Cup generation of Hamm, Foudy, Lilly and Chastain closed their careers with gold in Athens in 2004, and Abby Wambach, Hope Solo and Carli Lloyd defined the 2008 and 2012 titles.

Norway's golden-goal win over the USA in Sydney in 2000, with Dagny Mellgren scoring in the 102nd minute, remains the only Olympic final the Americans have lost. Germany's 2016 gold in Rio was the swansong of coach Silvia Neid, and Canada's win in Tokyo, with Christine Sinclair at 38 and Jessie Fleming's penalty beating the USA in the semi-final, was the peak of a programme that had taken bronze at the two previous Games.

Paris 2024 brought the Americans back. Under new coach Emma Hayes, appointed only two months earlier, a rebuilt team with Trinity Rodman, Sophia Smith and Mallory Swanson beat Germany in extra time in the semi-final and [Brazil](/nations/brazil) 1-0 in the final at the Parc des Princes, a fifth gold and Marta's last international final.

## Format

The women's Olympic tournament is a senior competition with no age limits. From 2008 to 2024 it had 12 teams in three groups of four, with the top two in each group and the two best third-placed sides going to the quarter-finals. Los Angeles 2028 expands it to 16 teams in four groups of four, the first time the women's field is bigger than the men's.

Qualification works through the confederations: UEFA uses the Women's Nations League, CONCACAF its W Championship, CONMEBOL the Copa América Femenina, AFC a dedicated Olympic qualifying tournament, CAF a two-legged knockout series and OFC its Nations Cup. The host qualifies automatically. For 2028, CONCACAF receives up to four places if the USA reaches the semi-finals of the 2026 W Championship, and the AFC's third-placed team plays off against a CONMEBOL side.

## Greatest editions and moments

- **1996, Atlanta.** Tiffeny Milbrett's 68th-minute winner against China in front of 76,481, the first gold and the start of the tradition of US women's football as a stadium sport.
- **2000, Norway's golden goal.** Dagny Mellgren controlled with her arm and finished in the 102nd minute; the USA, who had led, lost an Olympic final for the only time.
- **2004, the farewell.** Abby Wambach's extra-time header beat Brazil 2-1 in Athens and sent Mia Hamm, Julie Foudy, Joy Fawcett and Brandi Chastain into retirement with gold.
- **2008, Carli Lloyd in Beijing.** Marta and Cristiane's Brazil dominated the final but Lloyd scored in the 96th minute. Hope Solo, dropped at the 2007 World Cup, was outstanding in goal.
- **2012, Alex Morgan at Old Trafford.** The USA v Canada semi-final (4-3, Morgan's header in the 123rd minute) is the best women's match ever played at the Olympics; Sinclair scored a hat-trick in defeat.
- **2016, Sweden's revenge.** Pia Sundhage, who had coached the USA to two golds, took Sweden past them on penalties in the quarter-finals before losing the final to Germany.
- **2020, Canada's gold.** Jessie Fleming's penalty beat the USA 1-0 in the semi-final and Julia Grosso converted the decisive kick in the shootout against Sweden in Yokohama.
- **2024, Hayes' rebuild.** Sophia Smith's extra-time goal beat Germany in the semi-final, Mallory Swanson's 57th-minute strike beat Brazil in the final, and Alyssa Naeher saved from Adriana to seal it.

## Records

- **Most golds:** United States 5 (1996, 2004, 2008, 2012, 2024). Norway, Germany and Canada 1.
- **Most medals:** United States 7 (5 gold, 1 silver, 1 bronze). Brazil 3 silvers (2004, 2008, 2024) without a gold.
- **All-time Olympic top scorer:** Cristiane of Brazil, 14 goals across four Games (2004-2016).
- **Most goals in one tournament:** Cristiane and Birgit Prinz, 5 (2004); Christine Sinclair, 6 (2012).
- **Oldest scorer:** Formiga, 41, and Christine Sinclair, 38, at Tokyo 2020.
- **Record attendance:** 80,203 at Wembley for the 2012 final, USA 2-1 Japan.
- **Only coach with two golds for the USA:** Pia Sundhage (2008, 2012), who later coached Sweden and Brazil against them.

## Philosophies that won

For most of its history the women's Olympic tournament belonged to the American model: superior fitness, [direct play](/tactics/direct-play) into fast forwards, dominance from [set pieces](/tactics/set-pieces) and a goalkeeper (Scurry, Solo, Naeher) who won tight matches. Norway's 2000 team was a disciplined 4-4-2 that out-fought the USA at their own game. Germany in 2016 under Silvia Neid won with the organisation and transition play of a [counter-attacking](/tactics/counter-attacking) side built on Melanie Behringer's set pieces.

Canada in 2021 under Bev Priestman were the purest [low block](/tactics/low-block) champions in the tournament's history, scoring only six goals in six matches and winning two shootouts. Emma Hayes' USA in 2024 were a different animal from the old American sides: a 4-3-3 with a front three of Rodman, Smith and Swanson playing off each other at speed, a midfield of Sam Coffey, Lindsey Horan and Korbin Albert that could keep possession, and coordinated [gegenpressing](/tactics/gegenpressing) after losing the ball. The direction of travel is clear: the sides that win now combine the old athletic edge with [positional play](/tactics/positional-play) principles learned from Barcelona and Spain.

## Latest edition

Paris 2024 ran from 25 July to 10 August, with 12 teams and matches in Paris, Marseille, Lyon, Nice, Bordeaux, Nantes and Saint-Étienne. Emma Hayes had left Chelsea only at the end of May and had three friendlies before naming a squad without Alex Morgan and with 18-year-old Olivia Moultrie among the alternates. The USA beat Zambia 3-0, Germany 4-1 and Australia 2-1 in the group stage, then Japan 1-0 after extra time in the quarter-finals (Rodman's bending finish in the 102nd minute) and Germany 1-0 after extra time in the semi-final, Smith scoring in the 95th.

Brazil had arrived with Marta announcing her international retirement and then lost her to a red card in the final group match against Spain, a suspension that kept her out of the quarter-final against France (1-0, Gabi Portilho) and the semi-final against the world champions [Spain](/nations/spain-women), which Brazil won 4-2 with Portilho, Adriana and Kerolin scoring. Marta returned for the final.

The gold-medal match at the Parc des Princes on 10 August was tight. Swanson, who had missed the 2023 World Cup with a knee injury, ran onto Albert's pass and finished low in the 57th minute. Naeher made the save of the tournament from Adriana in stoppage time. Germany beat Spain 1-0 for bronze, with Alexia Putellas missing a late penalty. It was the United States' first major title since the 2019 World Cup and the fastest turnaround from appointment to gold by any coach in the tournament's history.

## Next edition

Los Angeles 2028 will stage the first 16-team women's Olympic tournament, with group matches from 12 July across seven US venues and the gold-medal match at the Rose Bowl on 29 July 2028, one day after the men's final. The United States qualify as hosts, and by November 2026 Brazil and Colombia had secured CONMEBOL's places through the 2025 Copa América Femenina. Europe's places are decided through the 2026-27 UEFA Women's Nations League, Africa's through a two-legged qualifying series, and Asia's through the 2026 AFC Women's Asian Cup qualifying process, with the remaining CONCACAF places coming from the 2026 W Championship. The USA will attempt to become the first host to win women's gold since their own in 1996.
