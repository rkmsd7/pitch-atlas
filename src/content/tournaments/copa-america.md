## History

The Copa América began as the Campeonato Sudamericano in Buenos Aires in July 1916, part of Argentina's independence centenary, with four teams and a final-day match abandoned when the crowd set fire to the stands. [Uruguay](/nations/uruguay) won it, and Uruguay and [Argentina](/nations/argentina) shared 20 of the first 23 titles, a rivalry across the River Plate that was the whole of South American football before [Brazil](/nations/brazil) became a power. The tournament was played almost every year, sometimes twice (1959 had an Argentine and an Ecuadorian edition), and with no fixed format: round-robins, no host, two-legged finals in 1975-83 and only from 1987 a modern knockout.

Brazil won in 1919 and 1922 at home but did not take the competition seriously for decades, sending reserve teams while the World Cup mattered more; their modern record (five titles between 1997 and 2019) coincides with the years when they decided to care. Peru (1939, 1975), Paraguay (1953, 1979), Bolivia (1963, at altitude) and Colombia (2001) are the other winners, with Chile's back-to-back titles in 2015 and 2016 under Jorge Sampaoli and Juan Antonio Pizzi the most recent break in the big three's dominance.

The modern era has been shaped by [Lionel Messi](/players/lionel-messi). He lost finals in 2007, 2015 and 2016 (missing a penalty in the Centenario shootout and briefly retiring), then won the 2021 edition at the Maracanã against Brazil, his first senior international trophy, and the 2024 edition in Miami with a squad that had become world champions in between. Since 1993 CONMEBOL has invited teams from CONCACAF and occasionally Asia to fill the field; Mexico reached two finals, and the 2016 and 2024 editions were played entirely in the [United States](/nations/united-states).

## Format

The Copa América has 10 CONMEBOL members plus invited guests. Since 2024 the format is 16 teams (the 10 South Americans and six from CONCACAF) in four groups of four, with the top two in each group going to quarter-finals, semi-finals, a third-place match and the final. Earlier editions used 12 teams in three groups with the two best third-placed sides advancing. Knockout ties in 2024 went straight to penalties after 90 minutes except the final, which had extra time. The competition is scheduled every four years in the same summer as the European Championship, though CONMEBOL has moved it frequently; the 2028 edition's host and dates were still to be confirmed in November 2026.

## Greatest editions and moments

- **1916, the first.** Uruguay beat Chile 4-0 in the opening match with Isabelino Gradín, a Black player at a time when Chile protested his inclusion, scoring twice.
- **1949, Brazil 7-0 Paraguay.** The play-off at São Januário was Brazil's statement a year before their home World Cup; Ademir scored 7 in the tournament.
- **1957, Argentina's Angels with Dirty Faces.** Maschio, Angelillo and Sívori beat Brazil 3-0 in Lima and were promptly sold to Italy; Argentina have never had a more admired forward line.
- **1975, Peru.** With Teófilo Cubillas and Hugo Sotil, Peru beat Colombia in a play-off in Caracas, the first edition with no host and the only title of the Cubillas generation.
- **1991, Batistuta.** Gabriel Batistuta's six goals and Alfio Basile's Argentina ended a 32-year wait, with a 3-2 win over Brazil in the final round-robin.
- **1995, Uruguay at home.** A shootout win over Brazil in Montevideo after Tulio's handball equaliser, Uruguay's 14th title and the last for 16 years.
- **2004, Adriano in the 93rd minute.** Brazil equalised in stoppage time of the Lima final and beat Argentina on penalties; Adriano, Tevez and Luis Fabiano in the same tournament.
- **2011, Forlán and Suárez.** Uruguay beat hosts Argentina on penalties in Santa Fe and Paraguay 3-0 in the final for a record-breaking 15th title.
- **2015 and 2016, Chile's double.** Claudio Bravo's saves and Alexis Sánchez's Panenka in Santiago; Francisco Silva's winning penalty in New Jersey after Messi had sent his over the bar.
- **2021, Messi's first.** Ángel Di María's lob over Ederson at the Maracanã beat Brazil 1-0 and Messi collapsed in tears in the middle of a circle of teammates.
- **2024, Lautaro in the 112th minute.** After a delayed kick-off caused by crowd chaos at Hard Rock Stadium, [Lautaro Martínez](/players/lautaro-martinez) finished Lo Celso's pass for a 16th Argentine title.

## Records

- **Most titles:** Argentina 16; Uruguay 15; Brazil 9; Paraguay, Chile and Peru 2.
- **Most tournament goals:** Norberto Méndez (Argentina) and Zizinho (Brazil), 17. **Most in one edition:** Jair da Rosa Pinto and Humberto Maschio, 9 (1949, 1957).
- **Most appearances:** Messi, 39 matches (2007-2024); Sergio Livingstone played in six editions.
- **Most finals lost:** Argentina 14; Messi lost three finals before winning two.
- **Biggest win:** Argentina 12-0 Ecuador (1942).
- **Most consecutive titles:** Argentina 3 (1945, 1946, 1947).
- **Host winners:** 16 occasions, most recently Brazil 2019. No host has won since.
- **Longest unbeaten run by a champion:** Argentina went 36 matches unbeaten into the 2021 title and beyond.

## Philosophies that won

The Copa's early decades belong to Uruguay's and Argentina's Rioplatense school, *la nuestra*: short passing, the *gambeta* dribble and an attacking 2-3-5 that was the ancestor of what today's analysts call [relationism](/tactics/relationism). Brazil's titles from 1997 onwards were built on 4-2-2-2 shapes with twin playmakers (Rivaldo, Ronaldinho) and a pair of strikers, a structure the rest of the world had abandoned. Uruguay in 2011 under Óscar Tabárez won with a compact 4-4-2 and a [low block](/tactics/low-block) released by Forlán and Suárez, the purest [counter-attacking](/tactics/counter-attacking) champions of the century.

Chile's 2015 and 2016 wins under Sampaoli and Pizzi were the Bielsa inheritance: a high line, man-oriented pressing and a 3-4-1-2 that pressed Argentina into submission. Argentina under Lionel Scaloni, champions in 2021 and 2024, are the clearest modern template: a 4-4-2 or 4-3-3 that becomes a mid block without the ball, Rodrigo De Paul and Enzo Fernández as pressing midfielders, Emiliano Martínez in goal and Messi free to roam. It is organisation first, with genius added, and it has now won three straight major tournaments.

## Latest edition

The 2024 Copa América, 20 June to 14 July, was played in 14 US stadiums with 16 teams including six CONCACAF guests. The hosts went out in the group stage after losing to Panama and Uruguay, a result that cost Gregg Berhalter his job; Mexico also fell at the first hurdle. Colombia, under Néstor Lorenzo and with James Rodríguez providing a tournament-record six assists, went unbeaten through to the final, beating Uruguay 1-0 in a Charlotte semi-final that ended with Darwin Núñez fighting in the stands.

Argentina beat Canada 2-0 in the opener and again in the semi-final, with Julián Álvarez and Messi scoring in New Jersey; in between they beat Chile 1-0, Peru 2-0 and Ecuador on penalties after Emiliano Martínez saved twice. Uruguay beat Canada on penalties for third place.

The final in Miami Gardens on 14 July kicked off 82 minutes late after thousands of ticketless fans breached the gates at Hard Rock Stadium. Messi limped off in tears with an ankle injury in the 66th minute, Di María played the last match of his international career, and Lautaro Martínez, who finished as top scorer with five, ran onto Giovani Lo Celso's pass to score in the 112th minute. Argentina became the first team to win three consecutive major titles (Copa 2021, World Cup 2022, Copa 2024) since Spain in 2008-2012, and Scaloni's side have since added the 2026 World Cup final to that run.

## Next edition

The next Copa América is scheduled for 2028. CONMEBOL had not confirmed the host or precise dates as of November 2026; options discussed include a return to South America after two US-based editions, with Ecuador and a joint bid both mentioned, and CONMEBOL president Alejandro Domínguez has spoken of continued cooperation with CONCACAF. Argentina will defend the title in what may be the first edition of the post-Messi era, with Lionel Scaloni's contract running to December 2026 and his future unresolved.
