## History

FIFA ran an unofficial "Women's Invitation Tournament" in China in 1988 to test the appetite, and the first official Women's World Cup followed in Guangdong in November 1991, with 12 teams, 80-minute matches and a sponsor's name attached because FIFA was not yet sure the words "World Cup" belonged on it. The [United States](/nations/united-states-women) won it, Michelle Akers scoring 10 goals in six games, and American dominance became the tournament's first story.

Norway broke the sequence in Sweden in 1995, but the 1999 edition in the United States changed the sport. 90,185 people watched the final at the Rose Bowl, Brandi Chastain scored the winning penalty against China, and the image of her celebration in a sports bra became the most famous picture in women's football. Germany, with Birgit Prinz and later Nadine Angerer, won back-to-back titles in 2003 and 2007 without conceding a goal in the latter. Japan's win in 2011, months after the Tōhoku earthquake, remains the most emotional final.

The USA then won two in a row with Carli Lloyd's 16-minute hat-trick in the 2015 final and a 2019 team led by Megan Rapinoe that doubled as a campaign for equal pay. The 2023 tournament in Australia and New Zealand was the first with 32 teams, the first in the southern hemisphere and the first won by [Spain](/nations/spain-women), whose 1-0 win over [England](/nations/england-women) in Sydney was overshadowed by the Rubiales scandal and later became the catalyst for reform in the Spanish federation.

## Format

Since 2023 the Women's World Cup has 32 teams in eight groups of four, with the top two in each group advancing to a round of 16, then quarter-finals, semi-finals, a third-place match and the final. The 2027 edition in Brazil keeps the 32-team format; FIFA has signalled an increase to 48 teams from 2031. Qualification runs through the six confederations' continental championships, with a 10-team inter-confederation play-off tournament deciding the last three places. Extra time and penalties apply from the round of 16 onwards.

## Greatest editions and moments

- **1991, the first final.** Michelle Akers scored both goals in a 2-1 win over Norway in Guangzhou, the second with two minutes left after dispossessing the goalkeeper.
- **1999, the Rose Bowl.** A sold-out stadium, Briana Scurry's save from Liu Ying and Brandi Chastain's fifth penalty. The tournament that made Mia Hamm a household name.
- **2003, Germany's golden goal.** Nia Künzer's header in the 98th minute beat Sweden 2-1 in Carson, California, after the tournament was moved from China because of SARS.
- **2007, Marta's tournament.** Seven goals and the Golden Ball, including a famous solo goal against the USA in a 4-0 semi-final, though Germany won the final 2-0.
- **2011, Japan over the USA.** Homare Sawa's flicked equaliser in the 117th minute and Ayumi Kaihori's saves in the shootout, four months after the tsunami.
- **2015, Carli Lloyd's hat-trick.** Three goals inside 16 minutes in the final against Japan, the third from the halfway line.
- **2019, 13-0 and Rapinoe.** The USA's 13-0 win over Thailand opened a tournament that ended with Rapinoe scoring the opener in the final and winning both the Golden Ball and Golden Boot.
- **2023, the Matildas' run.** Australia's quarter-final penalty shootout against France (7-6, 20 kicks) was watched by the largest Australian television audience ever recorded.
- **2023, Spain's first.** Olga Carmona's 29th-minute goal beat England 1-0 in Sydney, with Aitana Bonmatí winning the Golden Ball and Salma Paralluelo the young player award.

## Records

- **Most titles:** United States 4 (1991, 1999, 2015, 2019); Germany 2; Norway, Japan and Spain 1.
- **Most goals, all tournaments:** Marta 17 (2003-2023). **Most in one tournament:** Michelle Akers 10 (1991).
- **Most appearances:** Formiga played in seven World Cups (1995-2019); Marta played in six.
- **Biggest win:** USA 13-0 Thailand (2019), Alex Morgan scoring five.
- **Most final appearances:** USA 5 (won 4). Germany 3 (won 2).
- **Record attendance:** 90,185, Rose Bowl, 1999 final. The 2023 tournament set the aggregate record at just under 2 million spectators.
- **Clean-sheet record:** Germany conceded zero goals in winning the 2007 tournament.

## Philosophies that won

The early tournaments rewarded athleticism and [direct play](/tactics/direct-play): the USA of the 1990s pressed high, played fast and overwhelmed opponents physically. Germany's 2003 and 2007 titles were built on a disciplined 4-4-2, [set pieces](/tactics/set-pieces) and a world-class goalkeeper. Japan in 2011 proved the technical, possession-based alternative could win, keeping the ball against bigger teams and pressing in coordinated units.

The 2015 and 2019 American teams were hybrids, a 4-3-3 with pressing triggers and wide forwards cutting in, backed by the deepest squad in the sport. Spain's 2023 win imported the [positional play](/tactics/positional-play) of [Barcelona Women](/clubs/barcelona-women): Bonmatí, Alexia Putellas and Teresa Abelleira rotating in midfield, full-backs high and wide, patience in possession and quick counter-pressing after turnovers. England under Sarina Wiegman reached the final with a flexible [back three](/tactics/back-three) and the ability to drop into a [low block](/tactics/low-block). The next cycle looks to be about whether the Spanish model remains unbeatable or whether athletic pressing sides such as the USA under Emma Hayes can reclaim the title.

## Latest edition

The 2023 Women's World Cup ran from 20 July to 20 August across Australia and New Zealand, the first edition with 32 teams and the first co-hosted across two confederations. New Zealand beat Norway in the opening match for their first ever World Cup win, and the tournament produced a historic round of 16 in which the United States, two-time holders, went out on penalties to Sweden after Lina Hurtig's spot-kick was adjudged to have crossed the line by millimetres. Germany failed to escape their group for the first time, and Morocco, Jamaica and South Africa all reached the knockouts on debut or as rank outsiders.

Spain, who had been beaten 4-0 by Japan in the group stage, recovered to beat Switzerland 5-1, the Netherlands 2-1 after extra time and Sweden 2-1 in the semi-final, with Olga Carmona scoring the winner in the 89th minute. England, European champions the previous summer, beat Nigeria on penalties, Colombia 2-1 and co-hosts Australia 3-1 in a Sydney semi-final watched by 75,784.

The final at Stadium Australia on 20 August was decided by Carmona's low shot from the edge of the box in the 29th minute. Mary Earps saved a Jenni Hermoso penalty in the second half to keep England in it, and Earps won the Golden Glove. Aitana Bonmatí took the Golden Ball, Hinata Miyazawa of Japan the Golden Boot with five goals and Salma Paralluelo the young player award. The aftermath, when federation president Luis Rubiales kissed Hermoso at the medal ceremony, led to a players' strike, his resignation and a reform of the Spanish women's programme.

## Next edition

The 2027 Women's World Cup will be held in Brazil from 24 June to 25 July 2027, the first in South America, across eight host cities. Brazil qualified as hosts and the field is largely set, with most of the 32 places confirmed by November 2026 and the last three to be decided by the inter-confederation play-off tournament, whose preliminary phase runs from 24 November to 5 December 2026 with a final phase in February 2027. The final draw takes place on 11 December 2026 at the Museum of Modern Art in Rio de Janeiro. Spain will defend the title; England, the USA, Germany, France and Japan are the obvious challengers, with the Brazilian hosts, under Arthur Elias, hoping to give Marta's generation a fitting farewell.
