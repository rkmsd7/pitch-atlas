## History

The first European Competition for Women's Football was played in 1984 with 16 entrants, a four-team final tournament, 70-minute matches and a size-four ball, and ended with Sweden beating [England](/nations/england-women) on penalties in Luton after a 1-1 aggregate over two legs. Norway won in 1987 at home and in 1993 in Italy, and then [Germany](/nations/germany-women) took over. From 1989, when West Germany beat Norway 4-1 in Osnabrück, to 2013, Germany won eight of nine editions, six in a row between 1995 and 2013, with Silvia Neid, Birgit Prinz, Nadine Angerer and Inka Grings among the stars. Only Norway in 1993 interrupted them.

The tournament grew from four to eight teams in 1997, 12 in 2009 and 16 in 2017, when the [Netherlands](/nations/netherlands-women) under Sarina Wiegman won at home with Vivianne Miedema and Lieke Martens, and Germany's run ended in the quarter-finals. Wiegman then moved to England and won again in 2022 at Wembley, Ella Toone's lob and Chloe Kelly's extra-time winner against Germany in front of 87,192, a record for any European Championship final. Euro 2025 in Switzerland, with an aggregate attendance of 657,000, was the most-watched women's Euro ever, and England retained the title with a shootout win over [Spain](/nations/spain-women) in Basel after an extraordinary knockout run of three consecutive comebacks.

## Format

Since 2017 the finals have 16 teams in four groups of four, with the top two in each group going to quarter-finals, semi-finals and a final; there is no third-place match. Qualification uses the Women's Nations League structure: League A teams qualify directly through their group positions, with play-offs between League A and B sides deciding the remaining places. The host qualifies automatically. Knockout ties go to extra time and penalties. The tournament is held every four years, one year after the men's Euro, and will stay at 16 teams for 2029.

## Greatest editions and moments

- **1984, Luton.** Pia Sundhage scored Sweden's goal in the first leg and the decisive penalty in the second; Linda Curl missed for England.
- **1989, Osnabrück.** West Germany's first title, a 4-1 win over Norway with Doris Fitschen among the scorers, in front of 22,000.
- **1995, Kaiserslautern.** Germany's 3-2 win over Sweden, Maren Meinert and Birgit Prinz, who was 17, in the side.
- **2001, Ulm.** Claudia Müller's golden goal beat Sweden in the 98th minute; Germany's fifth title and the first of six in a row.
- **2009, Helsinki.** Germany 6-2 England, Inka Grings scoring twice and Prinz once, the highest-scoring final.
- **2013, Angerer's saves.** Nadine Angerer saved two penalties from Norway in Solna and Anja Mittag scored the only goal; Germany's eighth and last.
- **2017, Oranje at home.** Miedema's two goals against Denmark in Enschede, Martens the player of the tournament, 28,000 in the stadium.
- **2022, Wembley.** Alessia Russo's backheel against Sweden, Toone's lob and Kelly's winner against Germany, England's first major title in either game since 1966.
- **2025, Sweden 2-2 England (2-3 pens).** England were 2-0 down with 11 minutes left; Lucy Bronze and Michelle Agyemang scored and Hannah Hampton saved twice in a 14-kick shootout.
- **2025, Agyemang again.** The 19-year-old's 96th-minute equaliser against Italy in Geneva before Kelly's winner in the 119th minute.
- **2025, the Basel final.** Mariona Caldentey's header, Russo's equaliser, Hampton's shootout saves from Caldentey and Bonmatí, Salma Paralluelo's miss, and Kelly's winning kick.

## Records

- **Most titles:** Germany 8; England, Norway 2; Sweden, Netherlands 1.
- **Most consecutive titles:** Germany 6 (1995-2013).
- **Most goals, all tournaments:** Birgit Prinz and Inka Grings, 10 each. **Single tournament:** Grings, 6 (2009); Beth Mead, 6 (2022).
- **Most finals lost:** Norway 4; Sweden 3.
- **Biggest final win:** Germany 6-2 England (2009). **Biggest win:** England 8-0 Norway (2022).
- **Record attendance:** 87,192, Wembley, 2022 final.
- **Only finals decided on penalties:** 1984 and 2025, both involving England.
- **Youngest finalist scorer:** Michelle Agyemang, 19, in the 2025 knockouts; Birgit Prinz, 17, in 1995.

## Philosophies that won

Germany's dynasty was a model of a national federation's consistency: a 4-4-2 and later 4-2-3-1 built on physical dominance, [set pieces](/tactics/set-pieces), [direct play](/tactics/direct-play) to Prinz and Grings and goalkeepers (Silke Rottenberg, Angerer) who decided finals. Norway in 1987 and 1993 played a similar, if more conservative, long-ball game. The Netherlands in 2017 under Wiegman shifted the template towards a possession-based 4-3-3 with Martens and Shanice van de Sanden as wingers and Miedema as a flexible striker.

England under Wiegman in 2022 and 2025 have won with a 4-3-3 that can look like a [back three](/tactics/back-three) in possession, Keira Walsh as the pivot, Lauren James and Lauren Hemp wide, and a willingness to drop into a [low block](/tactics/low-block) and win on transitions and from the bench: the "finishers" Kelly, Agyemang and Toone were decisive in 2025. Spain, world champions, played the most beautiful football in Switzerland, with Aitana Bonmatí, Alexia Putellas and Patri Guijarro running a [positional play](/tactics/positional-play) system in which they averaged more than 65 per cent possession, but lost the final on penalties after creating most of the chances. That contrast, Spanish control against English resilience and depth, defines the current era.

## Latest edition

Euro 2025 in Switzerland ran from 2 to 27 July across eight cities. Spain, world champions under Montse Tomé, scored 14 goals in the group stage (6-2 Portugal, 6-2 Belgium, 3-1 Italy) and beat the hosts 2-0 in the quarter-final and Germany 1-0 after extra time in the semi-final, Bonmatí's near-post finish in the 113th minute. England lost their opening match 2-1 to France in Zurich, then beat the Netherlands 4-0 and Wales 6-1 before the comebacks: 2-0 down to Sweden in Zurich before Bronze and Agyemang scored and Hampton saved in a 3-2 shootout (nine of 14 penalties missed), then 1-0 down to Italy in Geneva until Agyemang's 96th-minute equaliser and Kelly's winner in the 119th, scored from the rebound of her own saved penalty.

Switzerland, under Pia Sundhage, reached the quarter-finals for the first time in front of sellout crowds; Germany lost a group match 4-1 to Sweden and had goalkeeper Ann-Katrin Berger make a stunning save against France in a quarter-final shootout; Norway and Italy both recovered form, Italy reaching a first semi-final since 1997.

The final at St. Jakob-Park in Basel on 27 July was Spain's for an hour. Caldentey headed in Ona Batlle's cross in the 25th minute, and Spain controlled the ball, but Russo equalised in the 57th from Kelly's cross, Hampton made saves from Bonmatí and Esther González, and the match went to penalties after a goalless extra time. Hampton saved from Caldentey and Bonmatí, Paralluelo dragged her kick wide, and Kelly, whose shot had been saved against Italy, drove the winning kick high into the net for a 3-1 shootout win. England became the first team since Germany in 2013 to retain the title and the first to win a major women's tournament outside their own country. Hampton was named goalkeeper of the tournament, Bonmatí player of the tournament, Esther González top scorer with four, and Agyemang young player. Wiegman had reached five consecutive major finals as a coach.

## Next edition

The 2029 Women's European Championship will be hosted by Germany, who were awarded the tournament by UEFA's Executive Committee in December 2025 ahead of bids from Poland, Portugal, Italy and a joint Nordic bid (a decision Pitch Atlas has not independently verified against UEFA's own records). It will be Germany's second hosting after 2001 and the first women's Euro in Germany since the 2011 Women's World Cup there. Qualification runs through the 2027-28 Women's Nations League cycle, after the 2027 World Cup in Brazil. England, under Sarina Wiegman, whose contract runs to 2027, will attempt a third consecutive title; Spain, Germany, France and the Netherlands are the main challengers.
