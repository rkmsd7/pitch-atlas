## Overview

The J1 League is the top division of the J.League, Japan's professional structure founded in 1992 and launched in 1993. Twenty clubs play 38 rounds; the top three qualify for the AFC Champions League Elite and the bottom three are relegated to J2. Clubs are community-rooted by statute (the "hometown" rule), stadiums are safe and family-friendly, and the league's organisation, youth development and broadcasting deal with DAZN are the envy of Asia.

The 2026-27 season is a landmark. After three decades of calendar-year football, the J.League moved to an autumn-to-spring season beginning in August 2026 and ending in May 2027, aligning with Europe to ease transfers and avoid the brutal summer heat. The 2025 season was the last calendar-year campaign, bridged by a short transitional tournament in early 2026.

## History and eras

Japanese club football was amateur and company-based (the Japan Soccer League) until the J.League was created to professionalise the game ahead of a World Cup bid. Verdy Kawasaki (now Tokyo Verdy) won the first two titles with Kazu Miura; Zico's arrival at Kashima Antlers in 1991 built the league's most successful club. Jubilo Iwata, Yokohama F. Marinos, Gamba Osaka and Urawa Red Diamonds (AFC Champions League winners in 2007, 2017 and 2022) dominated the 2000s. Sanfrecce Hiroshima won three in four years from 2012, Kawasaki Frontale four in five from 2017 under Toru Oniki with a possession style that produced Kaoru Mitoma and Ao Tanaka. Vissel Kobe, with Iniesta's legacy and Yuya Osako's goals, won in 2023 and 2024.

In 2025 the title went to the final day. Kashima Antlers beat Yokohama F. Marinos 2-1 on 6 December 2025 to finish on 76 points, one ahead of Kashiwa Reysol, who had won six in a row to chase them. It was Kashima's ninth title, their first since 2016, and Leo Ceara was the league's top scorer with 21. Yokohama FC, Shonan Bellmare and Albirex Niigata were relegated.

## Style and tactical trends

Japanese football is technical, disciplined and increasingly European in structure. Kawasaki's [positional play](/tactics/positional-play), Marinos' Postecoglou-era high pressing, Kashima's Brazilian-influenced pragmatism and Vissel Kobe's direct, physical 4-3-3 under Takayuki Yoshida show the range. The national team's success (round of 16 at the 2022 and 2026 World Cups) and the number of Japanese players in Europe (Mitoma, Kubo, Endo, Kamada, Doan, Minamino) reflect the league's coaching quality.

The tactical trend of the mid-2020s is intensity: the league's data shows pressing and sprint numbers now comparable to the Bundesliga. The autumn-spring switch is expected to accelerate that, with a mid-winter break in the snowy north (Sapporo, Niigata) and the summer rest the players have never had.

## Great clubs and dynasties

Kashima Antlers (9 titles, 2018 AFC Champions League) are the J.League's Manchester United. Yokohama F. Marinos (5), Kawasaki Frontale (4), Jubilo Iwata (3), Sanfrecce Hiroshima (3), Gamba Osaka (2), Urawa Red Diamonds (1 title but three Asian crowns), Vissel Kobe (2), Tokyo Verdy (2), Nagoya Grampus (2010 under Dragan Stojkovic) and Kashiwa Reysol (2011) are the champions.

## 2026-27 season storylines

- **The new calendar.** The 2026-27 J1 kicked off in August 2026 with 20 clubs and 380 matches; rounds 21 to 38 are scheduled for 2027 after a winter break. The title cannot be decided before the spring.
- **FC Tokyo leading.** As of the update of 1-2 November 2026, FC Tokyo were top after 13 rounds, with Vissel Kobe fifth on 26 points and Kashima sixth on 23 in the fight for the AFC places. Round 14 was played on 7 November. Earlier in the autumn Vissel Kobe and Kashiwa Reysol had led.
- **Kashima's defence.** The champions, with Leo Ceara, began the new season in mid-table and need a strong winter to retain the shield.
- **Promoted.** Mito Hollyhock, V-Varen Nagasaki and JEF United Chiba came up for the transitional season, while Yokohama FC, Shonan and Niigata went down.
- **Japan after the World Cup.** The Samurai Blue's 2026 World Cup, played in North American heat, raised the question of how many J1 regulars the national team should use; the new calendar is partly an answer.
