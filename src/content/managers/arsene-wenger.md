## Biography

Arsène Charles Ernest Wenger was born in Strasbourg on 22 October 1949 and grew up in his parents' bistro in the village of Duttlenheim, Alsace. He arrived at [Arsenal](/clubs/arsenal) in October 1996 as an unknown, "Arsène who?", from Japan, and left in May 2018 as the longest-serving and most influential manager in the club's history, having won three Premier League titles, seven FA Cups, gone a whole league season unbeaten in 2003-04, and changed the diet, training and recruitment of English football. Since 2019 he has been FIFA's chief of global football development, where he has pushed for changes to the offside law and to the international calendar.

## Playing career

Wenger was a tall, slow centre-back who played amateur football for Mutzig and Mulhouse while studying economics at the University of Strasbourg, and made a dozen appearances for Strasbourg in Ligue 1, including a few in the 1978-79 title season. He retired in 1981 and took over the club's youth team.

At [Monaco](/clubs/monaco) he won the title in his first season with Glenn Hoddle and signed George Weah and Jürgen Klinsmann. In Japan he learned patience. At Arsenal he won the double in 1998 with Dennis Bergkamp, Marc Overmars and Patrick Vieira around George Graham's back four, the double again in 2002, and the 2004 title without a defeat with Thierry Henry, Robert Pirès and Vieira; the 49-game unbeaten run ended at Old Trafford. The move to the Emirates in 2006, the same year as the Champions League final defeat to Barcelona, coincided with nine years without a trophy as the stadium was paid for, before three FA Cups between 2014 and 2017.

## Tactical identity

Wenger's Arsenal played a fast, fluid, technical game built on short passing, movement and speed on the counter. The early sides were a 4-4-2 with a rigid defence and a creative front four; the Invincibles added the pace of Henry and the elegance of Pirès and Freddie Ljungberg, and attacked with a [counter-attacking](/tactics/counter-attacking) ferocity that nobody in England could match. After 2006 he shifted to a 4-2-3-1 and then a 4-3-3 built on [positional play](/tactics/positional-play), with Cesc Fàbregas, Samir Nasri and Mesut Özil passing through the lines and a [high line](/tactics/high-line) that was often exposed. His innovations off the pitch were as important: nutrition, stretching, the scouting of French-speaking Africa and the sale of players at the top of their value. His weakness was the refusal, for years, to buy a destructive midfielder.

## Achievements

- Three Premier League titles, including the unbeaten 2003-04 season, the only one in the Premier League era
- A record seven FA Cups
- Ligue 1 with Monaco in his first season in the top flight
- Champions League final 2006, Arsenal's only one
- 22 years at Arsenal; the club's record for games managed; OBE 2003

## Storyline

Retired from club management. For many years Wenger's shadow was the problem at Arsenal: the stadium, the ideals, the decline, the "Wenger Out" banners. In 2025-26 [Mikel Arteta](/managers/mikel-arteta), who played under him, won the club's first Premier League title in 22 years and took Arsenal to the Champions League final in Budapest, which they lost to PSG on penalties. Wenger was in the stands for both, the first time the club's past and present had made peace.

The torch is carried by Arteta, who speaks of Wenger's values constantly; by the French coaches who followed his path to England; and by his own work at FIFA, where the "daylight" offside proposal and the global academy programme are extensions of a lifetime's belief that the game should be more open and more skilful. A bust of him stands at the Emirates.
