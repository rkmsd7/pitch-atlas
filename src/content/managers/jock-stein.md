## Biography

John Stein was born in Burnbank, Lanarkshire, on 5 October 1922 and worked down the pit until he was 27. He became the first British manager to win the European Cup, with [Celtic](/clubs/celtic)'s Lisbon Lions in 1967, every one of them born within 30 miles of Glasgow, and the first to win nine league titles in a row. Sir Alex Ferguson, who was his assistant with Scotland, called him the greatest. He died on 10 September 1985 at Ninian Park, Cardiff, suffering a heart attack on the bench as Scotland secured a World Cup play-off against Wales.

## Playing career

Stein was a centre-half for Albion Rovers, Llanelli and, from 1951, Celtic, where he was signed as a reserve, became captain and won the double in 1954. An ankle injury ended his career in 1957 and he took over the club's reserves, where he coached Billy McNeill and Bobby Murdoch, the future Lisbon Lions.

Dunfermline, a small club, won their first Scottish Cup under him and beat Valencia in Europe. At Celtic, where he was the first Protestant manager, he won the title in his first full season and then the 1967 quadruple: league, both cups and the European Cup, beating Herrera's Inter 2-1 in Lisbon with goals from Tommy Gemmell and Stevie Chalmers after Inter had led from a penalty. Celtic lost the 1970 final to Feyenoord in Milan. He survived a near-fatal car crash in 1975, won a tenth title in 1977, and was pushed out in 1978. Scotland qualified for the 1982 World Cup and were on the way to 1986 when he died.

## Tactical identity

Stein's Celtic attacked with a fury that overwhelmed the defensive systems of the era. The Lisbon final is the classic case: against Inter's [catenaccio](/tactics/catenaccio), Celtic pushed both full-backs, Jim Craig and Gemmell, forward constantly, played a 4-2-4 that became a 2-4-4 in possession, and had 39 shots. The wingers Jimmy Johnstone and Bobby Lennox hugged the touchlines, Murdoch and Bertie Auld ran the midfield, and the [high line](/tactics/high-line) was aggressive; the ideas anticipated [total football](/tactics/total-football) by several years and Stein, who studied Herrera and Hungary's 1953 side, was a serious tactician behind the image of a motivator. Against stronger sides he could sit and [counter-attack](/tactics/counter-attacking), as Celtic did to beat Leeds in the 1970 semi-final. His authority was total and his man-management, with the difficult Johnstone in particular, was his greatest art.

## Achievements

- European Cup 1967, the first by a British club, and the 1970 final
- Ten Scottish titles, including nine in a row (1966-74)
- 25 major trophies at Celtic in 13 years
- Scottish Cup with Dunfermline in 1961
- CBE 1970; Scottish Football Hall of Fame inaugural inductee

## Storyline

Died 1985. Stein is Scottish football's defining figure, and the Lisbon Lions are Celtic's identity: the stand named after him, the statue at Celtic Park, the lion rampant in the badge's heritage. The nine-in-a-row record was equalled by Rangers in 1997 and by Celtic again in 2020, but no British side has won the European Cup with a wholly local team since.

The torch is carried by [Alex Ferguson](/managers/alex-ferguson), who sat beside him in Cardiff and described Stein as the man who taught him most, and by the Celtic managers who followed, from Martin O'Neill to Brendan Rodgers, who have treated the nine-in-a-row as the standard. Scotland, coached by [Sébastien Pocognoli](/managers/sebastien-pocognoli) from 2026, play for a Jock Stein-shaped hole in their history: a home Euro 2028 and the knockout win they have never managed.
