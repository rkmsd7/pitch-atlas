## Biography

Telê Santana da Silva was born in Itabirito, Minas Gerais, on 26 July 1931. He is Brazilian football's great moralist, the coach who believed that winning ugly was a kind of losing, and the man behind the 1982 [World Cup](/tournaments/world-cup) side of Zico, Sócrates, Falcão and Éder, which lost 3-2 to Italy in Barcelona and is remembered more fondly than most teams that won. A decade later he proved that his football could win, taking São Paulo to two Copas Libertadores and beating Barcelona and Milan in consecutive Intercontinental Cups. A stroke ended his career in 1996, and he died in Belo Horizonte on 21 April 2006.

## Playing career

Santana was a quick right-winger, nicknamed "Fio de Esperança", the thread of hope, for Fluminense between 1951 and 1960, winning two Carioca titles, and finished at Guarani, Madureira and Vasco. He never played for Brazil. He was known even then as a disciplined, serious professional in a league of bohemians.

The 1982 team won their first five matches, beating the Soviet Union, Scotland, New Zealand and Argentina with football of extraordinary fluency, and were eliminated by Paolo Rossi's hat-trick in a match they needed only to draw. The 1986 side lost a quarter-final to France on penalties. At [São Paulo](/clubs/sao-paulo), with Raí, Cafu, Müller and the goalkeeper Zetti, he built the best Brazilian club side since Santos, beating Cruyff's Barcelona 2-1 in Tokyo in 1992 and Capello's Milan 3-2 a year later.

## Tactical identity

Santana's 4-2-2-2, the classic Brazilian "magic square", placed two creators, Zico and Sócrates in 1982, Raí and the Lisbon-born playmaker in the 1990s, between two holding midfielders, Falcão and Toninho Cerezo, and two forwards. The full-backs, Leandro and Júnior, then Cafu, attacked relentlessly; the defensive line was [high](/tactics/high-line); and the ball was moved by short passes and dribbling in a way that resembled [positional play](/tactics/positional-play) long before European coaches formalised it. Serginho was a conventional striker in 1982, which critics felt unbalanced the side; at São Paulo Müller often dropped into a [false nine](/tactics/false-nine) role. Santana was also a disciplinarian, intolerant of diving, time-wasting and dissent, which made him unpopular with some players and beloved by the public.

## Achievements

- Two Copas Libertadores and two Intercontinental Cups with São Paulo (1992, 1993), beating Barcelona and Milan
- Two Brasileirão titles
- The 1982 Brazil side, voted among the greatest teams never to win the World Cup
- Named by Brazilian press as the greatest club coach in the country's history

## Storyline

Died 2006. The 1982 defeat to Italy was described in Brazil as the day football died, and the pragmatic titles that followed, in 1994 and 2002, were won in explicit rejection of Santana's romanticism. The argument has never ended. [Fernando Diniz](/managers/fernando-diniz)'s relationism and the attacking Brazil that [Carlo Ancelotti](/managers/carlo-ancelotti) tries to coach are, in different ways, attempts to reconcile Santana's beauty with results.

The torch is carried by his São Paulo lineage: Cafu and Raí as ambassadors, Muricy Ramalho and the coaches who learned there, and by the belief, shared by [Pep Guardiola](/managers/pep-guardiola), who has called the 1982 team his inspiration, that a team's style is part of its obligation to the people who watch. São Paulo's training ground, the CT da Barra Funda, and a stand at the Morumbi carry his name.
