## Biography

Guus Hiddink was born in Varsseveld, in the rural east of the Netherlands, on 8 November 1946, the son of a schoolteacher. No coach has been more adaptable: he won the European Cup with [PSV Eindhoven](/clubs/psv-eindhoven) in 1988, took three different nations, the [Netherlands](/nations/netherlands), [South Korea](/nations/south-korea) and Russia, to tournament semi-finals, turned [Australia](/nations/australia) into a World Cup knockout team, and won the FA Cup in four months as [Chelsea](/clubs/chelsea)'s interim manager. In South Korea he is an honorary citizen with a stadium named after him. He retired after a spell with Curaçao in 2021.

## Playing career

Hiddink was a midfielder for De Graafschap, where he spent most of a 15-year career, with spells at PSV, the Washington Diplomats and San Jose Earthquakes in the NASL and NEC. He was a reliable rather than remarkable player, never capped, and retired in 1982 to become De Graafschap's assistant.

His first PSV, with Ronald Koeman, Eric Gerets and Wim Kieft, won the treble in 1988, beating Benfica on penalties in Stuttgart. The 1998 Dutch side, with Dennis Bergkamp, Edgar Davids and Patrick Kluivert, lost the semi-final to Brazil on penalties. South Korea in 2002, co-hosts, beat Portugal, Italy and Spain to reach the semi-finals, the best result by an Asian team, and the country stopped. His second PSV, with Park Ji-sung and Mark van Bommel, reached the 2005 semi-final and lost to Milan on away goals. Russia beat the Netherlands in the Euro 2008 quarter-final with Andrey Arshavin inspired. Chelsea won the 2009 FA Cup under him and the players asked him to stay.

## Tactical identity

Hiddink is a pragmatist with Dutch roots. His PSV sides played a 4-3-3 of [positional play](/tactics/positional-play) principles, but his national-team work was built on fitness, organisation and the [counter-attack](/tactics/counter-attacking): South Korea's relentless pressing and running in 2002 came from a six-month training camp he insisted on, and Russia's 2008 side was a 4-1-4-1 that broke at speed. He used a [back three](/tactics/back-three) with Australia and the Netherlands in 1998 and was comfortable changing shape mid-match, often with a decisive substitution. His great strength was psychological: he broke South Korea's hierarchical dressing-room culture, got Chelsea's veterans to play for a temporary boss, and made players at every level feel chosen.

## Achievements

- European Cup 1988 with PSV, plus a treble
- Six Eredivisie titles across two PSV spells
- World Cup semi-finals with the Netherlands (1998) and South Korea (2002); Euro 2008 semi-final with Russia
- FA Cup 2009 as Chelsea's interim coach
- Honorary citizen of South Korea; the Guus Hiddink Stadium in Gwangju

## Storyline

Retired. Hiddink is the model for the modern national-team specialist, the coach who arrives, imposes structure and belief, and produces a result beyond the squad's apparent level. South Korea's 2002 run, in particular, reshaped Asian football's ambitions, and [Hajime Moriyasu](/managers/hajime-moriyasu)'s Japan and the Korean generation of Son Heung-min grew up on it.

The torch is carried by [Jorge Jesus](/managers/jorge-jesus), [Carlo Ancelotti](/managers/carlo-ancelotti) in Brazil and the growing number of veteran club coaches who take national teams late in their careers, and by Dutch coaching's global reach, from [Xavi](/managers/xavi-manager)'s Netherlands to Mark van Bommel's Belgium. Hiddink lives in the Netherlands and advises the KNVB, still cheerful, still asked by Korean journalists to come back.
