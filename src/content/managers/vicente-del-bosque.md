## Biography

Vicente del Bosque González was born in Salamanca on 23 December 1950, the son of a railway clerk imprisoned under Franco for his republican views. He spent his whole life at [Real Madrid](/clubs/real-madrid), as player, youth coach and twice-sacked caretaker, until a four-year spell as first-team coach brought two Champions Leagues and two league titles, after which Florentino Pérez let him go for not being glamorous enough. He then coached [Spain](/nations/spain) to the 2010 [World Cup](/tournaments/world-cup) and [Euro 2012](/tournaments/euro), completing the only treble of its kind in the history of the game. He was made a marquis in 2011 and retired in 2016.

## Playing career

Del Bosque was a deep-lying midfielder for Real Madrid from 1970 to 1984, winning five La Liga titles and four Copas del Rey, and played 18 times for Spain, including at Euro 1980. He was a thoughtful, unspectacular passer, the kind of player Madrid's fans respected without adoring.

He took over Madrid in November 1999 and won the Champions League in Paris against Valencia six months later, then again in Glasgow in 2002 with Zinedine Zidane's volley against Bayer Leverkusen. His galácticos team, with Zidane, Luís Figo, Raúl, Roberto Carlos and Ronaldo, won the 2003 title and he was sacked the next day. With Spain he inherited Luis Aragonés's European champions and made them world champions: Andrés Iniesta's goal in Johannesburg, then a 4-0 in the Euro 2012 final against Italy, the first team to win three major tournaments in a row. The 2014 World Cup defence collapsed in the group stage, and he left after Euro 2016.

## Tactical identity

Del Bosque was the ideal custodian of [tiki-taka](/tactics/tiki-taka): a coach who understood that his job was to let Xavi, Iniesta, Sergio Busquets and Xabi Alonso play. Spain under him used a 4-2-3-1 or a 4-3-3 in which the midfield simply kept the ball until the opponent broke, and at Euro 2012 he went further, playing Cesc Fàbregas as a [false nine](/tactics/false-nine) in a side with no recognised striker, a radical idea that produced the most dominant final in the tournament's history. The principles were those of [positional play](/tactics/positional-play), inherited from the Barcelona of Guardiola, which supplied half the team. At Madrid he had been more pragmatic, trusting individual brilliance behind a solid base of Fernando Hierro and Claude Makélélé. His genius was for harmony: he managed the Madrid-Barcelona divide in the Spain dressing room with quiet decency.

## Achievements

- World Cup 2010, Spain's first
- Euro 2012, completing an unprecedented treble of major tournaments for Spain
- Two Champions Leagues and two La Liga titles with Real Madrid
- The only coach to win the World Cup, the European Championship and the Champions League
- FIFA World Coach of the Year 2012

## Storyline

Retired. Del Bosque's Spain were, with Guardiola's Barcelona, the peak of the possession era, and he was its most modest champion. When Spain won the 2026 World Cup under [Luis de la Fuente](/managers/luis-de-la-fuente), beating Argentina in New Jersey, the comparison was inevitable: a federation coach, a non-celebrity, who inherited a philosophy and won the biggest prize with it. De la Fuente was quick to credit his predecessor.

The torch runs from Del Bosque through De la Fuente, whose team keeps the passing identity with more verticality, to [Xavi](/managers/xavi-manager), now coaching the Netherlands, and [Xabi Alonso](/managers/xabi-alonso), his midfielder in 2010, now at Chelsea. The Marqués de Del Bosque remains a popular figure in Spanish public life, as decent in retirement as he was in charge.
