## Biography

Vittorio Pozzo was born in Turin on 2 March 1886 and studied languages and business in Zurich, Manchester and Bradford, where he fell in love with English football and with the style of Manchester United's Charlie Roberts. He coached [Italy](/nations/italy) for 19 years across three spells, the last from 1929 to 1948, and won the 1934 [World Cup](/tournaments/world-cup) at home, the 1936 Olympic gold in Berlin and the 1938 World Cup in France, the only coach to win the tournament twice. He worked unpaid for most of it, as a Pirelli executive and a journalist, and his association with the fascist era, including the Roman salutes of 1938, has complicated his memory. He died in Ponderano on 21 December 1968.

## Playing career

Pozzo was a midfielder for Grasshoppers in Zurich and then Torino, which he helped found in 1906, playing for them until 1911. He was an average player with an exceptional understanding of the game, and his family's disapproval of football meant he kept a business career throughout.

Appointed commissario unico in 1929, he built the first great Italian side around Juventus's defence, Inter's Giuseppe Meazza and the oriundi, Argentine-born players such as Luis Monti and Raimundo Orsi, whom he justified by noting that if they could die for Italy in the army they could play for it. The 1934 final against Czechoslovakia was won 2-1 in extra time in Rome; the 1936 Olympic team of students won in Berlin; and the 1938 side, with Silvio Piola and Meazza, beat Hungary 4-2 in Paris. His last match was the 1948 Olympics, after the Superga disaster had destroyed the Torino side that formed his post-war team.

## Tactical identity

Pozzo's Metodo was a 2-3-2-3, a modification of the old 2-3-5 pyramid: the two inside-forwards were withdrawn into midfield to create a five-man unit that could defend and launch attacks, with the centre-half, Monti, as a deep-lying organiser. Against the WM of Herbert Chapman's England it was more compact in the centre and more reliant on the [counter-attack](/tactics/counter-attacking): absorb pressure in a relatively [low block](/tactics/low-block), then release the wingers and Piola with [direct play](/tactics/direct-play) through Meazza. Pozzo was also a pioneer of the ritiro, the training retreat, of psychological preparation, taking players to the war graves before matches, and of the idea of a national style. The Metodo was the ancestor of the Italian preference for structure and counter-attack that became [catenaccio](/tactics/catenaccio).

## Achievements

- World Cup 1934 and 1938, a record two titles for a coach
- Olympic gold 1936
- Two Central European International Cups
- Italy unbeaten in 30 matches between 1935 and 1939
- A founder of Torino and the longest-serving Italy coach

## Storyline

Died 1968. Pozzo's record stands alone, and so does his reputation, which Italy has never quite known how to hold: the Stadio Olimpico in Turin was renamed after him in 1986, and the question of his politics returns with every anniversary. The Italian school of pragmatism, from [Vittorio Pozzo](/managers/vittorio-pozzo)'s Metodo through [Nereo Rocco](/managers/nereo-rocco), [Giovanni Trapattoni](/managers/giovanni-trapattoni) and [Marcello Lippi](/managers/marcello-lippi) to the 2006 World Cup, begins with him.

The torch is carried, in principle, by every Italy coach, and in 2026 that is [Roberto Mancini](/managers/roberto-mancini), reappointed in July after the Azzurri's third consecutive World Cup absence, a run that makes Pozzo's two titles in four years seem like a story from another country. Italy's four stars, two of them Pozzo's, remain on the shirt.
