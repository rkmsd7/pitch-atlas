## Biography

Helenio Herrera Gavilán was born in Buenos Aires on 10 April 1910 to Spanish immigrant parents and grew up in Casablanca, Morocco, in poverty. He became the first manager to be bigger than his players: the first to earn a star's salary, the first to give motivational speeches that were quoted in the papers, the first to be known by a nickname, Il Mago, the magician. With [Inter](/clubs/inter-milan) he won three Scudetti and two European Cups between 1963 and 1966 and made [catenaccio](/tactics/catenaccio) the dominant idea in world football. He died in Venice on 9 November 1997.

## Playing career

Herrera was a defender in France, for Red Star Paris, Stade Français and others in the 1930s and early 1940s, and won one cap for France in 1939. He was an average player with a sharp mind, and started coaching while still playing, at Puteaux, in 1944.

His [Barcelona](/clubs/barcelona), with László Kubala, Luis Suárez and Evaristo, won two titles but lost the 1960 European Cup semi-final to Real Madrid, and he was sacked. At Inter he built the Grande Inter: Giacinto Facchetti as the first attacking full-back, Armando Picchi as libero, Suárez, brought from Barcelona for a world record, as the deep playmaker, Sandro Mazzola and Mario Corso in attack. They beat Real Madrid 3-1 in the 1964 final in Vienna and Benfica 1-0 at San Siro in 1965. The 1967 final defeat to Celtic's Lisbon Lions ended the era.

## Tactical identity

Herrera did not invent catenaccio, which Karl Rappan and [Nereo Rocco](/managers/nereo-rocco) had used before him, but he perfected it and made it famous: a libero, Picchi, behind a man-marking back four, a [low block](/tactics/low-block) that conceded almost nothing, and rapid [counter-attacks](/tactics/counter-attacking) through Facchetti's overlaps and Suárez's long passing. The system was more attacking than its reputation, with Facchetti scoring ten league goals in 1965-66, but its defensive discipline defined an era of Italian football. Herrera was also a pioneer of psychology, of the ritiro, the pre-match retreat, of diet and fitness, and of slogans pinned on dressing-room walls: "Class + preparation + intelligence + athleticism = championships". He was football's first modern manager in the sense that he managed everything.

## Achievements

- Two European Cups and two Intercontinental Cups with Inter
- Seven league titles in two countries (four in Spain, three in Italy)
- Two Fairs Cups with Barcelona
- The first coach to win the league with three different Spanish and Italian clubs (Atlético, Barcelona, Inter)

## Storyline

Died 1997. Catenaccio became, for better or worse, Italian football's identity for thirty years, and Herrera's Inter were its masterpiece. His influence spread in both directions: the counter-attacking, man-marking tradition that produced [Giovanni Trapattoni](/managers/giovanni-trapattoni), Marcello Lippi and Fabio Capello, and, by reaction, the pressing revolution of [Arrigo Sacchi](/managers/arrigo-sacchi), who built his Milan as an explicit answer to Herrera's Inter.

The torch today belongs to the coaches who defend deep and break fast with modern tools: [José Mourinho](/managers/jose-mourinho), who won the 2010 treble at Inter with a team Herrera would have admired and who shares his gift for theatre; Diego Simeone at Atlético; and in a different register the [back three](/tactics/back-three) systems of Antonio Conte. Inter's 2025-26 Scudetto under Cristian Chivu was won with a defence that remains the club's pride, as Il Mago intended.
