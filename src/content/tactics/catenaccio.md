## The idea

Catenaccio means "door-bolt". The system puts an extra defender, the libero or sweeper, behind a line of man-markers. The markers follow their opponents everywhere; the libero stays free, reads the game and mops up anything that gets through. Behind this bolted door the team concedes very little, and in front of it a small group of quick, direct attackers wait to counter.

It was, above all, a philosophy of risk. [Helenio Herrera](/managers/helenio-herrera) believed a 1-0 win was a perfect result, and that a team that never conceded could never lose. The cynicism is overstated; his Inter scored plenty. But the priority order was clear: defend first, defend second, then attack with what is left.

## How it works on the pitch

**The bolt.** Four defenders man-mark the opponent's four attacking players. Behind them the libero (Armando Picchi at Inter) has no marking duty; he sweeps up through-balls, doubles up where needed and starts attacks with a long pass.

**The midfield.** A deep-lying playmaker (Luis Suarez, the Spanish one, at Inter) sits in front of the defence and provides the first forward pass. Two runners alongside him do the fetching and carrying.

**The attack.** Catenaccio teams attack with few players but attack fast. Inter's left-back Giacinto Facchetti was the revolutionary piece, a full-back who surged forward into the space vacated by the opponent's winger and scored 59 goals for the club. Up front, Sandro Mazzola and Jair ran the channels.

**The game plan.** Absorb pressure, keep the shape, wait for a turnover, then hit the long ball or the overlapping full-back. If a goal comes, bolt the door and run the clock.

## Origins and evolution

The sweeper was born in Switzerland. Karl Rappan's "verrou" (bolt) at Servette and the Swiss national team in the 1930s and 1940s used a withdrawn centre-half behind the defensive line to compensate for amateur players facing professionals. Nereo Rocco brought the idea to Italy with tiny Triestina, who finished second in Serie A in 1947-48, then won two European Cups with [AC Milan](/clubs/ac-milan) (1963, 1969).

Herrera, an Argentine-born Frenchman who had coached Barcelona, perfected it at [Inter Milan](/clubs/inter-milan) from 1960. La Grande Inter won Serie A in 1963, 1965 and 1966 and the European Cup in 1964 and 1965, beating Real Madrid and Benfica in the finals. Herrera also invented the modern coaching culture: training camps, psychology, motivational slogans on the walls, and the demand that players lived like athletes.

Catenaccio declined after Celtic's attacking Lisbon Lions beat Inter in the 1967 final and Ajax's [total football](/tactics/total-football) outran it in 1972. Italian football evolved it: the libero remained in Italy into the 1990s (Baresi, Scirea), and [Arrigo Sacchi](/managers/arrigo-sacchi)'s zonal Milan finally buried man-marking at the top level. But the mentality survived in every great Italian defence, from Juventus' BBC back line to Inter's 2010 treble under [Jose Mourinho](/managers/jose-mourinho), whose 10-man rearguard at the Camp Nou was called catenaccio by everyone who watched it.

## Who does it best

The pure 1960s system is extinct, but its descendants thrive:

- **[Jose Mourinho](/managers/jose-mourinho)**, now back at [Real Madrid](/clubs/real-madrid) since July 2026, won at Camp Nou in October 2026 with a compact mid-block and fast transitions, the same pattern that beat Barcelona in 2010.
- **[Diego Simeone](/managers/diego-simeone)'s [Atletico Madrid](/clubs/atletico-madrid)** play a modern 5-3-2 bolt with Oblak as the last line.
- **[Antonio Conte](/managers/antonio-conte)** at [Napoli](/clubs/napoli) uses a [back three](/tactics/back-three) that defends as a five and springs wing-backs forward exactly as Facchetti did.
- **[Inter Milan](/clubs/inter-milan)** under Cristian Chivu, Serie A champions in 2025-26, keep the 3-5-2 and the Italian art of the compact block.
- Italy's national team under Gennaro Gattuso, and almost every Serie A side facing a stronger opponent, still default to the bolt.

## Weaknesses and counters

Man-marking can be dragged apart: a striker who drops deep takes his marker with him and opens space for runners, which is what Celtic's overlapping full-backs did in 1967 and what the Dutch rotation did in the 1970s. Because the team sits so deep, it invites sustained pressure and depends on the libero never making a mistake. The counter needs forwards fast enough to cover 70 metres alone. And catenaccio concedes the initiative; a team that cannot score first ends up chasing a game it was never set up to chase. Modern zonal [low blocks](/tactics/low-block) solved several of these problems by defending space rather than men, and [positional play](/tactics/positional-play) was in part designed to pull man-markers out of position with overloads in the half-spaces.
