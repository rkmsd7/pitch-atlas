## The idea

Direct play is the belief that the shortest route to goal is the best one. Rather than building patiently through midfield, the team moves the ball forward in as few passes as possible, usually with a long pass from the back towards a target striker or into the channels, then fights for the second ball in the opponent's half. The territory gained is the point: a team that plays in the final third wins corners, throw-ins and free-kicks, and forces the opponent to defend facing its own goal.

It is often caricatured as "long-ball football", but modern direct play is precise. The long pass has a target, the runners have patterns, the second-ball structure is drilled, and the [set pieces](/tactics/set-pieces) that follow are rehearsed.

## How it works on the pitch

**The launch.** From the goalkeeper or a centre-back, a long diagonal or straight ball towards the target man (a Haaland, Watkins or Isak type) or into the channel for a winger running behind the full-back. The trigger is often a press: when the opponent commits to pressing high, the long ball goes over them.

**The second ball.** This is where direct play lives or dies. As the long pass travels, the midfield line pushes up to be within 10-15 metres of the striker, ready to win the knock-down or the clearance. Two midfielders attack the flick-on; the wingers tuck in.

**Sustained pressure.** Once in the final third, the team keeps the ball there: throw-ins taken quickly, crosses from wide, shots from distance, every loose ball contested. Possession stats may be poor but the shot map is in the right place.

**The reverse.** Direct teams defend in a mid-block or [low block](/tactics/low-block) and win the ball deep, so the long pass is also a [counter-attack](/tactics/counter-attacking) in one movement.

```
   Launch:                      Second-ball line:
   1 ----------------> 9             11  8  6  7
                    (flick)               9
        8   6                     (ball lands here)
```

Roles: a goalkeeper with a 60-metre kick (Ederson, Pickford), a target striker who wins headers and holds the ball, box-to-box midfielders who run beyond, wingers who attack the back post, and a back line that defends the counter.

## Origins and evolution

The Wing Commander Charles Reep, an RAF accountant, analysed matches in the 1950s and concluded that most goals came from moves of three passes or fewer. His numbers were flawed (he ignored that most moves are short) but they shaped English coaching for decades: Graham Taylor's Watford and Howard Wilkinson's Leeds in the 1980s, Wimbledon's Crazy Gang FA Cup win in 1988, and the FA's 1990s coaching manuals. Jack Charlton's Ireland took it to the 1990 and 1994 World Cups.

The Guardiola era made direct play unfashionable, but it never left. Sam Allardyce's Bolton beat elite sides with it in the 2000s. Tony Pulis and Sean Dyche kept Stoke and Burnley in the Premier League with it. Then the elite took it back: [Antonio Conte](/managers/antonio-conte)'s Inter and Tottenham used Lukaku and Kane as targets for long diagonals; Guardiola's City played long to Haaland when pressed; Jurgen Klopp's Liverpool used Alisson as a launcher; [Unai Emery](/managers/unai-emery)'s Aston Villa reached the 2025 Champions League quarter-finals with Watkins running channels. The 2024-25 Premier League saw the highest proportion of long goalkeeper passes in a decade as teams found that going long over a press was safer than playing through it.

## Who does it best

- **[Antonio Conte](/managers/antonio-conte)'s [Napoli](/clubs/napoli)**: 2024-25 champions with Lukaku as the target and McTominay arriving for second balls.
- **[Diego Simeone](/managers/diego-simeone)'s [Atletico Madrid](/clubs/atletico-madrid)**: fifteen years of long diagonals and channel runs.
- **[Andoni Iraola](/managers/andoni-iraola)'s [Liverpool](/clubs/liverpool)**: Iraola's Bournemouth were the most direct pressing team in the league, and at Anfield from June 2026 he has [Alexander Isak](/players/alexander-isak) and Bradley Barcola to run beyond.
- **[Unai Emery](/managers/unai-emery)'s [Aston Villa](/clubs/aston-villa)**: a mid-block with vertical transitions.
- **[Jose Mourinho](/managers/jose-mourinho)'s [Real Madrid](/clubs/real-madrid)**: long balls into the channels for [Mbappe](/players/kylian-mbappe) and Vinicius, as in the October 2026 Clasico.
- **England's lower leagues and [Scottish Premiership](/leagues/scottish-premiership) clubs** keep the tradition alive every weekend.

## Weaknesses and counters

Long passes are low-percentage; a direct team might complete 40 percent of them and spend much of the game chasing. Against a defence with tall, aggressive centre-backs the target man is nullified and the second balls are lost. Without a striker who can hold the ball, the team is just giving possession away in a different place. A calm opponent that does not press, sits in a mid-block and lets the direct team have the ball in its own half removes the trigger. And the style can struggle to break down a [low block](/tactics/low-block) since it depends on space behind the defence; direct teams that fall behind to a deep-sitting opponent often have no plan B. The modern answer is hybrid: play short when there is time, long when there is a press.
