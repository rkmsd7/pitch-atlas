## The idea

Man-marking is the simplest defensive principle: you are responsible for one opponent, and you stay with him wherever he goes. It contrasts with zonal defending, in which each player is responsible for a space. For most of the 20th century the two coexisted: Italian [catenaccio](/tactics/catenaccio) used man-markers with a spare libero, German football used a man-marking back line with a sweeper (Beckenbauer), and English teams marked zonally in a flat back four.

The modern revival is different. It applies man-to-man principles to the whole pitch in the pressing phase: when the opponent's goalkeeper has the ball, every outfield player has an opponent to shadow, so that there is no free pass anywhere. It is bold to the point of madness and, when it works, it suffocates the opponent completely.

## How it works on the pitch

**The assignment.** Before the match the coach assigns pairings based on the opponent's build-up shape. Against a 4-3-3: striker to one centre-back, winger to the other, midfielders to midfielders, full-backs to wingers, one spare centre-back against the striker or a sweeper behind.

**The press.** As the opponent's goalkeeper prepares to play, the team pushes up and every player steps onto his man. The keeper's only options are a long ball into a 1-v-1 or a pass into a marked player with his back to goal.

**Following.** If an opponent drops deep or drifts wide, his marker goes with him. This is the hardest discipline, because it drags the shape apart; a centre-back might end up on the halfway line.

**Passing on.** Sophisticated versions allow markers to swap when an opponent runs across the pitch, which keeps the structure but requires constant communication.

**In the box.** At set pieces, man-marking means each defender tracks a runner rather than guarding a zone; most teams now use a hybrid.

```
   Opp build-up:   GK    CB  CB    FB  FB    6   8  10   W  9  W
   Our markers:          9   11    7   3     8   6  10   2  5  4
   (every pass is into a marked man; spare: none)
```

## Origins and evolution

Man-marking was the norm in continental Europe from the WM era through the 1980s. [Helenio Herrera](/managers/helenio-herrera)'s Inter man-marked with four and kept Picchi free. Italy's Claudio Gentile famously marked Maradona out of a 1982 World Cup match. West Germany's Berti Vogts followed Cruyff everywhere in the 1974 final. [Arrigo Sacchi](/managers/arrigo-sacchi) led the zonal revolution at Milan in 1987-91 and for twenty years man-marking was considered obsolete at the top level.

[Marcelo Bielsa](/managers/marcelo-bielsa) brought it back. His Argentina, Chile, Athletic Bilbao, Marseille and Leeds teams pressed man-to-man across the pitch with a spare centre-back, and the 3-3-1-3 became his trademark. His disciples spread it: Mauricio Pochettino at Southampton and Tottenham, Jorge Sampaoli, and [Andoni Iraola](/managers/andoni-iraola), who played under Bielsa at Athletic and made Bournemouth the Premier League's most intense man-marking press in 2023-26. The Red Bull school under [Ralf Rangnick](/managers/ralf-rangnick) used man-oriented pressing as one of its tools, and by the mid-2020s "man-to-man high press" was a standard counter to positional build-up.

## Who does it best

- **[Marcelo Bielsa](/managers/marcelo-bielsa)'s [Uruguay](/nations/uruguay)**: the purest version, with Valverde and Ugarte as the engines.
- **[Andoni Iraola](/managers/andoni-iraola)'s [Liverpool](/clubs/liverpool)**: since June 2026, man-to-man from the front with Van Dijk as the spare.
- **[Roberto De Zerbi](/managers/roberto-de-zerbi)'s [Tottenham Hotspur](/clubs/tottenham-hotspur)**: De Zerbi uses man-oriented pressing to force the opponent long, and plays his own build-up to beat man-markers.
- **[RB Leipzig](/clubs/rb-leipzig)** and the Red Bull network.
- **Serie A** keeps the tradition at set pieces and in [low blocks](/tactics/low-block): [Inter Milan](/clubs/inter-milan) and [Atalanta](/clubs/atalanta) under Gasperini were famous for man-to-man across the pitch.
- Brendan Rodgers' Celtic and most of the [Scottish Premiership](/leagues/scottish-premiership) use man-oriented pressing against weaker build-up.

## Key numbers

Man-marking presses force more long balls than any other scheme: Bournemouth under Iraola made opponents go long on more than half of their goal kicks in 2024-25, the highest share in the Premier League, and won the ball in the final third more often than any side outside the top four. The price is distance: Bielsa's Leeds covered around 118 kilometres a match in 2020-21, three or four more than the league average, which is the physical bill the system always presents by March.

## Weaknesses and counters

The great weakness is the one-against-one it invites everywhere: a single lost duel means a free runner with nobody to cover. Positional teams beat man-marking by rotation (a full-back comes inside, a midfielder drops: who follows whom?) and by third-man runs. A dropping striker drags a centre-back out of the line and opens space behind for a winger, the [false nine](/tactics/false-nine) idea. The goalkeeper as an extra passer creates the free man the system cannot account for. Long balls into a fast striker against the spare defender are a straight race. And it is the most physically demanding system in football; Bielsa's and Iraola's teams tend to tire by spring. Hybrid zonal-man schemes, with a zonal back line and man-oriented midfield, are the compromise most clubs settle on.
