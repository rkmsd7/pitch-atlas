## The idea

A back three changes the maths of the pitch. With three central defenders, the team always has a spare man against two strikers, and the two wide defenders become wing-backs who patrol the full touchline, providing all the width in attack and dropping to make a five in defence. The shape is elastic: 3-4-3 in possession, 5-4-1 or 5-3-2 without it.

The attraction for coaches is the clarity of roles. Centre-backs defend and build. Wing-backs run. The midfield two or three control the centre. Two number tens or inside forwards fill the half-spaces behind a striker. Every lane is occupied without needing full-backs to invert or midfielders to drop.

## How it works on the pitch

**Build-up.** The three centre-backs spread across the width of the penalty area, with the central one (the "libero" in all but name) stepping into midfield when space allows. The wing-backs push to midfield height, pinning the opponent's wingers back. Against a two-man press, the spare centre-back always has time.

**Progression.** The classic 3-4-3 pattern: centre-back to wing-back, wing-back to the inside forward in the half-space, inside forward turns and feeds the far-side wing-back or the striker. Conte's Chelsea ran it to perfection with Moses and Alonso wide and Hazard and Pedro inside.

**Attacking shape.** 3-2-5: three centre-backs, two holding midfielders, and a front five of two wing-backs, two inside forwards and a striker. The 3-5-2 version uses two strikers and three midfielders, with the wing-backs as the only width.

**Defending.** The wing-backs retreat to form a five; the inside forwards drop to make a four in front; the striker screens. The back five means crosses can be defended with numbers and the half-spaces are always covered.

**Set pieces.** With three tall centre-backs, the back three is a natural [set-piece](/tactics/set-pieces) weapon.

```
   In possession (3-2-5):       Out of possession (5-4-1):
      5    9    11                       9
    10        8                   11  8    6   10
         6     4                 5  3  4  2  7
      3    4    2                     1
           1
```

## Origins and evolution

The first back threes were the WM of Herbert Chapman's Arsenal in the 1920s, with the centre-half dropping between two full-backs. The modern three-at-the-back arrived in the 1980s: Carlos Bilardo's Argentina won the 1986 World Cup with a 3-5-2 built around Maradona, and Franz Beckenbauer's West Germany used a libero-based three in 1990. Italy's Serie A in the 1990s was full of 3-5-2s (Parma, Lazio, Juventus).

The 2010s revival began with [Antonio Conte](/managers/antonio-conte), who won three Serie A titles at [Juventus](/clubs/juventus) (2012-14) with a 3-5-2 and then switched [Chelsea](/clubs/chelsea) to a 3-4-3 in September 2016 and won 13 games in a row and the Premier League. [Thomas Tuchel](/managers/thomas-tuchel) won the 2021 Champions League with Chelsea in a 3-4-2-1. Simone Inzaghi's [Inter Milan](/clubs/inter-milan) reached the 2023 and 2025 Champions League finals with a 3-5-2 of extraordinary fluency. [Xabi Alonso](/managers/xabi-alonso)'s [Bayer Leverkusen](/clubs/bayer-leverkusen) went unbeaten in the 2023-24 Bundesliga with a 3-4-2-1 in which Grimaldo and Frimpong were full-backs, wingers and goalscorers at once.

## Who does it best

- **[Xabi Alonso](/managers/xabi-alonso)'s [Chelsea](/clubs/chelsea)** (from July 2026): the Leverkusen model transplanted to London, with Morgan Rogers, a British-record signing, as one of the two tens behind the striker and wing-backs supplying the width.
- **[Antonio Conte](/managers/antonio-conte)'s [Napoli](/clubs/napoli)**: 2024-25 champions, a 3-4-2-1 that defends as a five and attacks through the wing-backs.
- **[Inter Milan](/clubs/inter-milan)** under Cristian Chivu, 2025-26 Serie A champions, inheritors of Inzaghi's 3-5-2.
- **[Cesc Fabregas](/managers/cesc-fabregas)' [Como](/clubs/como)**: the most watchable 3-4-3 in Italy, positional play from a back three.
- **[Thomas Tuchel](/managers/thomas-tuchel)'s [England](/nations/england)**: reached the 2026 World Cup semi-finals, and beat France 6-4 for third place, with a back three at key moments.
- **[Hansi Flick](/managers/hansi-flick)** has used a three in build-up at [Barcelona](/clubs/barcelona) with a full-back stepping into midfield.

## Key numbers

A back three concedes fewer crosses per game than a back four because the wing-backs are already deep, but it tends to concede more shots from the half-spaces. Conte's 2016-17 Chelsea allowed under nine shots a game after switching to the 3-4-3, the lowest in the league that season, and Alonso's unbeaten Leverkusen scored more goals from wing-back positions than any team in Europe in 2023-24. The role is also the most demanding physically: elite wing-backs cover close to 12 kilometres a match and record more high-intensity sprints than any other outfield position.

## Weaknesses and counters

Wing-backs have the hardest job in football and need extraordinary fitness; if they tire, the shape becomes a flat, passive five with no width. The half-spaces between wing-back and outside centre-back are the classic weak point: a winger who stays wide and a midfielder who runs the half-space can isolate the outside centre-back. Against a single striker, three centre-backs can be one too many, leaving the midfield outnumbered, which is why many back-three teams switch to a four mid-game. [Inverted full-backs](/tactics/inverted-full-backs) in a 3-2-5 are, in a sense, the positional-play answer: get the same shape with fewer specialist defenders. And fast [counter-attacks](/tactics/counter-attacking) into the channels behind advanced wing-backs are the surest way to beat a 3-4-3.
