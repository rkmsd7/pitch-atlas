## The idea

A pressing trap is pressing with a plan rather than with energy. Instead of chasing every ball, the team shapes its block so that one option looks safe for the opponent, usually a pass to a full-back or a wide centre-back, and then, the moment the pass is played, closes it down from several sides at once. The player receiving is on the touchline, facing the wrong way, with no team-mate in reach. The ball is won, and the [counter-attack](/tactics/counter-attacking) begins in the opponent's half.

The trap is the thinking person's press. It uses the touchline as an extra defender, the opponent's own passing habits as the trigger, and the team's compactness to guarantee that when the trap is sprung, three or four players arrive together.

## How it works on the pitch

**The bait.** The two strikers in a 4-4-2 stand just off the centre-backs, angled to block the pass into midfield but leaving the wide pass to the full-back open. The ball-side winger is a few metres too deep to intercept, on purpose.

**The trigger.** The centre-back plays to the full-back. That is the signal. The striker curves his run to block the return pass; the winger jumps to the full-back; the ball-side central midfielder pushes up to cut the inside pass; the full-back behind steps up to the opponent's winger.

**The cage.** Four players surround the receiver in a 10-metre box against the touchline. He can play long (where the centre-backs are ready), play back (where the striker has blocked it), or lose the ball.

**The strike.** On winning possession the first pass goes forward immediately to the far-side forward, who is already running into the space the opponent's full-back left.

**Variants.** Central traps that leave a pass into the pivot open and then collapse on him from behind (Simeone, Emery). Goalkeeper traps that allow a pass back and then press the keeper's weak foot. Set-piece traps on goal kicks.

```
   Bait: pass CB -> FB is "open"
        9    10         (strikers block centre)
     11 ........ 2(opp FB) <- ball goes here
        8    6          (8 jumps inside lane)
     3                  (3 steps to opp winger)
   Result: 11, 8, 9, 3 close in a 10 m box
```

## Origins and evolution

Pressing traps exist in every well-coached pressing team, but the German school made them explicit. [Ralf Rangnick](/managers/ralf-rangnick) and the Red Bull coaching network trained "pressing triggers" and "Pressingfalle" from the 2000s: Hoffenheim, Salzburg and [RB Leipzig](/clubs/rb-leipzig) all learned to leave the full-back open. [Julian Nagelsmann](/managers/julian-nagelsmann) added positional sophistication at Hoffenheim and Leipzig, with asymmetric traps on one side.

[Diego Simeone](/managers/diego-simeone)'s [Atletico Madrid](/clubs/atletico-madrid) 4-4-2 of 2012-2016 was the mid-block version: wait, invite the pass wide, trap, counter. [Unai Emery](/managers/unai-emery) built Villarreal's 2021 Europa League and Aston Villa's rise on the same logic. In the Premier League, [Andoni Iraola](/managers/andoni-iraola)'s Bournemouth (2023-2026) used the most aggressive man-oriented traps in the division, often springing them from a [high line](/tactics/high-line) at halfway. [Luis Enrique](/managers/luis-enrique)'s PSG won two Champions Leagues with a press that is not a swarm but a set of coordinated traps, especially against teams that build with a back three.

## Who does it best

- **[Andoni Iraola](/managers/andoni-iraola)'s [Liverpool](/clubs/liverpool)**: appointed in June 2026, he brought his Bournemouth traps to Anfield: man-to-man across the pitch with the full-back the designated target.
- **[Unai Emery](/managers/unai-emery)'s [Aston Villa](/clubs/aston-villa)**: a 4-4-2 mid-block with precise triggers.
- **[Diego Simeone](/managers/diego-simeone)'s [Atletico Madrid](/clubs/atletico-madrid)**: the mid-block trap in its purest form.
- **[Luis Enrique](/managers/luis-enrique)'s [Paris Saint-Germain](/clubs/paris-saint-germain)**: traps on the opponent's first pass, built into the positional structure.
- **[Julian Nagelsmann](/managers/julian-nagelsmann)** and the Red Bull alumni ([RB Leipzig](/clubs/rb-leipzig), Salzburg).
- **[Marcelo Bielsa](/managers/marcelo-bielsa)**, whose man-marking press is one giant trap.

## Weaknesses and counters

The trap relies on the opponent taking the bait. A team that recognises the open pass as a trap and plays long instead, or that moves the ball too fast for the cage to form, turns the trap into a gap. Teams that build with a goalkeeper as an extra passer (Ederson, Ter Stegen) can play through or over the trap. Third-man combinations, where the full-back receives and lays the ball straight back to a midfielder who has arrived behind the pressing striker, are the classic escape, which is why [positional play](/tactics/positional-play) coaches spend so long on them. If the trap is sprung and fails, the pressing team has four players on one side of the pitch and the far side is open for a switch. And a trap-based press must be perfectly synchronised; one player late and the receiver has time to turn.
