## The idea

Relationism (jogo de relacao, or "functional play") is the name given to a style that rejects the zonal grid of [positional play](/tactics/positional-play). Instead of spreading across five lanes and keeping distance, the players come close to the ball, often six or seven of them within 20 metres, and build attacks through short combinations, one-twos, dribbles and overlaps. The structure is not drawn on a whiteboard; it emerges from the relationships between players in the moment.

[Fernando Diniz](/managers/fernando-diniz), the Brazilian coach who made the term famous, calls it football played "with the ball as the centre of the universe". Where Guardiola's teams find the free man by occupying space, Diniz's teams create him by overloading one side so heavily that the opponent cannot match numbers, then switching or breaking through. It is jazz to positional play's orchestral score.

## How it works on the pitch

**Approximation.** When a player receives the ball, team-mates move towards him rather than away. Full-backs come inside, midfielders cluster, a winger from the far side drifts across. The "tilt" leaves the far side almost empty on purpose.

**Tabela and toco y me voy.** Short wall-passes and give-and-goes are the basic unit. A player passes and immediately moves to receive again, dragging a marker with him and opening a lane for someone else.

**The escadinha.** A diagonal staircase of players, each a few metres further forward, so that the ball can always go up one step. This Brazilian idea replaces the positional W.

**The switch.** Once the overload has pulled the opponent across, a long diagonal to the lone wide player on the far side, who is now in acres of space. Fluminense's left-side clusters and right-side switches to Samuel Xavier were the signature.

**Defending.** Relationist teams press immediately on turnover because so many players are near the ball. The danger is what happens when the press is beaten, because the shape is deliberately unbalanced.

## Origins and evolution

Relationism is a modern label for something old. Brazilian football of the 1970s and 1980s, the Flamengo of Zico and Junior, the Corinthians Democracy of Socrates, Tele Santana's 1982 Brazil, was built on approximation, improvisation and the tabela. Argentine and Uruguayan football has similar roots. European coaching, from [Rinus Michels](/managers/rinus-michels)' positional discipline to Guardiola's grid, pushed the game towards structure; South America kept the other tradition alive.

Diniz, a former midfielder, began articulating the ideas at Audax and Athletico Paranaense in the 2010s, then at Fluminense, where in 2023 he won the Copa Libertadores with a team of veterans (Marcelo, Felipe Melo, Cano, Ganso) playing the most daring football in the world. He briefly coached [Brazil](/nations/brazil) in 2023-24 before [Carlo Ancelotti](/managers/carlo-ancelotti) took over. European analysts, notably Jamie Hamilton, named the style "relationism" and set it up as the great alternative to the positional orthodoxy.

By 2026 the influence was visible in Europe: Ancelotti's Real Madrid had long allowed Vinicius, Rodrygo and Bellingham to cluster and improvise; [Roberto De Zerbi](/managers/roberto-de-zerbi)'s build-up uses deliberate approximation; [Marcelo Bielsa](/managers/marcelo-bielsa)'s teams rotate with a freedom that is closer to Diniz than to Guardiola.

## Who does it best

- **[Fernando Diniz](/managers/fernando-diniz)** in Brazil: the originator, whose clubs remain the reference for the style, whatever his current bench. His 2023 Fluminense Libertadores side is the canonical team.
- **[Flamengo](/clubs/flamengo)**: the 2025 Brasileirao and Libertadores winners under Filipe Luis play a more structured Brazilian game, but De Arrascaeta's clusters with the forwards are relationist in instinct.
- **[Carlo Ancelotti](/managers/carlo-ancelotti)'s [Brazil](/nations/brazil)**: Ancelotti has always let his attackers relate freely; with Vinicius, Raphinha and Rodrygo the Selecao's left-side approximation is the modern expression.
- **[Roberto De Zerbi](/managers/roberto-de-zerbi)'s [Tottenham Hotspur](/clubs/tottenham-hotspur)**: his baited build-up with the ball under the foot and players crowding close is a European cousin.
- **[Marcelo Bielsa](/managers/marcelo-bielsa)** with [Uruguay](/nations/uruguay).

## Weaknesses and counters

The deliberate imbalance means that a lost ball on the overloaded side leaves the far flank and the centre open; Fluminense conceded many goals this way and were beaten 4-0 by Manchester City in the 2023 Club World Cup final. The style depends on exceptional ball-carriers and quick thinkers, and on a coach willing to accept chaos; it is hard to teach and harder to replicate. Positional teams with a strong [counter-press](/tactics/gegenpressing) can smother the clusters before the combinations develop. And a disciplined [low block](/tactics/low-block) that refuses to shift across can turn the overloads into crowded, static passing in a small space. Critics argue relationism is a description, not a method; admirers answer that Guardiola himself has said he wants his players to "relate".
