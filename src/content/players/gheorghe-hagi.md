## Overview

Gheorghe Hagi is the greatest Romanian footballer and one of the finest left-footed playmakers of any era. He scored 76 goals in 97 games for Steaua Bucharest and reached a European Cup final at 24, played for [Real Madrid](/clubs/real-madrid) and [Barcelona](/clubs/barcelona) without quite conquering either, produced the performances of the 1994 [World Cup](/tournaments/world-cup) for Romania, and then, aged 31 and supposedly finished, built the most successful period in Turkish club history at [Galatasaray](/clubs/galatasaray), including the 2000 UEFA Cup. Romania's record scorer and most capped outfield player, he was known as the Maradona of the Carpathians, and [Diego Maradona](/players/diego-maradona) himself said he was happy to share the comparison.

## Early life and breakthrough

Hagi was born in February 1965 in Săcele, near Constanța, into an Aromanian family. He played for Farul Constanța as a 17-year-old and moved to Sportul Studențesc in Bucharest in 1983, where he scored 58 goals in four seasons and was league top scorer twice. He made his Romania debut at 18, against Norway in August 1983.

In 1987 the Ceaușescu regime arranged a one-match loan to Steaua, the army club, for the European Super Cup against Dynamo Kyiv; Hagi scored the winner and Steaua simply kept him. Under Anghel Iordănescu, with Marius Lăcătuș and Gavril Balint beside him, he won three consecutive doubles and reached the 1989 European Cup final in Barcelona, where Arrigo Sacchi's [AC Milan](/clubs/ac-milan) won 4-0. Steaua's 1988-89 side went the whole league season unbeaten.

## Peak years

Real Madrid paid about $4 million for him after the 1990 World Cup, and Italy's Brescia, in Serie B, took him in 1992 after two uneven seasons in Spain. The 1994 World Cup changed the way the world saw him. Romania beat Colombia 3-1 in Pasadena, Hagi scoring with a 35-yard shot that bent over Óscar Córdoba from the left touchline, then eliminated [Argentina](/nations/argentina) 3-2 in a classic, Hagi scoring the third after a sweeping counter-attack. Romania lost to Sweden on penalties in the quarter-final, having been seconds from a semi-final, and Hagi was named in the team of the tournament.

Barcelona signed him for the 1994-95 season but [Johan Cruyff](/players/johan-cruyff) used him sparingly in a side being rebuilt. Galatasaray, under Fatih Terim, paid $2 million in 1996 and got five years of Hagi at his most influential: four straight league titles, two cups, the 2000 UEFA Cup won on penalties against [Arsenal](/clubs/arsenal) in Copenhagen, the first European trophy for a Turkish club, and the 2000 UEFA Super Cup against Real Madrid. He also led Romania to the quarter-finals of Euro 2000, where he was sent off against [Italy](/nations/italy) in his last international.

## How he played

Hagi was a classic number 10 who could win a match from any position on the pitch. His left foot was the whole instrument: long diagonals that dropped on a winger's run, curled crosses, passes clipped over a defence, and shots from anywhere beyond 25 yards struck with a dipping topspin that swerved late. The lob against Colombia was no fluke; he had been scoring from the touchline since Sportul.

He had a slow-fast rhythm. He walked, scanned, received on the half-turn with a first touch that stopped the ball dead, and then burst two or three strides into space before the pass or the shot. He was not a dribbler of Maradona's kind, more a one-touch eliminator, using a feint and a change of pace. He took every free-kick within 35 yards.

His limitations were tempo and temperament. He did not press, drifted out of matches when the ball did not come to him, and collected red cards for dissent and retaliation; he was sent off in his final match for both club and country. In modern football he would be a free-roaming 10 in a 4-2-3-1 or a left-sided 8 in a 4-3-3, trusted with set pieces and the final pass, asked to do little without the ball. A less mobile Kevin De Bruyne with a thunderous left foot is the nearest modern shape.

## Evolution

Hagi's career divides into three acts. At Sportul and Steaua he was a goalscoring inside-forward who played high and shot often. At Madrid, Brescia and Barcelona he was a creator in sides that did not fully trust him, and the frustration of those years shows in the 35 goals from 124 caps, few of them before 1994. At Galatasaray he became the complete playmaker: deeper, slower, more generous, conducting Terim's counter-attacking 4-4-2 and taking the big set pieces. The sport around him moved from the man-marking of the 1980s to zonal pressing, and Hagi's late peak in Turkey owed something to a league where he still had time on the ball. He retired in 2001 at 36 with a testimonial in Bucharest against a World XI.

## Legacy

Hagi is the reference point for all Romanian football. He has run the Gheorghe Hagi Academy and the club Farul Constanța since 2009, producing a generation of internationals including his son Ianis, and he has managed the national side and Galatasaray. In Turkey he is "Comandante", the player who proved that a Turkish club could win in Europe and whose success under Fatih Terim created the Galatasaray that reached the 2001 Champions League quarter-final.

Stylistically he sits with [Hristo Stoichkov](/players/hristo-stoichkov) in the generation of Eastern European left-footers who flourished once the borders opened, and with [Michel Platini](/players/michel-platini) and [Zinedine Zidane](/players/zinedine-zidane) in the line of number 10s who controlled games with the pass and the long shot. The 1994 Romania side, with Hagi, Dan Petrescu, Ilie Dumitrescu and Florin Răducioiu, remains the model for how a small nation can play with ambition at a [World Cup](/tournaments/world-cup). He was named Romania's Golden Player at UEFA's 50th anniversary and played in the Galatasaray team of the century.
