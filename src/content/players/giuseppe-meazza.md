## Overview

Giuseppe Meazza is the reason the stadium shared by [Inter](/clubs/inter-milan) and [AC Milan](/clubs/ac-milan) carries a player's name. "Il Balilla", the kid, scored 287 goals in 408 games for Inter between 1927 and 1940, won two [World Cups](/tournaments/world-cup) as the leader of Vittorio Pozzo's [Italy](/nations/italy), and was the first Italian footballer to be a celebrity: a dandy who slept in brothels before matches, turned up late and was forgiven because nobody else could do what he did with a ball. He remains Inter's second-highest scorer and the only man to captain Italy to a World Cup win and a Central European title in the same decade.

## Early life and breakthrough

Meazza was born in Milan in August 1910 and lost his father in the First World War. He grew up in the Porta Vittoria district kicking rag balls barefoot, and was rejected by Milan for being too thin before Inter's scouts took him at 12. Inter's coach Árpád Weisz, the great Hungarian, promoted him to the first team at 17. He scored twice on his league debut in September 1927 against Unione Sportiva Milanese and finished his first full season with 33 goals in 29 games.

By 1929-30, the first season of the national Serie A, Meazza was its top scorer with 31 goals as the club, renamed Ambrosiana by the Fascist regime, won the title. He debuted for Italy in February 1930 at 19 and scored twice against Switzerland. The nickname "Balilla", the name of the Fascist youth organisation, was given to him because he looked like a boy among men.

## Peak years

Meazza's peak ran from 1930 to 1938, bracketed by two World Cups. In 1934 on home soil he was Italy's best player, scoring against the United States and Spain and, by Pozzo's account, deciding the final against Czechoslovakia despite playing injured. In 1938 in France he was captain. He scored the penalty in the semi-final against Brazil, famously while holding up his shorts after the elastic snapped, and led the 4-2 final win over [Hungary](/nations/hungary) in Paris. He finished with 33 goals in 53 caps, a record that stood until Luigi Riva broke it in 1973.

For Inter, he was top scorer in Serie A three times, won the title again in 1938 and 1940, and the club's first Coppa Italia in 1939. A circulation problem in his left foot, probably caused by his lifestyle, kept him out for most of 1939 and ended his Inter career. A loan to AC Milan in 1940 made him one of the few men to play for both Milan clubs, and he wandered through Juventus, Varese and Atalanta before a farewell season at Inter in 1946-47 as player-coach.

## How he played

Meazza was short, around 1.69 m, with heavy thighs and a low centre of gravity. He began as a centre-forward and moved to inside-right in Pozzo's "metodo" system, a 2-3-2-3 in which the inside-forwards dropped into midfield to link the halves to the attack. That suited him completely: he liked to come towards the ball, receive with his back to goal, turn and run at defenders. Contemporary accounts describe dribbling in which the ball never seemed to leave his foot and a body feint that sent defenders the wrong way.

His finishing was intelligent rather than violent. He placed shots, often chipped the keeper, and liked to dribble round the goalkeeper and walk the ball in, a trick that made him a crowd favourite and infuriated opponents. His penalties were taken without a run-up, a glance at the keeper and a side-footed pass into the corner. He was a good header for his size and created as much as he scored. Weaknesses: his defensive contribution was nil, his fitness was managed around his night life, and the physical leagues of northern Europe might have bruised him. In today's game he is a second striker or a false nine, a [Sergio Agüero](/players/sergio-aguero) type with playmaking added, and his ability to take the ball under pressure in the half-spaces would translate directly into [positional play](/tactics/positional-play).

## Evolution

Meazza's career spanned the shift from the 2-3-5 pyramid to the WM and Pozzo's metodo. As a teenager at Inter he was an out-and-out centre-forward scoring 30 a season; Pozzo made him a deeper inside-forward because Italy had Angelo Schiavio and later Silvio Piola to lead the line, and because Meazza's intelligence was more useful in the build-up. By 1938 he was effectively a playmaking captain. His later years, in wartime Italy, were an elegant decline on one good foot. Football itself changed around him from a game of amateur-era dribblers to a professional, organised sport in which Italy, under Pozzo, pioneered the tactical discipline that would later harden into [catenaccio](/tactics/catenaccio).

## Legacy

After retiring Meazza coached Inter three times, took Atalanta and Beşiktaş, and ran Inter's youth sector, where he mentored Sandro Mazzola, whose father Valentino had been Meazza's successor as Italy's leading man. He died in August 1979, two days before his 69th birthday, and the following March the Stadio San Siro was renamed in his honour. His place in [Italy](/nations/italy)'s story is foundational: the first two of Italy's four World Cups were his, and the archetype of the Italian "fantasista", the small, clever forward who creates from nothing, begins with him and runs through [Gianni Rivera](/players/gianni-rivera), [Roberto Baggio](/players/roberto-baggio), Totti and Del Piero. At [Inter](/clubs/inter-milan) only Giuseppe Meazza, among all the legends, has the ground.
