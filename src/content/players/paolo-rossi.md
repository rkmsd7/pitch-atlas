## Overview

Few footballers have had a summer like Paolo Rossi's in 1982. He had not played a competitive match in two years, suspended over a betting scandal he always denied. Enzo Bearzot picked him anyway, he scored nothing in the first four matches, and then he scored six in three: a hat-trick against [Brazil](/nations/brazil), two against Poland and the opener in the [World Cup](/tournaments/world-cup) final against West [Germany](/nations/germany). He finished as top scorer and best player of the tournament, won the Ballon d'Or that December, and became "Pablito", the name Italy still uses for him.

## Early life and breakthrough

Rossi was born in Prato, Tuscany, in September 1956, and joined [Juventus](/clubs/juventus) as a 16-year-old winger. Three knee operations before he was 20 nearly finished him; Juventus loaned him to Como and then to Vicenza in Serie B, where coach Giovan Battista Fabbri moved him from the wing to centre-forward. The change made him. He scored 21 goals to take Vicenza up in 1977 and 24 in the top flight in 1977-78, when the promoted side finished second, making him the first player to top-score in Serie B and Serie A in consecutive seasons.

Bearzot took him to the 1978 World Cup in Argentina, where he scored three goals and was named in the team of the tournament as [Italy](/nations/italy) finished fourth, playing the most attractive football of the competition. Vicenza won a blind auction with Juventus for his full ownership in 1978, paying a then-record 2.6 billion lire.

## Peak years

Vicenza were relegated in 1979 and Rossi was loaned to Perugia. In March 1980 the Totonero betting investigation accused him of fixing a 2-2 draw against Avellino in which he had scored twice. He was banned for three years, reduced to two on appeal. Juventus bought him anyway in 1981 and he returned for the last three league games of 1981-82, enough for Bearzot, who had been pilloried for keeping faith with him.

After the slow start in Spain, the second-round match against Brazil at Sarrià in Barcelona made him immortal: a header from Antonio Cabrini's cross, a tap-in after Cerezo's loose pass, and a close-range finish from Marco Tardelli's shot in a 3-2 win over the most gifted side of the era. Two goals against Poland in the semi-final and the far-post header to open the final against West Germany followed. He was 25.

At Juventus under Giovanni Trapattoni, alongside [Michel Platini](/players/michel-platini), Zbigniew Boniek and the 1982 Italy core, he won the Scudetto in 1982 and 1984, the Cup Winners' Cup in 1984 and the European Cup in 1985, the Heysel final overshadowed by the deaths of 39 supporters.

## How he played

Rossi was 1.74 m and slim, with no great strength and only modest pace over distance. What he had was the quickest reading of a loose ball in the game. He positioned himself a fraction off the shoulder of a centre-back, waited for the defender's eyes to go to the ball, and moved. His goals were almost all from inside ten yards: tap-ins, near-post flicks, headers at the far post from crosses he had anticipated before they were struck.

He was two-footed in the box and a clean striker of a bouncing ball, but his best attribute was the first touch that was also the finish. He did not need a second. He rarely came short to link play; Bearzot's Italy and Trapattoni's Juventus used Bruno Conti, Platini and Boniek to create, and Rossi to arrive.

His weaknesses were everything outside the box. He gave little in build-up, was not a presser and could disappear for long stretches, as the first round of 1982 showed. Modern analytics would love him and modern coaches would struggle with him: a pure poacher in the tradition of [Gerd Müller](/players/gerd-muller) and later Filippo Inzaghi, who would need a system built to supply him rather than ask him to participate.

## Evolution

Rossi began as a winger and finished as a centre-forward, the switch at Vicenza defining him. The two-year ban stole his physical peak; he was never as sharp after 1982 as before 1980, and the knee operations of his teens left him fragile. At Juventus he adjusted by playing off Platini, drifting to the far post and leaving the centre to the Frenchman's late runs, which is how both scored so freely in 1983-84. Brief spells at [AC Milan](/clubs/ac-milan) and Hellas Verona ended his career at 30. He played in the last years before pressing and [high lines](/tactics/high-line) made the static poacher a luxury, and his style of goal has since become rarer at the top level.

## Legacy

Rossi's 1982 is the most famous redemption story in Italian sport and a reference point for every tournament striker since: Toto Schillaci in 1990, Roberto Baggio in 1994 and Luca Toni in 2006 were all measured against Pablito. The three weeks also settled an argument about Bearzot's method, trust in a settled group, silence with the press, and a belief in form arriving late, that Marcello Lippi copied in 2006.

As a player type, Rossi is the Italian link between Müller and Inzaghi, the poacher who proves that anticipation can beat athleticism. Juventus fans place him beside [Michel Platini](/players/michel-platini) and [Dino Zoff](/players/dino-zoff) in the Trapattoni side that won every competition it entered, and Vicenza renamed their stadium's stand for him. He died in December 2020 at 64; the whole 1982 team carried his coffin through Vicenza, and [Serie A](/leagues/serie-a) stopped for a minute to remember the man who had made a nation sing in the summer of 1982.
