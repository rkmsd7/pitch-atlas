## Overview

Lev Yashin is the only goalkeeper to have won the Ballon d'Or, in 1963, and the trophy FIFA and France Football give to the world's best keeper is named for him. He played his whole career for Dynamo Moscow, won Olympic gold in 1956 and the first European Championship in 1960, and is credited with saving around 150 penalties in a career of more than 800 matches. More than any single save, his legacy is a way of playing the position: off the line, into the box, sweeping behind the defence and shouting at it, which goalkeepers now treat as standard and which in the 1950s was considered reckless. He wore black, hence the Black Spider, and a flat cap, and he was the first global star of the Soviet game.

## Early life and breakthrough

Yashin was born on 22 October 1929 in Moscow to factory-worker parents and was working in a munitions factory at 13 during the war. He joined Dynamo Moscow's youth side in 1949 as a goalkeeper in both football and ice hockey, and won the Soviet ice hockey cup in 1953 before choosing football. His football debut in 1950 was a disaster, a collision with his own defender that gifted a goal, and he spent three years behind the veteran Alexei Khomich.

He became Dynamo's first choice in 1953 and was capped in September 1954 against Sweden in a 7-0 win. He kept goal as the USSR beat Yugoslavia 1-0 in the 1956 Olympic final in Melbourne and played in the 1958 [World Cup](/tournaments/world-cup), where he faced Pelé and [Garrincha](/players/garrincha) for the first time.

## Peak years

In 1960 the Soviet Union won the first European Nations Cup, beating Yugoslavia 2-1 after extra time in Paris, with Yashin's goalkeeping the reason it went to extra time at all. He was the dominant keeper in the world from then until 1966, with one bad tournament: at the 1962 World Cup he made two errors against Colombia in a 4-4 draw and was blamed for the quarter-final defeat to Chile.

The answer came at Wembley in October 1963, when the Football Association's centenary match between [England](/nations/england) and a Rest of the World XI gave him a stage. He played the first half, made a string of saves that the English crowd had not thought possible, and was given a standing ovation. Two months later he won the Ballon d'Or ahead of [Gianni Rivera](/players/gianni-rivera). He played in the 1964 European Championship final, lost 2-1 to [Spain](/nations/spain) in Madrid, and at the 1966 World Cup took the Soviets to fourth place, their best finish, losing the semi-final to West Germany with a man sent off and another injured.

## How he played

Yashin's innovation was the idea that the goalkeeper is the last defender rather than a separate player. He positioned himself high, on the edge of the six-yard box or beyond, so that through-balls could be swept up and crosses attacked before a forward could reach them. He punched crosses rather than catching them when the box was crowded, a habit that English observers in 1963 found strange and then admired, and he threw the ball out to start attacks rather than kicking long, often to a full-back on the far side of the pitch.

The physical gifts were real too. He was tall, with long arms and the reflexes of a hockey goaltender, and he saved penalties by reading the taker's run-up and waiting. Weaknesses: he was prone to the occasional rush of blood, which the 1962 errors showed, and his style depended on a defence that trusted him. In the modern game he would be the complete sweeper-keeper, a Manuel Neuer or Ederson, and he would be the first choice for any coach who wanted the keeper to be part of a [high line](/tactics/high-line) and the first pass of a build-up.

## Evolution

The young Yashin was a conventional shot-stopper learning not to collide with his own defenders. By the mid-1950s he had developed the off-the-line style under Dynamo coach Mikhail Yakushin, who built the team's attacking game on the assumption that Yashin would cover the space behind a pushed-up defence. After the criticism of 1962 he became, if anything, more aggressive, and the 1963 to 1966 version was the complete keeper: sweeping, commanding and saving. He played on for Dynamo until 1970, retiring at 41 with a farewell match in Moscow attended by Pelé, Eusébio, Charlton and [Franz Beckenbauer](/players/franz-beckenbauer) in front of 100,000.

Goalkeeping changed around him in his image. The British keeper of 1963 stayed on his line and caught everything; by the 1970s the better keepers in every country were coming for crosses and sweeping, and the back-pass rule of 1992 made Yashin's approach compulsory.

## Legacy

Every goalkeeper who leaves the line is Yashin's student. The sweeper-keeper of the modern game, Neuer, Ederson, Alisson and the Unai Simón who won the Golden Glove at the 2026 World Cup, plays the position he outlined in the 1950s, and the goalkeeper's role as a passer and organiser follows from his insistence on being a defender. [Dino Zoff](/players/dino-zoff), Gordon Banks and Sepp Maier all named him as the model, and Banks in particular credited the 1963 Wembley match with changing how English keepers thought.

The Soviet Union made him a hero, and his image was on the official poster of the 2018 World Cup in Russia. FIFA put him in its World Cup all-time team in 1994, and the Ballon d'Or's Yashin Trophy followed in 2019. He lost a leg to thrombosis in 1986 and died in Moscow on 20 March 1990 at 60. The all-black kit, the cap and the stare from the edge of the box are still the picture of what a goalkeeper should be.
