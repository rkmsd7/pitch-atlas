## Overview

Carlos Alberto Torres scored the goal that ends every film about the 1970 [World Cup](/tournaments/world-cup). Nine Brazilians touched the ball, Clodoaldo dribbled past four Italians, Jairzinho crossed the pitch, and [Pelé](/players/pele) rolled the ball into the path of a right-back who had sprinted 60 yards to hit it first time into the corner. Four minutes later the captain lifted the Jules Rimet trophy, which [Brazil](/nations/brazil) kept forever as three-time winners. He was 25 and had already won four Paulista titles playing beside Pelé at Santos; he would go on to win four more championships in the United States with [Franz Beckenbauer](/players/franz-beckenbauer). But he will always be the man who finished that goal.

## Early life and breakthrough

Carlos Alberto was born in Rio de Janeiro in July 1944 and joined Fluminense's youth system, debuting in 1963 and winning the Rio championship in 1964. He made his Brazil debut the same year, at 19, against England in the Maracanã. In 1966, having been left out of the World Cup squad in a notorious selection, he was sold to Santos for the then-substantial sum of 200 million cruzeiros, to play in the side that Pelé had made the most famous club in the world.

At Santos he won the Paulista title in 1967, 1968, 1969 and 1973, the 1968 Roberto Gomes Pedrosa tournament, effectively a national championship, and the Recopa. He played 445 games for the club over eight years, learned to attack from full-back on Santos' long foreign tours, and was made national captain by João Saldanha in 1969 for the World Cup qualifiers, in which Brazil won all six matches.

## Peak years

The 1970 tournament in Mexico was the peak. Zagallo had replaced Saldanha weeks before and kept Carlos Alberto as captain and right-back. He played every minute of the six matches, including the 1-0 against [England](/nations/england) in which Bobby Moore's tackle on Jairzinho is as famous as the Banks save, the 4-2 against Peru, the 3-1 semi-final against Uruguay and the 4-1 final against [Italy](/nations/italy). In the final he set the tempo from the back, and his goal, the fourth, is widely voted the greatest in World Cup history.

He missed the 1974 World Cup through injury and played his last Brazil match in 1977. After short spells at Botafogo, back at Fluminense, where he won two more Rio titles, and at Flamengo, he joined the New York Cosmos in 1977 at Pelé's invitation, winning the NASL Soccer Bowl in 1977, 1978, 1980 and 1982, often playing as a sweeper beside Beckenbauer.

## How he played

Carlos Alberto was the first modern attacking full-back on a world stage. Nílton Santos had shown the way at Botafogo in the 1950s on the left, but Carlos Alberto did it on the right in a team of attackers, which required the discipline to know when the run was on. In 1970 his movement was tied to Jairzinho's: when the winger came inside, the full-back went outside, and the Italian left-back Giacinto Facchetti, himself the pioneer of the attacking full-back in Europe, could not deal with both.

Defensively he relied on reading the game and a strong, committed tackle rather than pace. He was good in the air for his height and comfortable stepping into midfield when Clodoaldo pushed on. His right-footed striking was the best of any defender of his era; the 1970 goal was struck with the instep, rising and then sinking into the corner from 20 yards without a touch to control.

His weaknesses were recovery speed and the occasional gamble forward that left Brazil's centre-backs, Brito and Piazza, exposed; Zagallo covered this by playing Clodoaldo as a holding midfielder, one of the first in Brazilian football. In the modern game he would be a right-back in any possession side, close in style to Dani Alves or Trent Alexander-Arnold, with the captaincy thrown in.

## Evolution

Carlos Alberto started as a conventional full-back at Fluminense and became an attacking one at Santos, where the club's exhibition tours demanded entertainment. He was a true wing-back by 1970, and by the late 1970s in New York he had moved to sweeper, playing the libero role behind Beckenbauer that the Cosmos used to bring both veterans' passing into the game. The sport around him changed from the man-marking 4-2-4 of 1962 to the [total football](/tactics/total-football) of 1974, which made full-backs into auxiliary midfielders everywhere, and his 1970 goal is often used as the moment when the attacking full-back became an orthodoxy. After retiring in 1982 he coached Flamengo, Corinthians, Botafogo, Fluminense, Nigeria, Oman and Azerbaijan, winning the Rio championship with Botafogo in 1989, the club's first in 21 years.

## Legacy

Carlos Alberto is the captain of the team against which every other team is measured, and the goal he scored is the single most replayed sequence in football history. For full-backs he is the founding father of the attacking role: Cafu, who lifted the trophy in 2002, Roberto Carlos, Dani Alves and Marcelo all learned the trade in a Brazilian culture that treated his 1970 run as the definition of the position. [Paolo Maldini](/players/paolo-maldini) and Philipp Lahm, the great European full-backs, are often compared with him for the combination of defending and leadership.

As captain he set the Brazilian model, calm and authoritative, that Cafu and Thiago Silva inherited. At the Cosmos he helped establish professional football in the United States, and in later life he was a television analyst and a regular presence at Santos and Fluminense. He died of a heart attack in Rio in October 2016 at 72. The FIFA World Cup's "greatest goals" lists still start with him, and the image of a sweating captain raising the gold trophy in the Azteca remains Brazil's definitive football photograph.
