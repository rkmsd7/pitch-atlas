## Overview

Gary Lineker scored 48 goals in 80 games for [England](/nations/england), ten of them at World Cups, and won the Golden Boot in Mexico in 1986, the only Englishman to have done so. He was the First Division's top scorer with three different clubs, scored a hat-trick against [Real Madrid](/clubs/real-madrid) in his first Clásico for [Barcelona](/clubs/barcelona), and led England to within a penalty shoot-out of the 1990 World Cup final. He did all of this without ever being booked, and then became the most recognisable broadcaster in British football. His game was almost entirely about one thing, the finish, and he did that thing better than any English striker before or since.

## Early life and breakthrough

Lineker was born in Leicester in November 1960, into a family of market traders who sold fruit and vegetables in the city, and joined [Leicester City](/clubs/leicester-city) as a schoolboy. He made his debut in 1979 and scored steadily in a side that bounced between divisions, winning promotion in 1980 and 1983. In 1984-85 he scored 24 First Division goals, enough to share the Golden Boot with Kerry Dixon and to attract Howard Kendall's Everton, who paid £800,000.

His single season at Goodison Park was extraordinary: 40 goals in all competitions, 30 in the league, both player of the year awards, and a near-miss double as Everton finished second to [Liverpool](/clubs/liverpool) and lost the FA Cup final to them with Lineker scoring the opening goal. Terry Venables had already decided to take him to Barcelona.

## Peak years

Before he left, the World Cup. England lost to Portugal, drew with Morocco and were close to elimination when Lineker scored a hat-trick in 32 minutes against Poland. He added two against Paraguay and the consolation against [Diego Maradona](/players/diego-maradona)'s Argentina in the quarter-final, and finished as the tournament's top scorer with six.

At Barcelona he scored 20 league goals in his first season and the Clásico hat-trick in January 1987. When Johan Cruyff arrived as coach in 1988 he moved Lineker to the right wing, a decision that baffled the player, but Barcelona won the Cup Winners' Cup in 1989 with Lineker in the side. He returned to England with Venables at [Tottenham](/clubs/tottenham-hotspur), won the Golden Boot again in 1990, and at Italia 90 scored four goals, including the equaliser against West Germany in the semi-final, before England lost on penalties. He won the FA Cup with Spurs in 1991, a second Footballer of the Year award in 1992, and retired from England on 48 goals, one short of [Bobby Charlton](/players/bobby-charlton)'s record, after Graham Taylor substituted him in his final match at Euro 92. He finished in Japan with Nagoya Grampus, struggling with a toe injury.

## How he played

Lineker's game was an exercise in economy. He did not dribble, rarely passed more than ten yards, and could disappear from a match for 80 minutes before scoring twice. What he did was run: diagonal sprints across the near post, timed so that he met the cross or cut-back a fraction before the defender, and darts in behind when the ball was played over the top. Of his 48 England goals, barely a handful came from outside the penalty area.

He finished with both feet and his head, and his finishing was less about power than placement and speed of execution: a side-footed first touch into the corner, a stooping header, a toe-poke under a diving goalkeeper. Bobby Robson built England's attack on his movement, with John Barnes and Chris Waddle delivering from wide and Peter Beardsley dropping to link. The absence of yellow cards was partly temperament and partly tactics: he was never in positions where tackles were required.

His weaknesses were the obvious ones. He contributed nothing to build-up, was easily marked out of games by defenders who stayed goal-side and denied the near-post run, and had no second game to fall back on, as Cruyff's experiment on the wing showed. In today's football he would be a pure penalty-box nine in a side that crossed early and often, closer to a Filippo Inzaghi or a Chicharito than to any modern pressing centre forward, and a coach who valued expected goals would love him.

## Evolution

Lineker's career runs from the end of the First Division's physical, long-ball era to the eve of the Premier League, and from the 4-4-2 of Leicester and Everton to Cruyff's early [positional play](/tactics/positional-play) at Barcelona, which asked him to do things he had never done. His one major adaptation was learning to play with his back to goal at Tottenham, where [Paul Gascoigne](/players/paul-gascoigne) supplied him from midfield and he dropped a few yards to combine. His scoring declined in his final England years as his pace went, and the hamstring and toe problems that followed finished him at 33. The second career, as presenter of the BBC's Match of the Day for 25 years, made him more famous than the first.

## Legacy

Lineker is the proof that the penalty-box striker, the player who does nothing but score, can be the most important player in a national team. His World Cup record, ten goals across two tournaments, stood as England's until Harry Kane passed it, and his 48 international goals were second only to Charlton for 23 years. Alan Shearer, Michael Owen and Kane all learned from his movement across the near post and his refusal to chase the ball outside the box. His partnership with [Paul Gascoigne](/players/paul-gascoigne) at Tottenham and for England in 1990 was the last great creator-and-finisher pairing of the pre-Premier League era, in the line of [Kenny Dalglish](/players/kenny-dalglish) and [Ian Rush](/players/ian-rush). His time at [Barcelona](/clubs/barcelona) made him, with Steve Archibald and Mark Hughes, one of the few British players of the 1980s to succeed abroad, and his fair-play record, zero cautions in more than 600 matches, earned a FIFA award in 1990 that no comparable striker has matched. In 2022 the Leicester-born striker who had once sold fruit on the market became the highest-paid presenter at the BBC, and left it in 2025 as the voice of three decades of English football.
