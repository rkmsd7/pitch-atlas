## Overview

Paolo Maldini played 902 matches for [AC Milan](/clubs/ac-milan) across 25 seasons, won five European Cups and seven Scudetti, and captained Milan for twelve years. He was the first defender to come second in the Ballon d'Or in the modern era (2003, behind Pavel Nedvěd) and was runner-up in the 1994 [World Cup](/tournaments/world-cup) final and the Euro 2000 final with [Italy](/nations/italy). His father Cesare captained Milan to the 1963 European Cup; his son Daniel scored for Milan in Serie A. He is the measure for every defender since, because he made the position look like an art rather than a chore.

## Early life and breakthrough

Maldini was born in Milan while Cesare was still playing, and joined the Milan youth sector at 10. Nils Liedholm gave him a Serie A debut on 20 January 1985 at Udinese, aged 16, as a substitute at right-back. By 1985-86 he was a regular left-back, a right-footed teenager who had been moved to the left and learned to cross and tackle with both feet. When Arrigo Sacchi arrived in 1987 he found a 19-year-old already capable of playing the [high line](/tactics/high-line) and the offside trap that would define the new Milan.

His Italy debut came in March 1988 against Yugoslavia. He was in the Euro 88 semi-final side at 19 and started every game at Italia 90, where Italy lost on penalties to [Argentina](/nations/argentina) in the semi-final.

## Peak years

Maldini's prime stretched from 1988 to 2007, which is absurd. In the first phase he was the left-back of Sacchi's and then Fabio Capello's Milan, which won the European Cup in 1989 and 1990 and the Scudetto in 1988, 1992, 1993, 1994 and 1996, including the unbeaten 1991-92 season. In the 1994 Champions League final against [Barcelona](/clubs/barcelona) he played centre-back, with [Franco Baresi](/players/franco-baresi) and Alessandro Costacurta suspended or injured, and Milan won 4-0. That summer he played every minute of the World Cup, including the final lost on penalties to [Brazil](/nations/brazil).

In 1997 he inherited the Milan captaincy from Baresi and the Italy captaincy from Giuseppe Bergomi. The second phase began in 2001 when Carlo Ancelotti moved him permanently to centre-back alongside Alessandro Nesta. Milan won the 2003 Champions League final against [Juventus](/clubs/juventus) on penalties at Old Trafford, with Maldini named man of the match, the 2004 Scudetto, and the 2007 Champions League against [Liverpool](/clubs/liverpool) in Athens, two years after the Istanbul collapse in which Maldini had scored after 50 seconds. He lifted the 2007 trophy at 38, the oldest captain to do so.

## How he played

Maldini's defending was built on positioning, anticipation and the refusal to dive in. "If I have to make a tackle I have already made a mistake," is the quote attached to him, and his statistics bore it out: in a Milan career of more than 900 games he averaged fewer than one tackle a match. He defended one-on-one by jockeying, showing attackers onto their weaker foot and waiting for the heavy touch. His recovery pace was excellent into his thirties and his aerial ability, at 1.86 m, was strong.

Going forward he was a modern full-back long before the term was common: he overlapped, crossed with his left foot despite being right-footed, and scored occasional goals, including volleys. At centre-back under Ancelotti he organised a zonal back four, stepped up to intercept and passed cleanly from the back. Weaknesses were few. He was not a dribbler, his distribution was functional rather than creative, and in the last two seasons his pace finally went. Today he would be elite in any system: a left-back in [positional play](/tactics/positional-play) who holds width or inverts, or the left-sided centre-back in a [back three](/tactics/back-three) who carries the ball out. He played both roles thirty years early.

## Evolution

Maldini's career spans three distinct tactical eras at one club. Sacchi's Milan (1987-91) made him part of a compact, pressing, zonal 4-4-2 with a high line, which was revolutionary in Italy. Capello's Milan (1991-96) kept the shape but lowered the line and added muscle; Maldini became more conservative, a lock on the left. Ancelotti's Milan (2001-09) used a 4-3-1-2 or diamond, and Maldini, at centre-back, was the calm centre of a defence that conceded 24 goals in the 2003-04 title season.

His body held up partly because he avoided unnecessary contact. He retired at 40 in May 2009, after 647 Serie A games. He later served as Milan's technical director from 2018 to 2023.

## Legacy

Maldini is the standard against which defenders are judged, the reason "Maldini" is a comparison rather than a name. [Alessandro Nesta](/players/alessandro-nesta), [Giorgio Chiellini](/players/giorgio-chiellini) and [Leonardo Bonucci](/players/leonardo-bonucci) grew up in his shadow; [Sergio Ramos](/players/sergio-ramos), [Virgil van Dijk](/players/virgil-van-dijk) and [Alphonso Davies](/players/alphonso-davies) have all cited him across three different positions. Milan retired the number 3 for him in 2009, with the proviso that a son could wear it.

Tactically he was the proof that the zonal, pressing defence Sacchi introduced could be played with elegance rather than brute force, which made the system exportable. The idea that the best defending is preventive, position before tackle, is now standard in coaching from [Pep Guardiola](/managers/pep-guardiola) to [Mikel Arteta](/managers/mikel-arteta), and Maldini is the example used in most of their sessions. [Carlo Ancelotti](/managers/carlo-ancelotti), who coached him for eight years, calls him the most professional footballer he has worked with. [Watch him defend](https://www.youtube.com/results?search_query=Paolo+Maldini+best+defending) and notice how rarely he has to.
