## Overview

Eusébio was the first player from Africa to become one of the best in the world, and for 15 years he was [Benfica](/clubs/benfica). Born in colonial Mozambique, he arrived in Lisbon in 1960, scored 473 goals in 440 games for the club, won the European Cup in 1962 against [Real Madrid](/clubs/real-madrid), reached three more finals and won the Ballon d'Or in 1965. At the 1966 [World Cup](/tournaments/world-cup) he scored nine goals, four of them in the comeback from 3-0 down against North Korea, and his tears after the semi-final defeat to [England](/nations/england) were as famous as the goals. [Portugal](/nations/portugal) treated him as a national monument long before his death in 2014.

## Early life and breakthrough

Eusébio da Silva Ferreira was born on 25 January 1942 in Lourenço Marques, now Maputo, the son of an Angolan railway worker who died when the boy was eight. He played barefoot on wasteland before Sporting's feeder club in the city took him. His 77 goals in 42 games caught the attention of the Brazilian coach Bauer, who mentioned him to Benfica's Hungarian manager Béla Guttmann. Benfica got him to Lisbon in December 1960, with Sporting Lisbon claiming he was theirs, and hid him in the Algarve until the dispute was settled.

He made his debut in May 1961, scored a hat-trick against Pelé's Santos in Paris in June, and was in the team that won the 1962 European Cup. In the final in Amsterdam, Benfica trailed [Ferenc Puskás](/players/ferenc-puskas)'s hat-trick 3-2 at half-time, came back to 3-3 through Mário Coluna, and then Eusébio scored twice to win 5-3. He was 20. Puskás gave him his shirt.

## Peak years

Benfica reached the European Cup final again in 1963, losing to [AC Milan](/clubs/ac-milan) despite an Eusébio goal, in 1965, losing 1-0 to Inter's [catenaccio](/tactics/catenaccio), and in 1968, losing 4-1 to Manchester United at Wembley after Eusébio had been denied a late winner by Alex Stepney's save at 1-1. Guttmann, sacked in 1962 after a pay dispute, is said to have cursed the club never to win another European trophy, and Benfica have lost every European final since.

Domestically he was unstoppable: 11 league titles, seven times the Portuguese top scorer, Europe's Golden Boot in 1968 and 1973. In England in 1966 he scored twice against Brazil in the 3-1 group win that eliminated the holders, four against North Korea after Portugal had gone 3-0 down inside 25 minutes, and the penalty in the 2-1 semi-final defeat to England, where Nobby Stiles followed him everywhere. He finished as top scorer with nine.

## How he played

Eusébio's first weapon was speed, reportedly 11 seconds for 100 metres, and the second was the shot. He received with his back to goal or on the half-turn, used a single touch to set the ball ahead, and either outran the defender or struck from 25 yards before the defender could close. The right foot generated power with almost no backlift, and goalkeepers said the ball swerved late. He was also a confident penalty taker, a decent header, and a dribbler who preferred the straight line and the knock-and-run to tricks.

He played as an inside-right or striker in Benfica's 4-2-4 and as the main forward for Portugal alongside José Torres, with Coluna supplying him from midfield. The honest weaknesses were a lack of subtlety in tight spaces when compared with [Pelé](/players/pele) and a reliance on the right foot. His knees were destroyed by repeated surgeries, and he lost most of his pace by 30. In the modern game he would be a transition striker who runs the channels and shoots early, the kind of forward who thrives in a [counter-attacking](/tactics/counter-attacking) side, and his acceleration would make him a nightmare for a [high line](/tactics/high-line).

## Evolution

The 20-year-old in Amsterdam was raw power. The 24-year-old at the 1966 World Cup was the complete transition forward, scoring from range, from penalties and from headers, and already the leader of a team that had Coluna and Torres around him. By the early 1970s the knees had taken his explosiveness and he scored more from the penalty spot and inside the box; the 1973 Golden Boot, at 31, was a tribute to intelligence as much as speed. He left Benfica in 1975 for the North American Soccer League and Mexico, won the 1976 Soccer Bowl with Toronto Metros-Croatia, and retired in 1978.

Around him the sport moved from the attacking 4-2-4 of the early 1960s to the defensive Italian systems that beat Benfica in 1963 and 1965 and then to the [total football](/tactics/total-football) of the 1970s.

## Legacy

Eusébio opened the door for African players in Europe, though the colonial circumstances of his move were nothing like a modern transfer, and he gave Portugal its first global star. The line from him to Luís Figo and [Cristiano Ronaldo](/players/cristiano-ronaldo) is direct; Ronaldo, another explosive right-footed shooter who became the country's symbol, has called him the greatest Portuguese player, and the two shared the stage at Euro 2004. For [Benfica](/clubs/benfica) he is simply the club: a statue stands outside the Estádio da Luz and his body lay in state there in January 2014 before being moved to the National Pantheon, the first athlete so honoured.

His sportsmanship, applauding Stepney's save in 1968 and consoling opponents, made him the most popular foreign player in England of his era. Coaches have cited him as the prototype of the transition forward, the striker who turns a loose ball into a goal in four seconds. He died in Lisbon on 5 January 2014 at 71 and the government declared three days of national mourning.
