## Overview

Bobby Charlton is the English game's conscience and one of its two or three greatest players. He survived the Munich air crash that killed eight of his Busby Babes team-mates in February 1958, carried [Manchester United](/clubs/manchester-united) through the rebuilding that followed, and won the European Cup with them in 1968, scoring twice in the final. In between he won the [World Cup](/tournaments/world-cup) with [England](/nations/england) in 1966 and the Ballon d'Or in the same year. His 49 goals for England and 249 for United were records until Wayne Rooney passed both, and most were struck from range with a technique that looked effortless and sounded like a gunshot.

## Early life and breakthrough

Robert Charlton was born on 11 October 1937 in Ashington, Northumberland, a mining town, into a footballing family: his uncles included Newcastle's Jackie Milburn, and his elder brother Jack would later play beside him in the 1966 final. Manchester United signed him in 1953. He made his debut in October 1956 at 18, scored twice against Charlton Athletic, and was part of the side that won the 1957 First Division title under Matt Busby.

On 6 February 1958 the team's plane crashed on its third take-off attempt from a slush-covered runway in Munich. Charlton was thrown clear, still strapped in his seat; [Duncan Edwards](/players/duncan-edwards), Tommy Taylor, Roger Byrne and five others died. He returned to play within weeks and was capped for England in April 1958, scoring a volley against Scotland.

## Peak years

Through the early 1960s Charlton was a left winger for United and England, quick enough to beat full-backs and happy to cut in and shoot. Busby moved him into central midfield in 1964, alongside Paddy Crerand and behind [Denis Law](/players/denis-law) and [George Best](/players/george-best), and the move produced the best football of his life. United won the league in 1965 and 1967 and Charlton won the Ballon d'Or in 1966 ahead of [Eusébio](/players/eusebio) and [Franz Beckenbauer](/players/franz-beckenbauer).

For England, Alf Ramsey's wingless 4-4-2 was built around his running from deep. He scored the goal that opened England's 1966 tournament, a long-range drive against Mexico, and both goals in the semi-final against Portugal, a shot from the edge of the box and a thundering finish from a pull-back. In the final Beckenbauer was assigned to mark him, and the two cancelled each other out. At the 1970 World Cup, Ramsey took him off at 2-1 up against West Germany to save his legs; Germany scored twice, and the substitution became one of English football's most argued-over decisions. It was his 106th and final cap.

In 1968 he captained United to the European Cup, ten years after Munich, scoring with a rare header and a near-post finish in the 4-1 win over Benfica at Wembley.

## How he played

Charlton's defining skill was the shot from distance. He would carry the ball from the centre circle, lean back, and hit it with the instep of either foot, keeping it low and letting it swerve; his left was nearly as strong as his right, which was unusual for an English player of the time. Goalkeepers describe shots that moved in the air, and the goals against Mexico and Portugal in 1966 show the mechanics clearly. He also passed beautifully over long distances, switching the play with a single sweep to the far wing, and he ran constantly, box to box, with a long stride that ate ground.

As a winger he had genuine pace and a good cross; as a central midfielder he was the metronome that United and England played through. He was not a tackler and did not pretend to be; Nobby Stiles did that behind him. Weaknesses were a certain predictability, since he preferred to shoot rather than thread passes in the box, and an absence of trickery. He would translate to the modern game as a number 8 who arrives late and shoots, somewhere between Frank Lampard and Steven Gerrard, with a passing range that would fit a [positional play](/tactics/positional-play) side.

## Evolution

He was an inside-forward at 18, a left winger from 1958 to 1964, and a central midfielder from then until his retirement in 1973. Each move took him away from goal and made him more influential, and the 1966 and 1968 versions, deep-lying, two-footed and running the game, are the ones that history remembers.

Around him English football moved from the W-M of the Busby Babes to Ramsey's wingless 4-4-2 and then to the more defensive 4-4-2 of the 1970s. He played a season as player-manager at Preston before returning to Old Trafford as a director, where he advised on the appointment of [Alex Ferguson](/managers/alex-ferguson) in 1986.

## Legacy

Charlton is the template of the English attacking midfielder who scores from range, and the reason the long-distance strike still carries a special status in the [Premier League](/leagues/premier-league). Lampard, Gerrard and Paul Scholes, a United product who grew up in his shadow, are his descendants, as is the idea that a midfielder's first instinct from 25 yards should be to shoot.

For [Manchester United](/clubs/manchester-united) he is the link between the Busby era and the Ferguson one: a director for 39 years and the man whose name is on the South Stand at Old Trafford. For [England](/nations/england) he is the player who won the only World Cup, shared with [Bobby Moore](/players/bobby-moore) and the brother who marked the Germans at the back. He was knighted in 1994 and died on 21 October 2023 at 86 after living with dementia, a condition that has since prompted the sport to examine the heading of heavy leather balls. He is still, for many, the best English footballer there has been.
