## Overview

Michel Platini was the best player in the world for three consecutive years, the only man to win the Ballon d'Or three times in a row before [Lionel Messi](/players/lionel-messi). He led [France](/nations/france) to its first major trophy at Euro 1984 with nine goals in five matches, a record that still stands, and was Serie A's top scorer three seasons running for [Juventus](/clubs/juventus) while playing as a midfielder. He later became UEFA president, which ended badly, but the footballer remains the model for every French number 10 since.

## Early life and breakthrough

Platini was born in Jœuf in Lorraine to a family of Italian origin; his father Aldo was a maths teacher and coach. Metz rejected him as a teenager after a respiratory test, and he joined Nancy, making his debut in 1973. Over seven seasons he scored 127 goals for a modest club, winning the 1978 Coupe de France with the only goal of the final against Nice. He made his France debut in 1976, scoring a free kick against Czechoslovakia, and played at the 1976 Olympics and the 1978 World Cup, where France went out in the group stage despite his goal against [Argentina](/nations/argentina).

In 1979 he moved to Saint-Étienne, then the biggest club in France, and won the 1981 league title. The 1982 World Cup established him globally: France reached the semi-final in Seville against West Germany, drew 3-3 after leading 3-1 in extra time, and lost on penalties in a match remembered for Harald Schumacher's assault on Patrick Battiston. Platini scored a penalty and converted in the shoot-out.

## Peak years

Juventus signed him in 1982 after Serie A reopened to foreigners, and under Giovanni Trapattoni he became the finishing piece of a side containing six of Italy's 1982 World Cup winners. He was Serie A's capocannoniere in 1983, 1984 and 1985 with 16, 20 and 18 goals, won the Scudetto in 1984 and 1986, the Cup Winners' Cup in 1984, and the European Cup in 1985, scoring the penalty that decided a final against [Liverpool](/clubs/liverpool) that was overshadowed by the Heysel disaster.

Euro 1984, on home soil, was his masterpiece. He scored in every game: the winner against Denmark, hat-tricks against [Belgium](/nations/belgium) and Yugoslavia, the 119th-minute winner in the semi-final against [Portugal](/nations/portugal), and the opening goal, a free kick that squirmed through the goalkeeper, in the 2-0 final win over [Spain](/nations/spain). The midfield of Platini, Alain Giresse, Jean Tigana and Luis Fernández, the "carré magique", was the best in Europe.

## How he played

Platini was the prototype of the goalscoring number 10. He started nominally in central midfield but found space between the lines, where he took the ball facing goal with one touch and either released a runner or shot. He had a remarkably early release on his shots, often hitting them with the inside of the foot on the first bounce, and his heading was underrated for someone remembered as a technician; several Euro 1984 goals came from headers.

His free kicks were the signature move: a short, almost lazy run-up and a whipped inside-foot strike that rose over the wall and dipped. He practised with a wall of mannequins at Nancy long before it was standard. Weaknesses: no pace, no tackling, and a tendency to disappear in matches when the supply dried up. Trapattoni covered for him by surrounding him with runners and ball-winners, which is exactly how a modern coach would use him. Today he would be a [box-midfield](/tactics/box-midfield) number 10 or a second striker in a 4-4-2 diamond, expected to score 15 a season from midfield.

## Evolution

At Nancy he was a free-roaming attacker who did everything; at Saint-Étienne he learned to run a team. In Italy, against the tightest man-marking in the world, he had to adapt: fewer touches, quicker decisions, more movement away from his marker before the pass came. His goals per game actually went up. He also became a leader, captaining France from 1979 and effectively picking the tempo of games.

The game shifted around him from the man-to-man defending of the 1970s towards the zonal pressing that [Arrigo Sacchi](/managers/arrigo-sacchi) would introduce at [AC Milan](/clubs/ac-milan) just as Platini retired in 1987, aged 32, worn down by injuries. He moved into coaching France (1988-1992) with mixed results, then administration, organising the 1998 World Cup, becoming UEFA president in 2007 and being banned from football in 2015.

## Legacy

Platini is the bridge between [Raymond Kopa](/players/raymond-kopa) and [Zinedine Zidane](/players/zinedine-zidane): the player who proved a French team could win something. Zidane inherited the number 10, the Juventus move and the role of national talisman, and has spoken of Platini as the first French player he idolised. The Euro 1984 side established the idea of the French "carré magique", a midfield of four technicians, which [Didier Deschamps](/managers/didier-deschamps) and later Zidane as [France coach](/managers/zinedine-zidane-manager) have revisited in different forms.

Tactically he is the reference for the scoring playmaker, the lineage that runs through [Roberto Baggio](/players/roberto-baggio), [Dennis Bergkamp](/players/dennis-bergkamp), [Francesco Totti](/players/francesco-totti) and, in a different shape, [Kevin De Bruyne](/players/kevin-de-bruyne). At Juventus his five seasons are the measure against which every later foreign star, from Zidane to [Cristiano Ronaldo](/players/cristiano-ronaldo), is judged. His administrative career complicates his reputation, but the footage of 1984 does not: [watch it](https://www.youtube.com/results?search_query=Michel+Platini+best+goals+skills).
