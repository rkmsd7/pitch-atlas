## Overview

Rivaldo is the great individualist of Brazil's turn-of-the-century generation. Between 1997 and 2002 he was arguably the most decisive attacking player in Europe, carrying a [Barcelona](/clubs/barcelona) side in transition to two league titles, winning the 1999 Ballon d'Or and then forming the "three Rs" with [Ronaldo](/players/ronaldo-nazario) and [Ronaldinho](/players/ronaldinho) as [Brazil](/nations/brazil) won the 2002 [World Cup](/tournaments/world-cup). He scored in five consecutive matches at that tournament, a feat that still defines his international reputation.

## Early life and breakthrough

Born in Paulista, in the poor north-east state of Pernambuco, Rivaldo grew up in real hardship; his father was killed in a road accident when he was a teenager, and his bow-legged, undernourished frame led scouts to doubt him. He broke through at Santa Cruz, then Mogi Mirim, where he was snapped up by Corinthians and then Palmeiras. At Palmeiras he won the 1994 Brasileirão and became the league's outstanding attacker, which brought a move to Deportivo La Coruña in 1996. One season in Galicia, 21 goals, was enough: Barcelona paid around £16m in the summer of 1997 to replace Ronaldo, who had just left for [Inter](/clubs/inter-milan).

## Peak years

Under Louis van Gaal, Barcelona won back-to-back La Liga titles in 1998 and 1999 with Rivaldo as the goal-scoring heart of the side, usually from the left of a 4-3-3 that he routinely abandoned to drift inside. The relationship with Van Gaal soured over exactly that: Rivaldo wanted to play as a central number 10, the Dutchman wanted a disciplined winger, and the public row in 1999-2000 became one of the defining club stories of the era. It did not stop Rivaldo winning the Ballon d'Or in 1999, the year he also scored twice in the [Copa América](/tournaments/copa-america) final against [Uruguay](/nations/uruguay).

The signature night came on 17 June 2001, the last day of the season, when Barcelona needed to beat Valencia to qualify for the [Champions League](/leagues/champions-league). Rivaldo scored all three in a 3-2 win, the winner an overhead kick from the edge of the box in the 89th minute. Then came 2002: left out of the [AC Milan](/clubs/ac-milan) plans of Luis Fernández's successor at Barcelona, Rivaldo arrived in Korea and Japan with a point to prove and scored in every match from the group stage to the quarter-final, before a strong final against [Germany](/nations/germany) in which his parried shot was tapped in by Ronaldo.

## How he played

Rivaldo's first touch was unusual: he often killed the ball with the outside of his left boot and let it run slightly ahead of him, which looked loose but set up his long striding dribble. He rarely used body feints the way Ronaldinho did; instead he changed pace, dropping a shoulder and accelerating past a defender who had assumed he was slow. Decision-making was aggressive. He shot early from distance, he attempted the audacious when a safe pass was available, and his managers alternately loved and hated him for it.

His signature moves were the long-range strike with little backlift, the dipping free kick, and acrobatic finishes: volleys, overhead kicks, scissor kicks. He was tall for a creative player and surprisingly good in the air. Weaknesses were real too. He did not press, he gave the ball away trying things, he was not a natural team-builder in possession and his right foot was decorative. At his worst, famously at the 2002 World Cup against [Turkey](/nations/turkey), his gamesmanship (the hand-to-face reaction to a ball kicked at his leg) drew scorn.

## Evolution

Rivaldo was a straight number 10 at Palmeiras and Deportivo, became a left-sided inside forward for Van Gaal, and reverted to the central role under Lorenzo Serra Ferrer and Carles Rexach. At Milan under [Carlo Ancelotti](/managers/carlo-ancelotti) he found no room beside [Kaká](/players/kaka), [Andrea Pirlo](/players/andrea-pirlo) and Rui Costa and became a bit-part player who nevertheless collected a Champions League medal in 2003. His late career was a tour: Olympiacos, where he was the best player in Greece for three years and won three titles, AEK, Bunyodkor in Uzbekistan, a return to São Paulo and a final whistle at Mogi Mirim aged 43, playing alongside his son.

## Best plays and moments

- 1994, Brasileirão, Palmeiras v Corinthians: the goals that won his first national title and made him Brazil's hottest property.
- 1996, Olympic bronze-medal match v Portugal: a goal as Brazil salvaged a medal after the semi-final loss to Nigeria.
- 1999, Copa América final v Uruguay: two goals in a 3-0 win in Asunción.
- 1999, La Liga, Barcelona v Real Madrid: a hat-trick inside a 3-2 Clásico victory as he closed in on the Ballon d'Or.
- 2001, La Liga, Barcelona 3-2 Valencia: the hat-trick capped by the 89th-minute overhead kick, voted among the greatest Camp Nou goals ever ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/results?search_query=Rivaldo+hat+trick+Valencia+2001+bicycle+kick)).
- 2002, World Cup group, Brazil 2-1 Turkey: a late penalty winner, and the theatrical collapse that drew a FIFA fine.
- 2002, World Cup quarter-final v England: the equaliser just before half-time, latching on to Ronaldinho's slaloming run.
- 2002, World Cup final v Germany: his dummy opened space for Ronaldo's second goal in the 2-0 win ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/results?search_query=Rivaldo+2002+World+Cup+goals)).
- 2003, Champions League, Milan: a winner's medal after appearances in the group stage and knockouts.
- 2005, Greek league, Olympiacos v Panathinaikos: a derby winner at 33 that showed his left foot had lost nothing.

## Legacy

Rivaldo is the bridge between Brazil's 1994 pragmatists and the 2002 flair team. He is the only one of the three Rs who was never adored unconditionally: not glamorous like Ronaldinho, not a phenomenon like Ronaldo, often accused of being selfish. Yet his record stands with the best of his era, and Barcelona fans of a certain age remember the Rivaldo years, between the departure of [Luis Figo](/players/luis-figo) and the arrival of [Xavi](/players/xavi) and [Andrés Iniesta](/players/andres-iniesta) as senior players, as a time when one man kept the club afloat. He moved into management briefly at Mogi Mirim and remains a vocal, sometimes controversial voice on Brazilian football.
