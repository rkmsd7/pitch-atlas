## Overview

George Best was, for about five years, the most exciting footballer in the world and the most famous young man in Britain. He joined [Manchester United](/clubs/manchester-united) at 15, scored the goal that broke [Benfica](/clubs/benfica) in the 1968 European Cup final, and won the Ballon d'Or that year at 22, the youngest winner until Ronaldo in 1997. He was also the first player to be a celebrity in the modern sense, with boutiques, a nightclub and a lifestyle that the tabloids followed as closely as his dribbling. By 27 he had effectively left top-level football, and the rest of his life was a long, public struggle with alcohol.

## Early life and breakthrough

Best was born on 22 May 1946 in east Belfast, on the Cregagh estate, the son of a shipyard worker. United's Northern Ireland scout Bob Bishop sent Matt Busby a telegram in 1961 that read "I think I've found you a genius". Best went to Manchester at 15, was so homesick that he returned the next day, and was persuaded back. He made his debut against West Bromwich Albion in September 1963, at 17, and scored in his second match, against Burnley.

In March 1966, in a European Cup quarter-final second leg in Lisbon, he scored twice in the first 12 minutes against Benfica, who had never lost at home in the competition, in a 5-1 win. He came home in a sombrero and the press called him El Beatle. He was 19, and from that night he was never just a footballer.

## Peak years

United won the First Division in 1965 and 1967 with a forward line of Best, [Denis Law](/players/denis-law) and [Bobby Charlton](/players/bobby-charlton), the "Holy Trinity" now cast in bronze outside Old Trafford. Best was the league's top scorer with 28 in 1967-68 and scored the second goal in the European Cup final against Benfica at Wembley, rounding the goalkeeper in extra time in a 4-1 win. He was named Footballer of the Year and won the Ballon d'Or.

He was still only 22. The next three seasons were his most productive as a scorer, including six in one FA Cup tie at Northampton in February 1970. But Busby retired in 1969, the team aged around him, and Best began missing training and disappearing to London. He was fined, dropped and forgiven repeatedly until, on 1 January 1974, he played his last game for the club against QPR. He was 27.

## How he played

Best was listed as an outside-right, but he went wherever the ball was. His balance was the thing everyone mentions first: he could be hit from the side at full speed, stumble, and still be in possession, and he rode tackles that would have ended the careers of less agile players in an era when the tackle from behind was legal. He was genuinely two-footed, dribbling with both and scoring with both, and his close control at speed meant defenders could not commit without being beaten. He scored a great many goals by rounding goalkeepers rather than shooting past them.

He also worked. Unlike most wingers of the period he tracked back, tackled hard and won the ball, which Busby valued and which fans sometimes forget. Weaknesses were temperament and durability rather than technique: he could be selfish in possession, holding the ball when a pass was on, and his lifestyle ended his peak early. In today's game he would be a wide forward in a front three with licence to go inside, something like a two-footed Neymar with a work-rate, and the protection from tackles that modern referees provide would have extended his career by years.

## Evolution

The teenager of 1963 was an outright winger who stayed on the right and dribbled. By 1967 he was a wide forward drifting inside to score, and by 1970 he was United's main goalscorer, carrying a declining team. There was no later phase at the top: after United he played in the NASL for Los Angeles, Fort Lauderdale and San Jose, where he scored a famous solo goal in 1981, and for Fulham, Hibernian and Bournemouth. He played 37 times for [Northern Ireland](/nations/northern-ireland) but never at a World Cup.

Around him the game shifted from the free attacking football of the 1960s to the defensive, physical English First Division of the 1970s, which treated flair players with suspicion.

## Legacy

Best is the reason the Manchester United number 7 shirt is a title. [Eric Cantona](/players/eric-cantona), David Beckham and [Cristiano Ronaldo](/players/cristiano-ronaldo) were all consciously placed in his tradition, and Ronaldo, another two-footed winger who came to Old Trafford as a teenager, was the clearest heir. Every gifted, troubled British talent since, from [Paul Gascoigne](/players/paul-gascoigne) to Wayne Rooney, has been explained with reference to him, and the sport's relationship with fame, drink and the tabloid press was written in his life first.

For [Northern Ireland](/nations/northern-ireland) he is the greatest player the country has produced, and Belfast City Airport bears his name. Tactically he belongs to the pre-system era: a player a manager like Busby could simply release, and one that [Alex Ferguson](/managers/alex-ferguson)'s later United, with Giggs and Ronaldo on the wings, was in some ways an attempt to recreate with discipline. He died in London on 25 November 2005 at 59 after a liver transplant failed, and 100,000 people lined the route to his funeral at Stormont. Those who saw him play tend to settle on one word: genius.
