## Overview

Andrea Pirlo reinvented a position. A gifted but marginalised number 10 at [Inter](/clubs/inter-milan), he was moved in front of the back four at [AC Milan](/clubs/ac-milan) by Carlo Ancelotti in 2001 and became the regista around whom two Champions League-winning sides and [Italy](/nations/italy)'s 2006 [World Cup](/tournaments/world-cup) team were built. Written off at 32 and allowed to leave Milan on a free, he then won four straight [Serie A](/leagues/serie-a) titles at [Juventus](/clubs/juventus) and was Serie A's best player three times in a row.

## Early life and breakthrough

Pirlo was born in Flero, near Brescia, to a family that owned a steel business, which gave him an unusual calm about money and pressure. He made his Brescia debut at 16 in 1995, was coached by Mircea Lucescu, and became the star of Italy's under-21s, named player of the tournament as they won the 2000 European Championship.

Inter signed him in 1998 but could not fit him in: Roberto Baggio and later Álvaro Recoba occupied the number 10 role, and Pirlo spent two years on loan at Reggina and Brescia. At Brescia, Carlo Mazzone played him behind Baggio in a withdrawn role, and the idea stuck. In 2001 Milan signed him for about €17 million.

## Peak years

Ancelotti placed Pirlo at the base of a diamond with Gennaro Gattuso to his right as a destroyer, [Clarence Seedorf](/players/clarence-seedorf) to the left, and Rui Costa, then [Kaká](/players/kaka), at the tip. Milan won the 2003 Champions League final against Juventus on penalties, Serie A in 2004, lost the 2005 final to [Liverpool](/clubs/liverpool) from 3-0 up in Istanbul, and won the 2007 rematch in Athens, with Pirlo's free-kick deflected in off Filippo Inzaghi for the opener.

At the 2006 World Cup he was man of the match in three games including the final: he scored against Ghana, set up Fabio Grosso's semi-final winner against [Germany](/nations/germany) with a disguised pass, delivered the corner for Marco Materazzi's equaliser against [France](/nations/france) in the final and scored Italy's first penalty in the shoot-out. He finished in the top ten of the Ballon d'Or in 2006 and 2007.

Milan let him go in 2011. Antonio Conte put him at the base of a 3-5-2 at Juventus and he produced 13 assists in his first season as the club went unbeaten to the title. At Euro 2012 he dismantled England with a Panenka penalty in the quarter-final and set up both goals as Italy beat Germany in the semi-final. He won Serie A every year he was at Juventus and reached the 2015 Champions League final against [Barcelona](/clubs/barcelona), his fifth.

## How he played

Pirlo's game was slow by choice. He received from the centre-backs facing forward, usually with a touch that opened his hips to the whole pitch, and he did not hurry. His range was the thing: 40 to 60-metre diagonals to the wingers, chipped balls over a back line for a striker's run, and the disguised reverse pass, as against Germany in 2006, played without looking. He was effectively two-footed in passing and used the outside of the right boot to bend balls around pressers.

His free-kicks were the "maledetta", the cursed one: struck with three toes and very little spin so that the ball wobbled in flight. He scored 28 of them in Serie A and the national team. He also took penalties, and the Panenka at Euro 2012 was an act of psychological warfare on Joe Hart.

He could not tackle, could not run and could be pressed. Teams that put a man on him, as [Manchester United](/clubs/manchester-united) did with Park Ji-sung in 2010, took him out of games, and he needed a Gattuso or an Arturo Vidal beside him. In his thirties his lack of mobility made him a liability when the press was aggressive, which is why Milan sold him and why Conte surrounded him with runners.

## Evolution

Brescia and Inter saw him as a trequartista. Mazzone's "regista" idea at Brescia and Ancelotti's diamond gave him the role he kept for 14 years. Under Marcello Lippi for Italy he played slightly higher in a 4-4-1-1 with Daniele De Rossi and Gattuso covering, and Lippi described the 2006 team as built "to make Pirlo's job easy".

At Juventus, Conte and then Massimiliano Allegri used him in a three-man midfield with Vidal, Claudio Marchisio and Paul Pogba, which allowed him more time on the ball than Milan's ageing side had. At New York City FC in [MLS](/leagues/mls) he was more of a figurehead, playing as a deep passer alongside Frank Lampard and David Villa before retiring in 2017.

## Legacy

Pirlo made the deep-lying playmaker a glamour position. Before him, the player in front of the back four was a destroyer; after him, every elite side wanted a [Xavi](/players/xavi), a Sergio Busquets, a Toni Kroos or a [Rodri](/players/rodri), and English coaches used his name as shorthand for a type. His autobiography "I Think Therefore I Play" is one of the funniest books written by a footballer.

He coached Juventus in 2020-21, winning the Coppa Italia and the Super Cup but finishing fourth, and has since had short spells at Fatih Karagümrük and Sampdoria. He is also a member of the Italian Football Hall of Fame. With [Gianluigi Buffon](/players/gianluigi-buffon), [Francesco Totti](/players/francesco-totti) and [Alessandro Del Piero](/players/alessandro-del-piero), he belongs to the last golden generation of Italian football.
