## Overview

Roberto Baggio is the most loved Italian footballer of the modern era, which is a strange thing to say about a man remembered for missing a penalty. He was Ballon d'Or and FIFA World Player of the Year in 1993, scored five goals in the knockout rounds of the 1994 [World Cup](/tournaments/world-cup) to drag [Italy](/nations/italy) to the final in Pasadena, and finished his career with 205 Serie A goals, more than any player of his generation, despite a knee that was rebuilt before he turned 19. He was a trequartista in an age of systems, the player every Italian coach wanted and few knew how to use.

## Early life and breakthrough

Baggio was born in Caldogno, near Vicenza, the sixth of eight children. He joined Vicenza at 13 and was sold to [Fiorentina](/clubs/fiorentina) in 1985 for a large fee two days after rupturing the anterior cruciate ligament and meniscus of his right knee in a Serie C1 match. He was told he might never play again and converted to Buddhism during the recovery. He made his Fiorentina debut in September 1986, suffered another knee injury, and did not become a regular until 1987-88.

By 1989-90 he was the best young player in Italy, scoring 17 Serie A goals and taking Fiorentina to the UEFA Cup final, lost to [Juventus](/clubs/juventus). His world-record transfer to Juventus that summer caused riots in Florence. At Italia 90 he scored a slaloming solo goal against Czechoslovakia that remains one of the tournament's finest, but Azeglio Vicini preferred Salvatore Schillaci and Gianluca Vialli, and Baggio watched the semi-final defeat to [Argentina](/nations/argentina) from the bench until late on.

## Peak years

At Juventus under Giovanni Trapattoni he became the complete number 10. He scored 30 goals in 1992-93 as Juventus won the UEFA Cup, including both goals in the first leg of the final against Borussia Dortmund, and won the Ballon d'Or by a distance. He was the first Italian to win FIFA's World Player of the Year.

USA 94 was his tournament even though Italy almost went out in the groups. He scored the 88th-minute equaliser and extra-time winner against [Nigeria](/nations/nigeria) in the round of 16, the winner against [Spain](/nations/spain) in the quarter-final, and both goals in the semi-final against Bulgaria, all while nursing a hamstring injury. In the final against [Brazil](/nations/brazil), 0-0 after 120 minutes, he struck the decisive penalty over the bar. Arrigo Sacchi had never fully trusted him, and Marcello Lippi, arriving at Juventus in 1994, built around Vialli and [Alessandro Del Piero](/players/alessandro-del-piero) instead. Baggio won the 1995 Scudetto and Coppa Italia with Juventus and the 1996 Scudetto with [AC Milan](/clubs/ac-milan), then became a nomad.

## How he played

Baggio was a trequartista, the Italian word for a player who occupies the three-quarter line between midfield and attack. He received facing play, used an exceptionally balanced first touch to turn, and dribbled with the ball close, relying on changes of direction rather than speed. His signature was a drop of the right shoulder followed by a push with the outside of the right foot, which took him past defenders in the box. His finishing was calm: he placed shots, often with a slight curl, and rarely blasted.

He scored 68 Serie A penalties and missed few; the Pasadena kick was taken by an exhausted, injured player. Weaknesses: he did not press, did not track, and had no pace after his knee surgeries, which made him a problem for coaches who wanted compact, zonal teams. Carlo Mazzone at Brescia simply gave him freedom and got 12 goals a season from a 35-year-old. In today's game he would be a second striker in a 3-5-2 or a free ten behind a mobile nine, the role [Paulo Dybala](/players/paulo-dybala) plays, with the understanding that someone else does the running.

## Evolution

Young Baggio at Fiorentina was a winger-cum-striker who dribbled at everything. At Juventus he became a creator who scored, dropping deeper and taking more responsibility for tempo. After 1995 his career became a study in adaptation: at Milan under Fabio Capello and then Sacchi he was marginalised; at Bologna in 1997-98 he scored 22 Serie A goals as a near-complete centre-forward and forced his way into the 1998 World Cup squad, scoring against Chile and converting a penalty in the shoot-out defeat to [France](/nations/france). At Brescia from 2000 to 2004 he was a cult hero, keeping a small club in Serie A for four seasons and scoring his 200th Serie A goal in 2004.

Baggio belonged to none of the systems that came and went around him. He retired in May 2004 at San Siro, where 80,000 people, including Milan's fans, stood to applaud him.

## Best plays and moments

- 1990, World Cup v Czechoslovakia: a 60-metre solo goal through the entire defence in Rome. [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/results?search_query=Roberto+Baggio+goal+Czechoslovakia+1990)
- 1993, UEFA Cup final first leg v Borussia Dortmund: two goals in a 3-1 win in Dortmund, with another in the second leg.
- 1993, Serie A, Juventus v Genoa: a hat-trick in a season of 30 goals in all competitions that won him the Ballon d'Or.
- 1994, World Cup round of 16 v Nigeria: the 88th-minute equaliser and the extra-time penalty winner. [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/results?search_query=Roberto+Baggio+1994+World+Cup+goals)
- 1994, World Cup quarter-final v Spain: the 88th-minute winner, rounding Zubizarreta.
- 1994, World Cup semi-final v Bulgaria: two goals in five minutes, the second a curling finish from the edge of the box.
- 1994, World Cup final v Brazil: the penalty over the bar, the image of his career and the one he carried with most grace.
- 1998, Serie A, Bologna v Inter: a goal in a 22-goal season that earned a World Cup recall at 31.
- 2001, Serie A, Brescia v Juventus: a late equaliser against his old club, running half the pitch on one good knee. [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/results?search_query=Roberto+Baggio+Brescia+goals)
- 2004, Serie A, Brescia v Parma: his 200th Serie A goal, a lob, at 37.

## Legacy

Baggio is the great Italian "what if": what if Sacchi had trusted him, what if the knee had held, what if the penalty had gone in. He is the reference point for every Italian trequartista since: [Alessandro Del Piero](/players/alessandro-del-piero), who inherited his Juventus shirt, [Francesco Totti](/players/francesco-totti), who inherited his Italy number 10, and Andrea Pirlo, who idolised him at Brescia.

Tactically his career is the clearest case study in the conflict between the individual number 10 and the zonal system. The Sacchi-Baggio feud is still cited by coaches debating whether to accommodate a free creator inside a pressing structure, the same question [Luis Enrique](/managers/luis-enrique) and [Pep Guardiola](/managers/pep-guardiola) answer differently today. [Watch the goals](https://www.youtube.com/results?search_query=Roberto+Baggio+best+goals): there are more than any single penalty can erase.
