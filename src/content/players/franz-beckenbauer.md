## Overview

Franz Beckenbauer is the only man to captain and then manage a [World Cup](/tournaments/world-cup) winner, and the player who gave defending an attacking vocabulary. The libero existed before him, usually as a destroyer who sat behind the man-markers in [catenaccio](/tactics/catenaccio). Beckenbauer took the position and played it as a midfielder: carrying the ball out, directing play, starting attacks from the last line. With [Bayern Munich](/clubs/bayern-munich) he won three European Cups in succession and with West Germany the European Championship of 1972 and the World Cup of 1974, beating [Johan Cruyff](/players/johan-cruyff)'s Netherlands in the final in his own city. He won the Ballon d'Or twice as a defender, which has only been matched by Matthias Sammer and Fabio Cannavaro, and neither of them played like him.

## Early life and breakthrough

Beckenbauer was born on 11 September 1945 in Giesing, a working-class district of bombed-out Munich, the son of a postal worker. He supported 1860 Munich and was heading for their youth team until a scrap with an 1860 player in an under-14 match sent him to Bayern instead. He debuted for Bayern in the Regionalliga Süd in June 1965 as a left winger, won promotion that season, and in 1966 helped the newly promoted side win the DFB-Pokal; the Cup Winners' Cup followed in 1967 with a side that also included [Gerd Müller](/players/gerd-muller) and Sepp Maier.

Helmut Schön capped him in September 1965 against Sweden and took him to the 1966 World Cup as a 20-year-old midfielder. He scored four goals, including two against Switzerland on his debut, and was marked out of the final at Wembley by Bobby Charlton, who was in turn marked out by him; England won 4-2. He was voted the tournament's best young player.

## Peak years

Beckenbauer moved into the sweeper position for Bayern in the late 1960s, partly on the example of Inter's Armando Picchi and partly because coach Branko Zebec saw that a player with his passing was wasted chasing in midfield. The transformation was complete by 1970, when he played the World Cup semi-final against [Italy](/nations/italy) with a dislocated shoulder strapped in a sling because Schön had used both substitutes, in a 4-3 defeat after extra time that is still called the Game of the Century.

From 1971 to 1976 he was the best player in Europe. West Germany won Euro 1972 with a side considered their best ever, beating England at Wembley in the quarter-final and the Soviet Union 3-0 in the final; he won the Ballon d'Or that year. Bayern won three successive Bundesliga titles from 1972 to 1974 and then three European Cups: 1974 against Atlético Madrid after a replay, 1975 against Leeds United and 1976 against Saint-Étienne. In 1974 he captained West Germany to the World Cup, coming from a goal down in the final to beat the Netherlands 2-1 in Munich; he had reportedly taken over selection and tactics from Schön mid-tournament after a defeat to East Germany. His second Ballon d'Or came in 1976, after a European Championship final lost on penalties to Czechoslovakia.

## How he played

Beckenbauer's game was built on reading the ball a second early. He was quick without being a sprinter, strong without being a bruiser, and he positioned himself so that the interception arrived before the tackle was needed. With the ball he had two gears: the long, raking diagonal pass to a winger, and the surge upfield through the middle of the pitch, ball at his feet, head up, drawing a midfielder out and then slipping a pass or continuing into the box. His signature image is the outside-of-the-foot pass with the right boot, struck with the posture of a man strolling.

He was a leader by instruction and by example rather than by shouting, and his composure gave Bayern and West Germany a calm that other teams lacked. The weaknesses were the natural price of his role: he was not an aerial dominator, and a libero who steps out leaves space behind him, which required a disciplined stopper like Georg Schwarzenbeck beside him and a goalkeeper like Maier behind. In today's game he would be the ball-playing centre-back every big club wants, or more exactly the centre-back who steps into midfield, the role Pep Guardiola gave John Stones at Manchester City. A [back three](/tactics/back-three) with him in the middle and licence to advance would look very like his Bayern.

## Evolution

He began as a winger, became a box-to-box midfielder by 1966 and a sweeper by 1969, and each move took him deeper and gave him more of the game. The 1972 version was still a midfielder in disguise, often the furthest forward German player in possession. By 1976 he was the organiser behind a defence that had learned to cover his runs. In the NASL with the New York Cosmos from 1977, playing alongside [Pelé](/players/pele) and [Carlos Alberto](/players/carlos-alberto), he returned to midfield and won three Soccer Bowls, and he finished with a Bundesliga title at Hamburg in 1982 at 36.

The game changed around him as well. The libero spread through German and Italian football for two decades, from [Franco Baresi](/players/franco-baresi) to [Lothar Matthäus](/players/lothar-matthaus), whom Beckenbauer himself moved into the role for the 1990 World Cup he won as national coach. Then the zonal back four and the offside trap made the spare man redundant, and the libero disappeared until it returned in another form as the deep-building centre-back.

## Legacy

Beckenbauer is the ancestor of every defender who is praised for his passing. The modern insistence on building from the back, on centre-backs who step into midfield and on a spare man in the first phase, is a return to the idea he introduced, and a coach like [Pep Guardiola](/managers/pep-guardiola) or [Xabi Alonso](/managers/xabi-alonso) would recognise him instantly. [Matthias Sammer](/players/matthias-sammer), [Lothar Matthäus](/players/lothar-matthaus) and Franco Baresi inherited the libero; Sergio Ramos and John Stones inherited its spirit.

As an administrator he ran [Bayern Munich](/clubs/bayern-munich) as president and then honorary president through the club's rise to European superpower, and led the bid and organising committee for the 2006 World Cup in [Germany](/nations/germany), a reputation tarnished late in life by investigations into payments connected to that bid. He died on 7 January 2024 at 78, a few days after Mário Zagallo, the only other man to win the World Cup as player and coach. For Germany he is the Kaiser, the player whose elegance made a nation known for efficiency look like a nation of artists.
