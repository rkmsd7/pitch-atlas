## Overview

Gianni Rivera was the golden boy of Italian football, the Golden Boy quite literally: the nickname "il Golden Boy" followed him from the moment he arrived at [AC Milan](/clubs/ac-milan) as a 16-year-old in 1960 to his retirement in 1979 after 658 games for the club. In between he won two European Cups, three Scudetti, Euro 1968 with [Italy](/nations/italy) and, in 1969, became the first Italian to win the Ballon d'Or. More than the trophies, Rivera was an idea: that an Italian team drilled in [catenaccio](/tactics/catenaccio) could still carry a slender artist at number 10 who did nothing but create, and that this was worth the cost.

## Early life and breakthrough

Rivera was born in Alessandria, Piedmont, in August 1943, the son of a railway mechanic. He debuted for his hometown club in Serie A at 15 in June 1959, and within a year Milan had paid roughly 65 million lire to take him, a huge fee for a teenager. Gipo Viani, Milan's technical director, called him a player who would "make the ball talk". Coach Nereo Rocco, the gruff Triestine who built the Milan of the early 1960s around [catenaccio](/tactics/catenaccio), gave the boy a sweeper behind him, man-markers around him, and freedom in front.

Rivera was a Serie A champion at 18 in 1962 and a European Cup winner at 19, supplying both assists for José Altafini's goals in the 2-1 win over [Benfica](/clubs/benfica) at Wembley in 1963, Italy's first European Cup. He played at the 1962 World Cup in Chile as a teenager, one of the few bright spots of an ugly tournament for Italy.

## Peak years

The second Rocco era, from 1967, produced the finest Rivera. Milan won the Scudetto in 1968 with a defence that conceded 14 goals in 30 games and Rivera pulling the strings behind Pierino Prati and Kurt Hamrin. The following season he scored twice as Milan dismantled Rinus Michels' young [Ajax](/clubs/ajax), with a 22-year-old [Johan Cruyff](/players/johan-cruyff), 4-1 in the 1969 European Cup final in Madrid. The Intercontinental Cup followed against Estudiantes, a brutal tie in Buenos Aires that left Prati and Néstor Combin injured. Rivera's 1969 Ballon d'Or recognised the sum of it.

With Italy, Rivera won Euro 1968 on home soil but his international career became a national argument. Coach Ferruccio Valcareggi believed he could not pair Rivera with Inter's Sandro Mazzola, and at the 1970 [World Cup](/tournaments/world-cup) introduced the "staffetta", the relay: Mazzola for the first half, Rivera for the second. It was absurd and it almost worked. Rivera came on in the semi-final against West Germany and scored the winner in the 4-3 extra-time epic in the Azteca, the Game of the Century, seconds after being blamed for Gerd Müller's equaliser. In the final against [Brazil](/nations/brazil) he was given six minutes. Italy lost 4-1 to [Pelé](/players/pele)'s team. Rivera retired from the national side after 1974 with 60 caps and 14 goals.

## How he played

Rivera played in the hole, the trequartista position Italy would later give to [Roberto Baggio](/players/roberto-baggio) and Francesco Totti. He was not fast, not strong and not a tackler, and his critics, led by the journalist Gianni Brera, called him an "abatino", a little abbot, too fragile for the game. What he had was time. He received on the half-turn, usually on his right foot, and his first touch pulled the ball away from a marker before the marker arrived. His passing range was short and medium rather than long: his best ball was the weighted through-pass into the space between a centre-back and full-back for Prati or Altafini to run onto.

He was also a goalscorer, 164 for Milan, which creators of his type rarely manage. He arrived late in the box, timed his runs onto cut-backs and struck cleanly with either foot. Set pieces and penalties were his too. His weaknesses were real: without protection he could be bullied, and he did little defensive work, which is why Rocco's Milan needed the sweeper Cesare Maldini and later a fierce midfield of Giovanni Lodetti and Roberto Rosato to do the running. In today's game he profiles as a number 10 in a 4-2-3-1 or a free eight in a possession side, a Thomas Müller-style space interpreter with far better feet; the pressing demands would be his problem, as they were for [Riquelme](/players/juan-roman-riquelme) a generation ago.

## Evolution

The teenage Rivera was a pure inside-forward in a 1960s WM-derived shape, operating wide of centre. Rocco's catenaccio centralised him, making him the one free player in a team of markers, and this is the Rivera of 1968-69: the orchestrator who could rest with the ball. In the 1970s, as Milan declined and Italian football moved towards the more mobile "gioco all'italiana", he became a captain who dictated tempo from deeper positions. He won a final Scudetto in 1979, the "star" of Milan's tenth title, at 35, in a side coached by Nils Liedholm that played a more fluid, zonal game than Rocco's. The sport around him had changed: [total football](/tactics/total-football) had been and gone, and the trequartista was becoming a luxury that only the very best teams carried.

## Legacy

Rivera became [AC Milan](/clubs/ac-milan)'s vice-president in 1979, left when Silvio Berlusconi took over in 1986, and spent two decades as a member of the Italian and European parliaments. His football legacy is the Italian number 10 itself. He showed Rocco and his successors that a defensive system could, and should, carry a player who exists only to create, and the line from him runs through [Roberto Baggio](/players/roberto-baggio), Totti, Alessandro Del Piero and the trequartisti who defined Serie A in the 1990s. The staffetta of 1970 remains a cautionary tale for every national coach who hesitates between two artists. Milan's later greats, [Franco Baresi](/players/franco-baresi) and [Paolo Maldini](/players/paolo-maldini), grew up at the club he had made European, and Arrigo Sacchi, who replaced catenaccio with a [high line](/tactics/high-line) and pressing in 1987, admitted the thing he was rebelling against had produced Italy's most beautiful player. He stands with [Giuseppe Meazza](/players/giuseppe-meazza) as a founding figure of the Italian game.
