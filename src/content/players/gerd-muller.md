## Overview

Gerd Müller is the purest goalscorer the game has produced. He scored 365 goals in 427 Bundesliga matches for [Bayern Munich](/clubs/bayern-munich), 68 in 62 internationals for [West Germany](/nations/germany), and the goal that won the 1974 [World Cup](/tournaments/world-cup). His 40 league goals in 1971-72 stood as the Bundesliga record for 49 years until Robert Lewandowski passed it in 2021, and his 85 goals in a calendar year (1972) stood until [Lionel Messi](/players/lionel-messi) beat it in 2012. Nobody who saw him play thought he looked like a footballer; everybody who saw him play knew he would score.

## Early life and breakthrough

Müller was born in Nördlingen, a small Bavarian town, in November 1945, and lost his father at 15. He left school to work as a weaver's apprentice and scored 46 goals in 31 games for TSV 1861 Nördlingen in 1963-64, which brought Bayern Munich, then in the Regionalliga Süd, to his door. Coach Zlatko Čajkovski famously looked at his short legs and thick body and said he did not need a weightlifter. Within a season "kleines dickes Müller" (little fat Müller) had helped Bayern win promotion, and in 1965-66 he scored 33 goals as the club took the DFB-Pokal in its first top-flight season.

Bayern won the Cup Winners' Cup in 1967 and Müller, with [Franz Beckenbauer](/players/franz-beckenbauer) and Sepp Maier, became the spine of the team that turned a Munich second club into Germany's dominant force.

## Peak years

Between 1967 and 1974 Müller was almost absurdly productive. He was Bundesliga top scorer seven times, including 38 goals in 1969-70 and 40 in 1971-72. At the 1970 World Cup in Mexico he scored 10 goals in six games, including hat-tricks against Bulgaria and Peru, the extra-time winner against [England](/nations/england) in the quarter-final and two in the 4-3 "Game of the Century" semi-final loss to [Italy](/nations/italy). He won the Ballon d'Or that year, the first Bayern player to do so.

In 1972 he scored twice in the European Championship final against the USSR and four times in a single international against Switzerland. Then came 1974: three European Cups in a row for Bayern (1974, 1975, 1976), with two goals in the 4-0 replay win over Atlético Madrid in the 1974 final, and the World Cup on home soil, where his swivelling 43rd-minute strike beat [Johan Cruyff](/players/johan-cruyff)'s [Netherlands](/nations/netherlands) 2-1 in the Munich final. He retired from international football that night, aged 28, with 68 goals.

## How he played

Müller was a penalty-box specialist in the most literal sense. He stood 1.76 m but his centre of gravity was extraordinarily low, which let him receive with his back to goal, absorb a defender's weight, spin on a sixpence and shoot before the goalkeeper had set himself. His backlift was almost non-existent; many goals came from half-volleys, scuffs, toe-pokes and knee deflections that other strikers would have miscontrolled. He was genuinely two-footed in the box and deadly in the air for his height because of his timing.

What made him different was anticipation. He did not sprint to the ball; he was already standing where it would bounce. Teammates described him as a player who "scored goals nobody else would even have attempted." Weaknesses were obvious: little pace over distance, minimal involvement in build-up, no inclination to press or track. In a modern pressing system a manager would have to build a role around him the way [Carlo Ancelotti](/managers/carlo-ancelotti) built around Filippo Inzaghi. But his finishing numbers (roughly a goal every 0.7 games across his Bayern career) would make any analyst overlook the rest. He is the archetype of the pure number 9, the opposite of the [false nine](/tactics/false-nine).

## Evolution

Müller's early years were as a bustling, rough-edged forward in a promoted side. Under Čajkovski and then Branko Zebec and Udo Lattek he became more economical, moving less but arriving more precisely, and from 1970 onwards he was essentially a finishing machine fed by Beckenbauer's passes from deep and Uli Hoeness's running. The game around him changed too: the Dutch [total football](/tactics/total-football) of 1974 was built on movement and interchange, and Müller's winning goal in the final was a rebuke to the idea that a static forward could not thrive against it.

His decline was sudden. After 1976 injuries and alcohol caught up with him, he lost his place under Pál Csernai in 1979 and left for Fort Lauderdale in the NASL. His later life was difficult; Bayern brought him back as a youth coach and he was credited with helping develop a number of forwards before Alzheimer's disease took hold. He died in August 2021.

- 1970, World Cup group stage v Bulgaria and Peru: back-to-back hat-tricks in Mexico. [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/results?search_query=Gerd+Muller+1970+World+Cup+goals)

## Legacy

Müller defined the German striker for fifty years. [Uwe Seeler](/players/uwe-seeler) came before him and [Karl-Heinz Rummenigge](/players/karl-heinz-rummenigge) and [Jürgen Klinsmann](/players/jurgen-klinsmann) after, but [Miroslav Klose](/players/miroslav-klose) needed 137 caps to pass his 68 international goals, and Lewandowski needed a 34-game season and a penalty-ridden era to pass his 40. [Harry Kane](/players/harry-kane), scoring at Bayern a half-century later, has spoken of chasing his numbers.

His influence on coaching is the idea of the box specialist: a player judged purely on expected-goals conversion rather than all-round contribution. Managers like [Jürgen Klopp](/managers/jurgen-klopp) and [Pep Guardiola](/managers/pep-guardiola) have both admitted the modern game lacks players who simply live between the posts. Bayern's standing as Germany's superclub rests on the 1960s and 1970s team he scored for, alongside Beckenbauer and Maier. The Bundesliga's Golden Boot trophy and Bayern's training ground street are named after him. [Watch his goals](https://www.youtube.com/results?search_query=Gerd+Muller+best+goals) and the first thing you notice is how little time he needed.
