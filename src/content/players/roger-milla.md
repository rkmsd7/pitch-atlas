## Overview

Roger Milla was a good striker in France for a decade and a legend in three weeks. Retired from international football, playing on the island of Réunion and 38 years old, he was recalled by presidential request for the 1990 [World Cup](/tournaments/world-cup) and scored four goals as a substitute, two against Romania and two against Colombia, taking [Cameroon](/nations/cameroon) to a quarter-final with [England](/nations/england) that they led 2-1 with eight minutes to go. His wiggle at the corner flag became the first globally famous goal celebration. Four years later, at 42, he scored against Russia to become the oldest World Cup goalscorer, a record that still stands.

## Early life and breakthrough

Milla was born in Yaoundé in May 1952, his surname anglicised to Milla by a French clerk who misread Miller. His father worked on the railways and the family moved around Cameroon. He joined Éclair de Douala as a 13-year-old and made his name at Léopard Douala, winning the league in 1972 and 1973, before moving to Tonnerre Yaoundé, where he won the 1975 African Cup Winners' Cup and was voted African Footballer of the Year in 1976.

He made his international debut in 1973 and went to France in 1977, to Valenciennes, where he struggled. A brief spell at [Monaco](/clubs/monaco) led to Bastia, where he scored in the 1981 Coupe de France final win over Saint-Étienne and played in the Corsican side's most successful era. He was already 29.

## Peak years

Milla's French peak came late. At Saint-Étienne in 1984-85, in Ligue 2, he scored 22 goals, and at Montpellier under Louis Nicollin he scored 37 in three seasons, taking the club up to the top flight in 1987 and becoming the fans' idol. He won the Africa Cup of Nations with Cameroon in 1984 and 1988 and played at the 1982 World Cup in Spain, where Cameroon went out unbeaten, Milla having a goal wrongly disallowed against Peru.

He retired from the national side in 1988 and moved to Réunion to play semi-professionally for JS Saint-Pierroise. In 1990 Cameroon's president, Paul Biya, telephoned to ask him to return. The squad resented it, coach Valery Nepomnyashchy was unconvinced, and Milla started on the bench. Cameroon beat the holders [Argentina](/nations/argentina) 1-0 in the opening match, then Milla came on against Romania and scored twice in a 2-1 win. In the second round against Colombia he came on in extra time, scored once with a turn and a low drive, then robbed goalkeeper René Higuita, who had dribbled out to the halfway line, and rolled the ball into an empty net. Against England he came on at half-time, won the penalty for 1-1 and set up Eugène Ekéké for 2-1 before two [Gary Lineker](/players/gary-lineker) penalties sent England through 3-2.

## How he played

Milla was a classic African centre-forward of his generation: strong in the upper body, low to the ground, comfortable receiving with his back to goal and turning on either side. His best quality was the turn itself, a quick shift of the hips followed by a short burst that left the marker half a step behind, exactly what he did to Colombia's Luis Perea before the first goal in Naples. He finished low and early, across the goalkeeper.

He was clever rather than quick in his late thirties, playing off the shoulder and arriving in the box at the right time, and his pressing of Higuita showed a sharp reading of opponents' habits. He had a playful streak that the corner-flag makossa dance made famous but which also showed in his dribbling, with nutmegs and feints inside the area.

His weaknesses by 1990 were stamina and pace over distance, which is why Nepomnyashchy used him as a substitute. He was not an aerial striker and his hold-up play could be slow against physical defenders. Today he would be the impact substitute of a counter-attacking side, a role modern squads value more than the 1990 Cameroon camp did.

## Evolution

Milla's career was unusually long and unusually shaped. In Cameroon in the early 1970s he was a dribbling, goalscoring inside-forward; in France he adapted to a more physical, structured game and became a target man who could also finish. By 1990 he was a pure finisher used for the last half-hour, and by 1994, at 42, a symbolic presence who still scored. He played on in Indonesia until he was 44. The sport around him changed from the amateur African leagues of his youth to professional, televised global football, and Milla was one of the first African players whose fame crossed continents. The 1990 run forced FIFA to give Africa a third World Cup place for 1994 and set up the arrival of [George Weah](/players/george-weah), Samuel Eto'o and the modern African striker.

## Legacy

Milla is the player who put African football on the world map. Before 1990 an African side had never gone beyond the second round; after Cameroon, the continent's allocation grew and the stereotype of naive African defending was joined by a new one of thrilling African attacking. [George Weah](/players/george-weah) called him the inspiration for his generation, Samuel Eto'o wore his legacy at Barcelona and in two further Cameroon quarter-final attempts, and Didier Drogba's Ivory Coast and Senegal's 2002 run with Papa Bouba Diop followed the path he opened.

His corner-flag dance made goal celebrations a global language, a line that runs to Bebeto's cradle in 1994 and every choreographed routine since. He was named one of FIFA's 100 greatest living players by [Pelé](/players/pele) in 2004 and voted the best African player of the century by the CAF. Since retiring he has worked as a roving ambassador for Cameroon and for FIFA, and he remains, with [Eusébio](/players/eusebio) before him and Weah after, one of the three faces of African football's first century on the world stage.
