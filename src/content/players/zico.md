## Overview

Zico is the finest Brazilian footballer never to win a [World Cup](/tournaments/world-cup), and for many the finest of the generation between [Pelé](/players/pele) and [Romário](/players/romario). He scored more than 500 goals in official matches, won the 1981 Copa Libertadores and Intercontinental Cup with [Flamengo](/clubs/flamengo), and was the creative heart of the 1982 [Brazil](/nations/brazil) team that lost to [Italy](/nations/italy) in Barcelona and has been mourned ever since. His free kicks, his vision and his understated leadership made him the idol of Rio's biggest club for two decades.

## Early life and breakthrough

Arthur Antunes Coimbra was born in Quintino, a working-class district of Rio de Janeiro, the youngest of six children of a Portuguese immigrant father. He was skinny to the point that Flamengo's youth coaches put him on a programme of weight training and a special diet to build him up after he joined the club at 14. He made his first-team debut in 1971 and by 1974 was the club's main attraction, scoring freely in the Campeonato Carioca.

He won his first Brazil cap in 1976 and was at the 1978 World Cup in Argentina, where a disallowed late header against Sweden (the referee blew for full time as the ball was in the air) set the tone for a frustrating tournament under Cláudio Coutinho's cautious management.

## Peak years

From 1979 to 1983 Zico was the best player in South America and arguably the world. He led Flamengo to the Brazilian title in 1980, then in 1981 to the Libertadores, scoring twice in the second leg of the final against Cobreloa, and to the Intercontinental Cup in Tokyo, where Flamengo beat Bob Paisley's [Liverpool](/clubs/liverpool) 3-0 and Zico set up all three goals. He was South American Footballer of the Year in 1977, 1981 and 1982.

The 1982 World Cup was Brazil at their most beautiful. Telê Santana's team, with Zico, [Sócrates](/players/socrates), Falcão, Éder and Júnior, scored 15 goals in five games and played with a freedom that still defines the Brazilian ideal. Zico scored four, including the bicycle-kick-assisted winner against Scotland and a free kick against New Zealand. The 3-2 defeat to Italy, with [Paolo Rossi](/players/paolo-rossi)'s hat-trick, is the most famous match Brazil ever lost. Zico moved to Udinese in 1983 for a Brazilian-record fee and scored 19 Serie A goals in his first season, second only to [Michel Platini](/players/michel-platini).

## How he played

Zico was a number 10 who did everything the modern game asks of the position except press. He received between the lines with his body already half-turned, used a short, sharp feint to shift his marker, and either played a disguised through ball or shot. His shooting technique was unusual: a very short backlift and a snapped follow-through that generated surprising power from a slight frame. He was lethal from free kicks, both direct and with his signature low, bending strike under the wall's jump.

He was also a far better finisher than most playmakers, with 48 goals in 71 internationals, and his heading, for a player of 1.72 m, was excellent because of his leap and timing. Weaknesses were physical: he was fragile, suffered serious knee injuries after being fouled repeatedly in Italy and Brazil, and lacked pace. In the man-marking era he was kicked constantly. In today's game, with better protection and specialised roles, he would thrive as a free number 10 behind a striker or as the "8.5" in a [box midfield](/tactics/box-midfield), a role not unlike [Bruno Fernandes](/players/bruno-fernandes) with far more subtlety.

## Evolution

Early Zico was a goalscoring forward playing off a centre-forward; by the late 1970s he had dropped into the hole and became the organiser of Flamengo's attack. Telê Santana's Brazil used him as one of four creators in a midfield with no conventional ball-winner, the apotheosis of the Brazilian jogo bonito and its last great expression before the 1990s pragmatism of [Carlos Alberto Parreira](/managers/carlos-alberto-parreira).

Injuries defined his thirties. A brutal tackle from Bangu's Márcio Nunes in 1985 damaged his knee, and he played the 1986 World Cup half-fit, missing a penalty in the quarter-final against [France](/nations/france). He returned to Flamengo for one more Brazilian title in 1987, retired in 1989, then came back to pioneer Japanese football with Sumitomo Metals, which became Kashima Antlers, laying the foundations of the J.League. He later coached Japan to the 2006 World Cup, managed Fenerbahçe to a Champions League quarter-final in 2008, and worked across Turkey, Greece, Russia, Iraq, Qatar and India.

## Legacy

Zico is the standard for Flamengo, the club with the biggest support in Brazil: every number 10 at the Maracanã, from Petković to Arrascaeta, is measured against him. He is also the symbol of the 1982 team whose defeat convinced a generation of Brazilian coaches, including [Carlos Alberto Parreira](/managers/carlos-alberto-parreira) and later [Dunga](/managers/dunga), that beauty had to be balanced with structure, a shift that produced the 1994 and 2002 World Cups and the arguments about Brazilian identity that still run under [Carlo Ancelotti](/managers/carlo-ancelotti)'s current side.

Internationally his legacy is as a pioneer of Brazilian football abroad: in Italy, where he opened the path for later stars, and in Japan, where Kashima Antlers' trophies and the J.League's founding ethos trace directly to him. Players as different as [Ronaldinho](/players/ronaldinho), [Kaká](/players/kaka) and [Neymar](/players/neymar) have named him as a model for the Brazilian 10, and his free-kick technique was studied by [Juninho Pernambucano](/players/juninho-pernambucano).
