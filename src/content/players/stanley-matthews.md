## Overview

Stanley Matthews played first-division football from 1932 to 1965, winning his last cap for [England](/nations/england) at 42 and playing his final league match five days after his 50th birthday. In between he was the most famous footballer in the world, the "Wizard of the Dribble" whose outside-right play filled grounds across Britain and whose 1953 FA Cup final performance for Blackpool is still known simply as the Matthews Final. He won the first Ballon d'Or in 1956 at the age of 41, and in 1965 became the first footballer to be knighted while still playing. He never won a league title and scored comparatively few goals. He did not need to.

## Early life and breakthrough

Matthews was born in Hanley, Stoke-on-Trent, in February 1915, the son of a barber and featherweight boxer, Jack Matthews, who trained his sons with the discipline of a fighter: cold baths, deep breathing exercises, sprints before school. Stanley signed for Stoke City as an office boy at 15, made his debut at 17 in 1932 and helped the club win the Second Division in 1933. His England debut came in 1934, against Wales, and he scored.

He spent the war playing for Stoke, Blackpool and England while serving in the RAF, and by 1947 the relationship with Stoke's board had soured. Blackpool paid £11,500 for a 32-year-old many thought was finished.

## Peak years

Matthews' best years, by the standards of a career that lasted 33 seasons, came in his thirties and forties at Blackpool. He was the Football Writers' Footballer of the Year in 1948, the first winner of the award, reached FA Cup finals in 1948 and 1951, losing both, and then in 1953, at 38, produced the final that named itself. With Blackpool 3-1 down to Bolton with 22 minutes left, he took hold of the match, crossed for Stan Mortensen's second, tormented Bolton's left-back Ralph Banks, and in the 92nd minute pulled the ball back for Bill Perry to score the winner in a 4-3 victory.

He played at the 1950 and 1954 World Cups, the latter at 39, and in 1956 beat [Alfredo Di Stéfano](/players/alfredo-di-stefano) and [Raymond Kopa](/players/raymond-kopa) to the first Ballon d'Or. His final cap came in 1957, at 42 years and 103 days, still an England record. He returned to Stoke in 1961, filled the ground again, won the Second Division in 1963 at 48 and was Footballer of the Year for a second time, fifteen years after the first.

## How he played

Matthews' game was almost comically narrow and absolutely devastating. He played on the right touchline and waited for the ball to come to his feet. Then he moved towards the full-back at walking pace, the ball a yard ahead, swayed inside as if to cut in, and as the defender shifted his weight he was gone on the outside, accelerating over the ten yards that mattered. Defenders knew exactly what was coming and still could not stop it. His cross, delivered from the byline on the run, was flat and early, aimed at the penalty spot for Mortensen, Tommy Lawton or Nat Lofthouse.

He scored few goals because he had no interest in shooting; he was in the team to create. He did not tackle or track back in any modern sense, and in the English W-M of the time, with the wing-half behind him covering, he did not have to. His conditioning was the hidden strength. He did not drink or smoke, fasted on Mondays, trained alone on the beach at Blackpool, and his acceleration survived into his late forties when every other part of his game would have been expected to decay.

Translated to today he would be a touchline winger in a side that still uses them, a Jarrod Bowen or a prime Arjen Robben's mirror image, and his lack of defensive work would make him a luxury in a pressing team. But the fundamental skill, isolating a full-back one against one and beating him, remains the most valuable thing a wide player can do.

## Evolution

Matthews' career maps the entire first half of English football's modern history. He began in a W-M with wingers hugging touchlines and ended in the age of 4-2-4 and overlapping full-backs, when Alf Ramsey's "Wingless Wonders" were two years from winning the [World Cup](/tournaments/world-cup). His own style never changed; his role did. At Stoke in the 1930s he was a flying winger in a young side; at Blackpool he was the pivot of a team built around his crosses; back at Stoke in the 1960s he was a cult figure whose return added 20,000 to the gate and whose mere presence seemed to lift a stale side out of the Second Division.

He also saw football stop being a working-class pastime played by men on the maximum wage and start becoming a television industry. His own earnings peaked at £20 a week. After retiring he coached Port Vale, worked in Malta and, in apartheid South Africa, ran a team of black players from Soweto known as "Stan's Men", for which he is still remembered there.

## Legacy

Matthews was football's first global celebrity, and the player who made the touchline winger an English institution that lasted until the 1990s. Every orthodox wide man who followed, from Tom Finney and [George Best](/players/george-best) to Steve Coppell, John Barnes and Ryan Giggs, was compared to him, and the trick itself, the feint inside and the burst outside, remains the basic grammar of wing play taught to children in [England](/nations/england). [Garrincha](/players/garrincha), who could do the same thing with a bent leg, is his only serious rival as a pure dribbling winger. His longevity made him a case study in professional conditioning decades before sports science, and his knighthood in 1965 began the process by which football was accepted by the British establishment. He died in Stoke on 23 February 2000, three weeks after his 85th birthday, and his funeral drew 100,000 people to the streets of the city. A statue stands outside Stoke's stadium; another, in Hanley, shows him in three stages of the move he made famous.
