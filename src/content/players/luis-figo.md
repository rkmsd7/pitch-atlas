## Overview

Luís Figo is remembered first for a transfer. In July 2000 he moved from [Barcelona](/clubs/barcelona) to [Real Madrid](/clubs/real-madrid) for a world-record €62 million, the first signing of Florentino Pérez's Galácticos project and the most hated move in Clásico history. He was also, between 1998 and 2002, the best winger in the world, a Ballon d'Or and FIFA World Player winner, and the captain of a [Portugal](/nations/portugal) side that reached the final of Euro 2004 on home soil.

## Early life and breakthrough

Born in Almada, across the river from Lisbon, in November 1972, Figo joined [Sporting CP](/clubs/sporting-cp) at 11 and came through alongside Rui Costa and João Pinto in the youth sides that won the 1989 Under-16 European Championship and the 1991 Under-20 World Cup, the core of the golden generation. He made his Sporting debut at 17, won the Portuguese Cup in 1995, and after a contractual farce in which he signed pre-contracts with both [Juventus](/clubs/juventus) and Parma, was banned from moving to Italy, which is how Barcelona got him for around £2 million in 1995.

## Peak years

Under Bobby Robson and then Louis van Gaal, Figo was the creative heart of Barcelona, winning the Cup Winners' Cup in 1997 with [Ronaldo Nazário](/players/ronaldo-nazario) up front and back-to-back La Liga titles in 1998 and 1999 alongside [Rivaldo](/players/rivaldo). He was captain, a crowd favourite, and the man who sang "white crybabies, salute the champions" from the Camp Nou balcony in 1998.

At Euro 2000 he scored a 25-yard drive against [England](/nations/england) and led Portugal to the semi-finals, then signed for Madrid that summer after Pérez made the release clause a campaign promise. He won the 2000 Ballon d'Or weeks later and the 2001 FIFA World Player award. At Madrid he won La Liga in 2001 and 2003 and the 2002 Champions League, with [Zinedine Zidane](/players/zinedine-zidane), [Raúl](/players/raul) and [Roberto Carlos](/players/roberto-carlos) beside him, and absorbed abuse on every return to Barcelona, most famously the pig's head thrown at him while he took a corner in November 2002.

## How he played

Figo was not quick in a straight line. He beat defenders with a rhythmic step-over, a drop of the shoulder and a sudden acceleration into the gap he had created, and he could go outside to cross or inside to shoot. His crossing from the right was his most reliable product; he was the supply line for Raúl, Fernando Morientes and Ronaldo at Madrid and for Ronaldo, Sonny Anderson and Patrick Kluivert at Barcelona.

He drifted inside to act as a second playmaker alongside Zidane, took corners and free-kicks, and struck a hard, low shot from 20 yards. He was also a surprisingly physical winger, with strong legs and a willingness to track back, and a leader who argued with referees and opponents alike. Weaknesses: he was never a prolific scorer, with 57 goals in 245 Madrid games, and his pace faded by 2004, when the Galácticos' lack of balance was blamed partly on his unwillingness to defend.

## Evolution

At Sporting he was an attacking midfielder in a 4-4-2. Robson and van Gaal made him a right winger in a 4-3-3 and then a free creator, and Vicente del Bosque at Madrid used him on the right of a 4-2-3-1 or 4-4-2 where he had licence to roam. As the Galácticos aged under Carlos Queiroz and Vanderlei Luxemburgo, he lost his place to [David Beckham](/players/david-beckham) on the right and was moved to the left or the bench. He joined [Inter Milan](/clubs/inter-milan) on a free in 2005 at 32 and spent four seasons under Roberto Mancini and [José Mourinho](/managers/jose-mourinho) as a right-sided midfielder and senior professional, winning four straight Scudetti. He retired in 2009 after Inter's title and a Milan derby farewell.

## Legacy

Figo's transfer changed football's economics and the Clásico's emotional temperature; the Galácticos era, [tiki-taka](/tactics/tiki-taka) Barcelona's rise, and the modern transfer market all have roots in that summer. As a player he was the bridge between the old winger, a crosser hugging the touchline, and the modern inside forward, and he mentored a young [Cristiano Ronaldo](/players/cristiano-ronaldo), who inherited his Portugal number seven and his step-over. He has worked as a UEFA advisor and, in 2015, ran against Sepp Blatter for the FIFA presidency before withdrawing. In Madrid he is honoured; in Barcelona he is still whistled on sight.
