## Overview

George Weah is the only African to win the Ballon d'Or, and in 1995 he won it with the FIFA World Player of the Year award and the African Footballer of the Year award in the same season, the only man to hold all three at once. He went from the Liberian league to [Monaco](/clubs/monaco), [Paris Saint-Germain](/clubs/paris-saint-germain) and [AC Milan](/clubs/ac-milan) in under a decade, scoring the most famous end-to-end goal in Serie A history on the way. His country never qualified for a [World Cup](/tournaments/world-cup), which is the great gap in his career, and in 2018 he was sworn in as President of Liberia, the first former footballer to lead a nation.

## Early life and breakthrough

Weah was born in October 1966 in Clara Town, a slum on a reclaimed swamp in Monrovia, and was raised by his grandmother. He played for Young Survivors, Bongrange and Mighty Barrolle before Invincible Eleven, where he scored 24 goals in 1986-87 as a 20-year-old and was spotted by Claude Le Roy, then coaching Cameroon. Le Roy sent him to Tonnerre Yaoundé, [Roger Milla](/players/roger-milla)'s old club, and recommended him to a friend at Monaco, a young coach called Arsène Wenger.

Monaco paid about £12,000 in 1988. Wenger, who would later say Weah was the player who changed his life, taught him to play within a team and to use his pace in the channels rather than only on the ball. Weah scored 14 goals in his first season and was African Footballer of the Year in 1989, won the Coupe de France in 1991 and reached the 1992 Cup Winners' Cup final.

## Peak years

PSG bought him in 1992 and he scored in every round of the 1994-95 Champions League, finishing as the competition's top scorer with seven as PSG reached the semi-finals. His goal against [Bayern Munich](/clubs/bayern-munich) in the group, and two against Barcelona in the quarter-final, made him the most wanted striker in Europe. He had won Ligue 1 in 1994 and two more Coupes de France.

Fabio Capello's Milan paid about £6.5 million in 1995. In his first season Weah scored 11 goals as Milan won the Scudetto, and in September 1996, against Hellas Verona, he collected a corner on the edge of his own box, ran 85 yards through the whole Verona team and finished. He won a second title under Alberto Zaccheroni in 1999 and his five seasons at San Siro, alongside [Paolo Maldini](/players/paolo-maldini) and [Franco Baresi](/players/franco-baresi), produced 58 goals in all competitions. The Ballon d'Or of 1995 was the first and remains the only one awarded to an African, and the first to a non-European since the rules changed that year.

## How he played

Weah was a centre-forward built like a sprinter. He was 1.85 m, powerful through the chest and thighs, and had genuine top-end speed that he could reach while running with the ball. The Verona goal shows his whole game in twelve seconds: a chest control, a shrug past one marker, a change of direction past another, a 60-yard sprint and a cool finish. He could receive with his back to goal and spin either way, go outside the full-back or inside the centre-half, and finish with both feet.

He was two things that strikers rarely combine, a dribbler in open space and a poacher in the box. His headers were powerful, his volleys clean, and his willingness to chase and tackle, a Wenger inheritance, made him the first great pressing centre-forward of the Serie A era. Capello used him as a lone striker with Dejan Savićević and Roberto Baggio behind him.

His weaknesses were consistency and the occasional heavy touch at speed, and his goal totals, 46 in 114 Serie A games, were lower than the quality of the performances because he did so much work outside the box. He would translate to the modern game almost perfectly: a transition striker with the physique of Erling Haaland and the dribbling of Didier Drogba's prime, exactly the number 9 that [gegenpressing](/tactics/gegenpressing) sides want.

## Evolution

Weah was a raw, individual talent at Invincible Eleven and a finished European striker within five years, the acceleration due mostly to Wenger. At PSG under Artur Jorge and Luis Fernández he became a Champions League striker; at Milan, in a league built on [catenaccio](/tactics/catenaccio)'s descendants, he adjusted to fewer chances and the physical attention of Italian defenders. Injury and age slowed him by 1999. Loans to [Chelsea](/clubs/chelsea), where he won the 2000 FA Cup, and [Manchester City](/clubs/manchester-city), then a season at [Marseille](/clubs/marseille) and two in the United Arab Emirates, ended his playing days in 2003. With Liberia he was coach, captain and financier at once, paying for kits and flights, and came within a point of the 2002 World Cup. The sport around him went from the limited foreign quotas of 1988 to the open market of the 2000s, and Weah was the proof that African players belonged at the very top.

## Legacy

Weah is the bridge between [Roger Milla](/players/roger-milla)'s generation and the modern African superstar. Didier Drogba, Samuel Eto'o and later Mohamed Salah and Victor Osimhen all describe him as the player who showed that an African forward could be the best in the world, not only the best in Africa. Wenger's success in developing him at Monaco established the model, later used with Thierry Henry and Patrick Vieira, of a French club as the gateway for African talent; and his pressing, athletic centre-forward play prefigured the number 9s that [Jürgen Klopp](/managers/jurgen-klopp)'s systems demand.

His son Timothy Weah has played for the [United States](/nations/united-states) at two World Cups and for Juventus and Marseille. George Weah himself was named African Player of the Century, one of FIFA's 100 greatest living players, and, after years of humanitarian work with UNICEF, won the Liberian presidency in 2017, serving one term until 2024. No other footballer has gone from a Monrovia slum to the Ballon d'Or to a head of state, and none is likely to.
