## Overview

Lothar Matthäus played 150 times for [Germany](/nations/germany), a record that still stands, appeared at five [World Cups](/tournaments/world-cup) and four European Championships, and captained West Germany to the 1990 World Cup. He won the Ballon d'Or that year and was the first FIFA World Player of the Year in 1991. [Diego Maradona](/players/diego-maradona) called him the best opponent he ever faced. He won seven Bundesliga titles with [Bayern Munich](/clubs/bayern-munich), a Scudetto with [Inter](/clubs/inter-milan) and two UEFA Cups, and played at the top level until he was 39 by reinventing himself as a libero.

## Early life and breakthrough

Matthäus was born in Erlangen, Bavaria, the son of a caretaker at the Puma factory in Herzogenaurach, and trained as an interior decorator before Borussia Mönchengladbach signed him from FC Herzogenaurach in 1979. Jupp Heynckes made him a regular within months. He was capped for West Germany in June 1980, aged 19, and came on in the Euro 80 group match against the Netherlands; West Germany won the tournament. He was an unused squad member at the 1982 World Cup final.

Five seasons at Gladbach, including a 1984 DFB-Pokal final lost on penalties to Bayern in which he missed his kick, established him as the best young midfielder in Germany. Bayern signed him that summer.

## Peak years

At Bayern from 1984 to 1988 he won three Bundesliga titles and reached the 1987 European Cup final, lost to [Porto](/clubs/porto). At the 1986 World Cup he was deployed by [Franz Beckenbauer](/players/franz-beckenbauer) to man-mark Maradona in the final; West Germany lost 3-2 but Matthäus had announced himself. He joined Inter in 1988 under Giovanni Trapattoni, with Andreas Brehme and later [Jürgen Klinsmann](/players/jurgen-klinsmann), and won the 1989 Scudetto with a record 58 points, scoring nine goals from midfield.

Italia 90 was his peak. As captain he scored four goals, including a 60-metre run and finish against Yugoslavia in the opening match and a thunderous strike against Czechoslovakia from the spot and in open play, and ran the final against [Argentina](/nations/argentina) in Rome, won 1-0 with Brehme's penalty. He won the Ballon d'Or and, the following year, led Inter to the UEFA Cup, scoring in the final against Roma. A cruciate injury in 1992 kept him out of Euro 92, and he returned to Bayern that summer.

## How he played

Matthäus was the German ideal of a midfielder: tireless, aggressive, fast over 30 metres and in possession of one of the hardest shots of his era, a right-foot drive with a full follow-through that beat goalkeepers from 25 metres. He tackled front-on, won second balls and then drove forward, carrying the ball through midfield at speed, which is why Maradona respected him: he could do the physical side and the technical side in the same run.

His passing was direct and accurate rather than subtle, and he took penalties with confidence. Weaknesses: he was not a creative passer in tight spaces, his heading was average for a 1.74 m player, and his temperament produced public feuds with Klinsmann, Stefan Effenberg and others. As a libero from 1994, he used his reading of play, his passing range and his long diagonals to orchestrate from behind a [back three](/tactics/back-three). In the modern game the younger Matthäus would be a box-to-box eight in a transition team, the older one a ball-playing centre-back in a 3-4-3, the German prototype for both [Joshua Kimmich](/players/joshua-kimmich) and, at a stretch, Mats Hummels.

## Evolution

Few careers show such a clean two-act structure. From 1979 to 1992 he was an attacking central midfielder who scored at a rate of one in four. After his cruciate injury Beckenbauer, then Giovanni Trapattoni and Ottmar Hitzfeld, used him as a sweeper, and he was German Footballer of the Year in 1999 at 38, after a season in which he reached the Champions League final, lost in injury time to [Manchester United](/clubs/manchester-united), and was substituted 10 minutes before the end with Bayern leading. He played the 1998 World Cup at 37 and Euro 2000 at 39, the latter a humiliation for a declining Germany, before a short spell with the MetroStars in [MLS](/leagues/mls).

The game changed around him from the libero-based West German system to Sacchi's zonal pressing in Italy, which he played in and against, to the 4-4-2 and 3-5-2 of 1990s Germany. He managed Rapid Vienna, Partizan Belgrade, Hungary, Atlético Paranaense, Red Bull Salzburg, Maccabi Netanya and Bulgaria without lasting success and became a television pundit.

## Legacy

Matthäus is the German footballer of the post-Beckenbauer era: the captain who won the last West German World Cup, the first FIFA World Player of the Year, the man with 150 caps. [Michael Ballack](/players/michael-ballack), Bastian Schweinsteiger and [Toni Kroos](/players/toni-kroos) each inherited parts of his role; none matched his longevity. His reinvention as a sweeper extended the German libero tradition from Beckenbauer into the late 1990s, just before the position disappeared under zonal defending and the [high line](/tactics/high-line).

His feuds and his outspokenness as a pundit have made him a divisive figure at home, but the respect from opponents is unequivocal: Maradona's verdict, and the fact that Bayern's modern dynasty under coaches from Hitzfeld to [Vincent Kompany](/managers/vincent-kompany) was built on the title culture of his years, secure his place.
