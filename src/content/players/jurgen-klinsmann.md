## Overview

Jürgen Klinsmann belongs to the small group of forwards who scored at three World Cups (1990, 1994 and 1998), and he did it for a [Germany](/nations/germany) side that moved from Beckenbauer's champions of 1990 to the lean years of the late 1990s. He was never a stylist in the Italian sense, nor a penalty-box poacher in the mould of [Gerd Müller](/players/gerd-muller); he was something closer to a middle-distance runner with a striker's instincts, a player whose value lay in the sheer number of good positions he reached in ninety minutes.

He also became, almost by accident, one of the most liked foreign players English football has ever had. His one full season at [Tottenham Hotspur](/clubs/tottenham-hotspur) in 1994-95 produced 29 goals in all competitions, the Football Writers' award and a celebration that turned a reputation for diving into a running joke he was in on.

## Early life and breakthrough

Klinsmann was born in Göppingen in July 1964, the son of a baker, and grew up in Stuttgart. He played for Stuttgarter Kickers in the second division before moving across the city to VfB Stuttgart in 1984. There his career took off: 79 goals in five seasons, the Bundesliga top-scorer award in 1987-88 with 19, and a run to the 1989 UEFA Cup final, which Stuttgart lost to Diego Maradona's Napoli. His Germany debut came in December 1987 against Brazil, and by Euro 1988 on home soil he was a starter.

## Peak years

His 1989 move to [Inter Milan](/clubs/inter-milan) put him alongside [Lothar Matthäus](/players/lothar-matthaus) and Andreas Brehme in Giovanni Trapattoni's German-flavoured Inter, the side that had just won Serie A. He won the UEFA Cup in 1991 and, more importantly, the 1990 [World Cup](/tournaments/world-cup) in Italy, scoring three times including a cheeky header against Yugoslavia and a crucial goal in the round of 16 against the Netherlands, the game remembered for Frank Rijkaard spitting at Rudi Völler.

Two seasons at [Monaco](/clubs/monaco) under Arsène Wenger followed, then the Tottenham year, then two seasons at [Bayern Munich](/clubs/bayern-munich) that brought the 1996 UEFA Cup (he scored a competition-record 15 goals) and the 1996-97 Bundesliga. In between, he captained Germany to the Euro 1996 title in England, playing the final against the Czech Republic with a calf injury. He finished with 47 international goals, then the third-highest in Germany's history.

## How he played

Klinsmann was a runner first. He timed his movement from the offside line as well as anyone of his generation, using a drop of the shoulder to draw a centre-back inside before going outside, and he loved the diagonal ball played behind a full-back. That meant he was most dangerous in teams with a midfielder who could play early: Matthäus at Inter, Andreas Möller and Thomas Häßler for Germany, and, at Tottenham, the raking passes of Teddy Sheringham and Darren Anderton.

In the air he was excellent for a player of 1.81 m, with a spring that let him attack the front post, and he headed the ball down rather than up. His finishing was more forceful than delicate; he struck through the ball with a low, firm contact and rarely tried to chip or curl. He was also one of the first elite strikers to press defenders systematically rather than as a gesture, something Wenger later credited as a reason for signing him.

Weaknesses were real. His first touch under pressure could be heavy, he was not a link player in tight areas, and his tendency to go to ground earned him the diving tag that followed him from Italy to England. Today he would be an ideal striker for a [counter-attacking](/tactics/counter-attacking) side built around transition and the ball in behind, and his pressing would make him a natural for [gegenpressing](/tactics/gegenpressing) systems that ask the number nine to lead the first wave.

## Evolution

The young Klinsmann at Stuttgart was a raw sprinter who scored on volume of chances. Italy slowed him down and taught him to hold his position and use his body. Trapattoni's Inter was a deep, defensive side and he learned to score on a diet of two or three chances a game. Wenger at Monaco reintroduced him to a more fluid attack, and the Tottenham year, under Ossie Ardiles' five-forward "Famous Five" experiment and then Gerry Francis's more balanced 4-4-2, saw his most complete football: dropping to link, drifting wide, heading, scoring from range.

By Euro 1996 he was a captain and a leader more than a pure scorer, and by the 1998 World Cup, at 33, he was a reference point for a tired Germany side that went out to Croatia in the quarter-finals. His career ended quietly at Sampdoria and on loan back at Spurs, where nine goals in 15 games helped keep them up, including four against Wimbledon.

## Legacy

Klinsmann's playing legacy is twofold. He is the model of the modern "running" striker, the forward who creates chances through movement rather than waiting for them, and his approach to pressing from the front anticipated what [Jürgen Klopp](/managers/jurgen-klopp) and others would demand of their number nines two decades later. English football's affection for him also helped change the perception of foreign imports in the early [Premier League](/leagues/premier-league), a door through which [Eric Cantona](/players/eric-cantona), Dennis Bergkamp and Gianfranco Zola walked.

His second act as a coach was more divisive. As Germany manager he overhauled the national set-up with Joachim Löw before the 2006 home World Cup, a tournament that reconnected the country with its team and laid the ground for the 2014 winners. He later led the [United States](/nations/united-states) to the 2014 World Cup round of 16 and had shorter spells at Bayern Munich, Hertha Berlin and South Korea. Among Germany's great forwards he sits with [Karl-Heinz Rummenigge](/players/karl-heinz-rummenigge), [Uwe Seeler](/players/uwe-seeler) and [Gerd Müller](/players/gerd-muller), the athletic bridge between Müller's box play and the pressing strikers of the modern era.
