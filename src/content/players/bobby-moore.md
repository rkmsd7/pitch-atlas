## Overview

Bobby Moore lifted the Jules Rimet trophy at Wembley on 30 July 1966, wiping his hands on the velvet before shaking the Queen's, and the image is the English game's founding photograph. He captained [England](/nations/england) 90 times, played 108 games for his country, and was the defender [Pelé](/players/pele) named as the best he faced, after their meeting in Guadalajara in 1970 ended in a tackle on [Jairzinho](/players/jairzinho) that is still shown as the definition of the skill. He was not quick and not a great header, and he was the best defender of his era because he understood where the ball was going before anyone else did. He spent his whole prime at [West Ham United](/clubs/west-ham-united) and died at 51.

## Early life and breakthrough

Robert Frederick Chelsea Moore was born on 12 April 1941 in Barking, Essex, and joined West Ham as a schoolboy in 1956. His youth coach was Malcolm Allison, who taught him to think about the game and whose tuberculosis cleared the first-team place that Moore took in September 1958 against Manchester United, at 17. He became a regular under Ron Greenwood, whose academy of coaches at Upton Park treated football as a subject to be studied, and Moore absorbed all of it.

He was capped in May 1962 against Peru, weeks before the World Cup in Chile, played every match there as England went out to Brazil in the quarter-final, and was made captain by Alf Ramsey a year later at 22. He never gave the armband back.

## Peak years

West Ham won the FA Cup in 1964, beating Preston 3-2 with Moore named Footballer of the Year, and the Cup Winners' Cup in 1965, beating 1860 Munich 2-0 at Wembley in a final regarded as one of the best played there. Moore, Geoff Hurst and Martin Peters formed a West Ham core in Ramsey's England, and in 1966 the three combined for the goals that won the World Cup. Moore's long free-kick found Hurst's head for the equaliser against West Germany, and in the final minute of extra time, with Jack Charlton screaming at him to clear, he brought the ball down, looked up and played the pass that Hurst hit for 4-2.

In 1970 he was arrested in Bogotá before the World Cup over a bracelet that disappeared from a hotel shop, released after four days, and then played the tournament of his life. The 1-0 defeat to Brazil in the group stage, with the tackle on Jairzinho and the shirt swap with Pelé at the end, was his performance. He won his 108th and last cap in 1973 against Italy, having passed Bobby Charlton's record.

## How he played

Moore's game was anticipation. He watched the ball and the man, judged the pass before it was made, and stepped in to take it with a sliding or standing tackle timed so exactly that he was usually back on his feet with the ball before the opponent had finished falling. He did not dive in, did not foul, and almost never gave the ball away afterwards.

The passing was the second half of it. He played the first pass of England's attacks, short to the midfielders or long and flat to Hurst, and the 1966 assist is the perfect example: a defender refusing to hoof the ball because he had seen a better option. He organised the line around him with quiet instructions and never looked flustered. His weaknesses were pace, which he hid with positioning, and the aerial game, which he managed by reading flight paths and by having Jack Charlton beside him. In the modern game he would be a ball-playing centre-back on the left of a pair or a [back three](/tactics/back-three), a John Stones or Virgil van Dijk type without the height, and his first-phase passing would have delighted any [positional play](/tactics/positional-play) coach.

## Evolution

He started as a wing-half, the position Ramsey's wingless 4-4-2 abolished, and moved to centre-back as England and West Ham reorganised around a flat back four in the early 1960s. His game barely changed from 1964 to 1973 because it was never built on physical gifts that could fade. He left West Ham in 1974, joined Fulham and reached the 1975 FA Cup final against his old club at 34, and played two NASL summers before retiring in 1978.

Around him the game became more defensive and more tactical, and Moore's style, the sweeper's reading inside a zonal back four, pointed towards the German libero of [Franz Beckenbauer](/players/franz-beckenbauer) and the Italian organiser of [Franco Baresi](/players/franco-baresi), both of whom he resembled in temperament.

## Legacy

Moore is the standard for English defending and English captaincy. Every England captain since, from Kevin Keegan and Bryan Robson to Tony Adams, David Beckham and [Harry Kane](/players/harry-kane), has been handed his example along with the armband, and the statue of him outside Wembley is the first thing supporters see. His style, the reading centre-back who steps in and passes out, was a minority taste in English football for decades, overshadowed by the stopper, and has returned to fashion with the modern emphasis on playing from the back.

[West Ham United](/clubs/west-ham-united) retired his number 6 and named a stand for him. [Pelé](/players/pele), [Franz Beckenbauer](/players/franz-beckenbauer) and [Franco Baresi](/players/franco-baresi) all spoke of him as the defender they admired most. He died of bowel cancer on 24 February 1993 at 51, having been largely ignored by the game after retiring, and the guilt about that shaped how English football has since treated its old champions. The Bobby Moore Fund has raised millions for cancer research.
