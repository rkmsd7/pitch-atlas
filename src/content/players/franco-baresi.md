## Overview

Franco Baresi played 719 games for [AC Milan](/clubs/ac-milan) over twenty seasons, captained the club for fifteen, and was the on-field architect of the offside trap and [high line](/tactics/high-line) that made Arrigo Sacchi's Milan the most influential team of the late 20th century. He won six Scudetti and three European Cups, was runner-up in the 1989 Ballon d'Or, and played the 1994 [World Cup](/tournaments/world-cup) final for [Italy](/nations/italy) less than four weeks after a meniscus operation, producing one of the great defensive performances before missing the first penalty. Milan retired his number 6 the day he stopped playing.

## Early life and breakthrough

Baresi was born in Travagliato, near Brescia, and lost both parents as a teenager. His older brother Giuseppe was already at [Inter](/clubs/inter-milan), which rejected Franco as too small; Milan took him in 1974. He made his Serie A debut in April 1978 at 17 under Nils Liedholm and, in 1978-79, played every game as a libero as Milan won the Scudetto, the club's tenth, the star on the shirt.

Then came the fall. Milan were relegated to Serie B in 1980 for the Totonero betting scandal, promoted, relegated again on merit in 1982, and promoted again in 1983. Baresi stayed through both demotions, became captain at 22 in 1982, and was a non-playing squad member of Italy's 1982 World Cup winners. He made his international debut in December 1982 against Romania.

## Peak years

Silvio Berlusconi bought Milan in 1986 and hired Sacchi in 1987. Sacchi's zonal 4-4-2, with a line held 30 metres from goal and a press triggered by the ball, depended on a sweeper who would abandon sweeping: Baresi stepped up alongside the centre-backs and ran the trap, with [Paolo Maldini](/players/paolo-maldini), Mauro Tassotti and Alessandro Costacurta, and [Frank Rijkaard](/players/frank-rijkaard) screening in front. Milan won the Scudetto in 1988, the European Cup in 1989 (beating [Real Madrid](/clubs/real-madrid) 5-0 in the semi-final second leg and Steaua 4-0 in the final) and again in 1990, plus two Intercontinental Cups. Baresi was second to [Marco van Basten](/players/marco-van-basten) in the 1989 Ballon d'Or.

Under Fabio Capello from 1991 Milan went unbeaten through 1991-92 and won three straight titles; the 1993-94 side conceded 15 goals in 34 games. Baresi missed the 1994 Champions League final through suspension but was Italy's captain at the World Cup that summer. He damaged his meniscus against Norway in the second match, was operated on, and returned for the final against [Brazil](/nations/brazil) in Pasadena, where he blocked, intercepted and marshalled for 120 minutes against [Romário](/players/romario) and Bebeto before missing the opening penalty of the shoot-out. He won a sixth Scudetto in 1996 and retired in 1997.

## How he played

Baresi was the libero who turned a defensive position into an attacking one. Traditional Italian sweepers, from Armando Picchi to Gaetano Scirea, sat behind the line and mopped up. Baresi, following the model of [Franz Beckenbauer](/players/franz-beckenbauer), stepped in front of it: he read the pass before it was played, intercepted it, and either drove forward with the ball or released it early. Under Sacchi he became the conductor of the offside trap, timing the collective step forward to the moment the passer's head went down.

He was short but won headers through timing and spring, tackled cleanly and rarely, and was fast over 10 metres. His passing was accurate and his ball-carrying from the back was far ahead of its time. Weaknesses: his height could be exploited by big strikers on crosses, and the trap, when mistimed, left him exposed. In today's game, with VAR removing the referee's benefit of the doubt, his offside skills would be even more valuable; he would be the central defender in a [back three](/tactics/back-three) or the libero-style centre-back in [positional play](/tactics/positional-play), a smaller, quicker John Stones.

## Evolution

He began as a conventional libero in Liedholm's zonal Milan of the late 1970s, survived two relegations as a young captain, and then, at 27, learned a completely new way to defend under Sacchi. That adaptation is the heart of his greatness: he gave up the libero's safety for a position that was actively exposed, and became better for it. Under Capello the line dropped a little and the system became more cautious, which suited his thirties.

The game changed from [catenaccio](/tactics/catenaccio)'s descendants, the man-marking plus sweeper system Italy used at the 1982 World Cup, to Sacchi's zonal pressing, which Baresi embodied, and then towards the back four without a sweeper that became universal in the 1990s. Baresi's retirement in 1997 effectively ended the libero as a top-level position in Italy.

## Legacy

Baresi is the bridge between the Italian sweeper tradition and the modern ball-playing centre-back. He took the libero, a position designed for caution, and turned it into the engine of the most aggressive defensive system of its era. Every high-line, trap-setting defence since, from [Pep Guardiola](/managers/pep-guardiola)'s Barcelona to [Mikel Arteta](/managers/mikel-arteta)'s Arsenal, uses principles Sacchi and Baresi worked out at Milanello, and [Arrigo Sacchi](/managers/arrigo-sacchi) has said the system was unplayable without him.

For players he is the model of the small, intelligent defender: [Fabio Cannavaro](/players/fabio-cannavaro), 1.76 m and World Cup-winning captain in 2006, is his most direct heir, and [Alessandro Nesta](/players/alessandro-nesta) and Maldini carried his standards into the Ancelotti era. Milan's retired number 6, the first shirt the club retired, is the most concrete tribute. [Watch him](https://www.youtube.com/results?search_query=Franco+Baresi+best+defending) and you see a defender who treated the ball as something to win, not something to clear.
