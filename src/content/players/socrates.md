## Overview

Sócrates was the captain of the 1982 [Brazil](/nations/brazil) team, a doctor of medicine, a chain-smoker, a political activist and the most elegant tall midfielder the game has seen. He is remembered for the backheel, which he used as a first option rather than a flourish, for the headband and beard, and for Corinthians Democracy, the player-run movement at [Corinthians](/clubs/corinthians) that challenged Brazil's military dictatorship. His younger brother Raí won the 1994 [World Cup](/tournaments/world-cup); Sócrates never won it, and said he did not mind, because 1982 had shown the world something better.

## Early life and breakthrough

He was born in Belém do Pará, named after the philosopher by a self-taught father who burned his library when the 1964 coup came. The family moved to Ribeirão Preto in São Paulo state, where Sócrates studied medicine at the local university while playing for Botafogo-SP, often training only twice a week because of his degree. He qualified as a doctor in 1977 and, in 1978, joined Corinthians, the club of São Paulo's working class.

He was already 24 and unusually mature. At Corinthians he scored freely, won the Paulista championship in 1979, and was called up by Brazil in May 1979 against Paraguay. By the time of the 1982 World Cup he was captain.

## Peak years

Between 1982 and 1984 Sócrates was at the centre of two phenomena. The first was the Brazil of Telê Santana: a midfield of Sócrates, [Zico](/players/zico), Falcão and Toninho Cerezo with Éder and Júnior overlapping, which scored 15 goals in five games in Spain. Sócrates opened Brazil's tournament with a long-range strike against the USSR and scored the equaliser against [Italy](/nations/italy) with a shot from a tight angle after a one-two with Zico. The 3-2 defeat to Italy in Barcelona ended the tournament, and the romantic era of Brazilian football with it.

The second was Corinthians Democracy. From 1982 the players, led by Sócrates, Wladimir and Casagrande, voted on every decision at the club, from signings to whether to have pre-match concentração. They wore shirts reading "Democracia" and "Dia 15 Vote" during the 1982 state elections, won the Paulista title in 1982 and 1983 and became a symbol of the Diretas Já campaign for free elections. Sócrates promised to stay in Brazil if the 1984 constitutional amendment passed; it failed, and he left for Fiorentina.

## How he played

Sócrates was an attacking midfielder who operated mostly on the right side of a four, drifting inside to link play. His technique was built around economy. He rarely took more than two touches, used the backheel to play passes that his body shape suggested he could not, and struck the ball with a short, clean, almost vertical swing that produced remarkable power and accuracy from either foot. He scored 22 in 60 for Brazil, a strong return for a creator.

His height made him a threat at set pieces and a target for long balls, which he could cushion to runners. His weaknesses were everything physical: he smoked, he drank, he did not train hard, and he was slow over the first five yards. He also played in a Brazil side without a holding midfielder, which the 1982 defeat exposed. Today a coach like [Pep Guardiola](/managers/pep-guardiola) would use him as a free eight in [positional play](/tactics/positional-play), a player who holds a half-space and receives on the half-turn, provided the defensive structure around him was built by others.

## Evolution

Sócrates began as a centre-forward at Botafogo-SP and moved back into midfield at Corinthians as his reading of the game outgrew his pace. He reached his peak in his late twenties, unusually late because of his studies. His year at Fiorentina in 1984-85 was unhappy: the climate, the training regime and the Italian man-marking did not suit a player who disliked running, and he returned to Brazil with [Flamengo](/clubs/flamengo) and then Santos before retiring in 1989.

The game moved towards athleticism and systems while he was still playing. The 1986 World Cup, where Brazil lost on penalties to [France](/nations/france) and Sócrates missed his kick with his trademark no-run-up technique, was his last; Brazilian football's turn towards pragmatism under [Carlos Alberto Parreira](/managers/carlos-alberto-parreira) was a direct response to the 1982 and 1986 defeats. He spent his later life as a doctor, writer, pundit and briefly a manager, played one game for Garforth Town in England in 2004 at 50, and died of septic shock in São Paulo on 4 December 2011, the day Corinthians won the Brazilian title.

## Legacy

Sócrates is the only footballer whose political legacy rivals his sporting one. Corinthians Democracy is studied as a model of player power and is the reason Corinthians remains "the people's team". His brother Raí carried the family name to the 1994 World Cup and [Paris Saint-Germain](/clubs/paris-saint-germain). As a player he is the reference for the tall, technical midfielder, a lineage that runs through [Rui Costa](/players/rui-costa), [Yaya Touré](/players/yaya-toure) and, in his economy of touch, [Toni Kroos](/players/toni-kroos).

The 1982 Brazil side remains the romantic ideal against which every Brazil team is judged, from [Dunga](/managers/dunga)'s functional 1994 to [Carlo Ancelotti](/managers/carlo-ancelotti)'s current project. Managers as different as [Marcelo Bielsa](/managers/marcelo-bielsa) and [Pep Guardiola](/managers/pep-guardiola) have cited that team as the proof that an attacking collective can be beautiful without being naive, even though it lost. The Ballon d'Or's Sócrates Award, given for humanitarian work and won in 2026 by [Bukayo Saka](/players/bukayo-saka), is named after him, which he would have found funnier than anyone.
