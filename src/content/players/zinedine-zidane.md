## Overview

Zinedine Zidane is the player most often picked when football people are asked who looked most beautiful with a ball at his feet. He was a big, upright attacking midfielder who seemed to play in slow motion, yet nobody could take the ball off him. He won the [World Cup](/tournaments/world-cup) and the [Euro](/tournaments/euro) with [France](/nations/france), the [Champions League](/leagues/champions-league) with [Real Madrid](/clubs/real-madrid), league titles with [Juventus](/clubs/juventus) and Madrid, and the 1998 Ballon d'Or. His career ended with a red card in a World Cup final, which only added to the mythology.

He then became one of the most successful coaches of the modern era, winning three consecutive Champions Leagues with Real Madrid between 2016 and 2018, and in July 2026 he took charge of the French national team. His coaching career has [its own page](/managers/zinedine-zidane-manager).

## Early life and breakthrough

Zidane was born in Marseille in 1972 to Algerian Kabyle parents and grew up in La Castellane, a housing estate in the north of the city, where he learned the game on concrete pitches. He was spotted by Cannes at 14, made his Ligue 1 debut at 17, and celebrated his first goal in 1991 by being given a car by the club president, as promised.

Bordeaux signed him in 1992 and it was there, alongside Bixente Lizarazu and Christophe Dugarry, that he became a full international. His France debut in August 1994 was extraordinary: he came off the bench with France losing 2-0 to the Czech Republic and scored twice in 20 minutes. Bordeaux reached the 1996 UEFA Cup final, and Juventus paid around £3 million for him that summer, one of the great bargains.

## Peak years

Under Marcello Lippi, Zidane was turned from a gifted dribbler into the organising mind of a brutally efficient [Serie A](/leagues/serie-a) side. Juventus won the league in 1997 and 1998 and reached three straight Champions League finals, losing two of them, in 1997 and 1998, and the 1998 defeat to Real Madrid stung.

The summer of 1998 defined him. In the World Cup final at the Stade de France he scored two near-identical headers from corners against [Brazil](/nations/brazil), and France won 3-0. He was voted Ballon d'Or and FIFA World Player of the Year. Two years later at Euro 2000 he was the tournament's best player, scoring a free-kick against [Spain](/nations/spain) and a golden-goal penalty against [Portugal](/nations/portugal) in the semi-final, as France became the first side to hold the World Cup and European title at the same time since West Germany in the 1970s.

In 2001 Real Madrid paid a world-record £46 million to make him the centrepiece of the Galácticos alongside [Luis Figo](/players/luis-figo), [Raúl](/players/raul) and, from 2002, [Ronaldo](/players/ronaldo-nazario). He repaid them in his first season with the volley that won the 2002 Champions League final against [Bayer Leverkusen](/clubs/bayer-leverkusen) at Hampden Park, and he added La Liga in 2003.

## How he played

Zidane's first touch was the foundation of everything. He received passes on the half-turn with either foot, the sole of his boot or his chest, and the ball was instantly under control and facing the goal. He rarely sprinted. Instead he used his size, 1.85 m and strong hips, to shield the ball while he waited for the picture to change, then released it with a pass that often looked a fraction too early but was perfectly timed.

His signature moves were the roulette, a 360-degree spin dragging the ball with both feet, and the Marseille turn, used to escape pressure on the touchline rather than to show off. He was genuinely two-footed, could strike volleys with technique rather than power, and was a superb header for a midfielder, as Brazil found out in 1998.

Tactically he was a classic number 10 who drifted left to find space, linking a deep midfield with the forwards. He was not a prolific scorer, averaging roughly a goal every five games, and he was not a presser. His weakness was temperament: he collected 14 red cards in his career, including a stamp on a Saudi Arabian player in 1998 and the headbutt on Marco Materazzi in the 2006 final. Critics also noted he could drift out of games against physical, well-organised sides when the service dried up.

## Evolution

At Cannes and Bordeaux he was a free-roaming dribbler with poor positional discipline. Lippi at Juventus made him work without the ball, sharpened his decision-making and taught him to play in a team that defended as a block. At Madrid under Vicente del Bosque he was given freedom again, sometimes on the left of a 4-4-2 diamond, sometimes as a pure trequartista behind Raúl and Ronaldo, and his passing range grew as his dribbling was used more sparingly.

For France in 2006, aged 34 and having come out of international retirement, he reinvented himself as a deeper controller, almost a regista, and still produced the tournament's best individual performance against Brazil in the quarter-final. He won the Golden Ball despite the red card in the final.

- 1994, friendly, Czech Republic: two goals off the bench on his France debut.
- 1996, UEFA Cup, AC Milan: Bordeaux's 3-0 comeback win in the quarter-final second leg, which put him on Europe's radar.
- 1997, Serie A, Inter: a chip over the goalkeeper that became the enduring image of his Juventus years. [Juventus goals](https://www.youtube.com/results?search_query=Zidane+Juventus+best+goals)

- 2006, World Cup final, Italy: a Panenka penalty, then the headbutt on Materazzi and the red card that ended his career.

## Legacy

Zidane is the reference point for the elegant number 10 and a touchstone for players like [Kaká](/players/kaka), [Andrés Iniesta](/players/andres-iniesta) and later [Jude Bellingham](/players/jude-bellingham), who have all been compared with him. He was part of France's first World Cup win and the country's golden generation with [Thierry Henry](/players/thierry-henry) and [Patrick Vieira](/players/patrick-vieira).

His second career has been equally remarkable. He coached Real Madrid to three straight Champions Leagues (2016, 2017, 2018) and two La Liga titles across two spells, and in July 2026 he replaced Didier Deschamps as coach of [France](/nations/france) after the World Cup. The full story of his management, his tactical approach and his current France squad is on the [Zinedine Zidane manager page](/managers/zinedine-zidane-manager).
