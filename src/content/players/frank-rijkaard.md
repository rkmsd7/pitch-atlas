## Overview

Frank Rijkaard is the least celebrated of Milan's three Dutchmen and arguably the most important. He was the midfield anchor of Arrigo Sacchi's and Fabio Capello's [AC Milan](/clubs/ac-milan), scored the only goal of the 1990 European Cup final, won Euro 88 with the [Netherlands](/nations/netherlands), and ended his playing career by winning a third European Cup with [Ajax](/clubs/ajax) in 1995, passing to the next generation in the process. As a manager he built the [Barcelona](/clubs/barcelona) of [Ronaldinho](/players/ronaldinho) and a young [Lionel Messi](/players/lionel-messi) that won the 2006 Champions League and prepared the ground for [Pep Guardiola](/managers/pep-guardiola).

## Early life and breakthrough

Rijkaard was born in Amsterdam to a Surinamese father and Dutch mother, and grew up on the same streets as [Ruud Gullit](/players/ruud-gullit), his friend since childhood. He joined the Ajax youth system and made his first-team debut in August 1980 at 17, scoring. Leo Beenhakker and then Aad de Mos used him as a centre-back and a midfielder; he won the Eredivisie in 1982, 1983 and 1985 and the 1987 Cup Winners' Cup under [Johan Cruyff](/players/johan-cruyff), alongside [Marco van Basten](/players/marco-van-basten).

He made his Netherlands debut in 1981 at 19. In 1987 a row with Cruyff ended his Ajax career; he was sold to Sporting Lisbon, who could not register him, loaned to Real Zaragoza, and in 1988 bought by Milan, where Sacchi, who had wanted him for a year, finally had his midfield.

## Peak years

Euro 88 came first. Rijkaard played centre-back next to Ronald Koeman in Rinus Michels' side, dominated the semi-final against West [Germany](/nations/germany) and the final against the USSR, and was named in the team of the tournament. At Milan he was the holding midfielder in the 4-4-2 that won the European Cup in 1989 and 1990; in the second final, against [Benfica](/clubs/benfica) in Vienna, he ran onto Van Basten's flick and finished calmly for the only goal. Milan also won two Intercontinental Cups.

Under Capello he played in the 1991-92 side that went through the season unbeaten and won again in 1993, reaching the Champions League final against [Marseille](/clubs/marseille). His international career ended with the 1994 World Cup quarter-final defeat to [Brazil](/nations/brazil), though its most notorious moment was earlier: in the 1990 World Cup round of 16 against West Germany, a running feud with Rudi Völler ended with both sent off and Rijkaard spitting in Völler's hair, an act he apologised for and that cost him his reputation in Germany for years.

Back at Ajax from 1993 under Louis van Gaal, now a libero behind a team of twenty-somethings, he won two Eredivisie titles and the 1995 Champions League, beating Milan 1-0 in Vienna with his pass setting up Patrick Kluivert's winner. He retired that night.

## How he played

Rijkaard was a defensive midfielder in the modern sense a decade early. He did not simply destroy; he screened the back four, intercepted with his reach, and then built play. His positional sense allowed Milan's famous offside trap and [high line](/tactics/high-line) to function: when Baresi stepped up, Rijkaard covered. His long passing was crisp and he could carry the ball out of pressure with a sweeper's stride, which is why Cruyff and Van Gaal used him at libero.

He scored useful goals, often from late runs or set pieces, and his heading was dominant in both boxes. Weaknesses: he was not quick over short distances, could be baited into temper, and had little creativity in the final third. In today's game he would be a hybrid number 6 and centre-back of the type Guardiola uses, a [Rodri](/players/rodri) who could also play in a [back three](/tactics/back-three). It is no accident that as a coach he prized that profile.

## Evolution

He began as a box-to-box midfielder who scored 50 goals for Ajax, became a disciplined screen in Sacchi's pressing machine, and ended as a libero in Van Gaal's 3-4-3, where his job was to start the build-up. Each change was a step towards the elegant ball-playing defender who is now standard. The game shifted from the man-marking of early 1980s Italy to Sacchi's zonal system, which Rijkaard's intelligence made possible, and then to Van Gaal's positional system at Ajax, an ancestor of [positional play](/tactics/positional-play).

As a coach he took the Netherlands to the Euro 2000 semi-final, relegated Sparta Rotterdam, and then transformed Barcelona between 2003 and 2008, winning two La Liga titles and the 2006 Champions League against [Arsenal](/clubs/arsenal). He later managed Galatasaray and Saudi Arabia.

## Legacy

Rijkaard was the prototype of the modern holding midfielder: a defender's reading, a passer's feet, a leader's temperament. [Edgar Davids](/players/edgar-davids), Clarence Seedorf and the 1995 Ajax generation grew up with him as their senior; [Sergio Busquets](/players/sergio-busquets), who broke through under Guardiola at a club Rijkaard had rebuilt, played a role Rijkaard would have recognised.

As a manager his influence is larger than his trophy count. He restored Barcelona's Cruyffian identity after the chaos of the early 2000s, gave debuts to Messi and Andrés Iniesta, signed Ronaldinho, Samuel Eto'o and Deco, and won the 2006 Champions League with a 4-3-3 built on possession. Guardiola inherited that squad, that style and that structure. The Milan he played for remains the template for every pressing team since, and its coaches [Arrigo Sacchi](/managers/arrigo-sacchi) and [Fabio Capello](/managers/fabio-capello) both said that nothing they built worked without him.
