## Overview

Jairzinho is the only man to score in every round of a [World Cup](/tournaments/world-cup) from the first group game to the final, seven goals in six matches in 1970. He was the right winger of the Brazil side that is still the reference for attacking football, the direct, physical complement to [Pelé](/players/pele), Tostão, Gérson and Rivellino. "Furacão", the Hurricane, replaced [Garrincha](/players/garrincha) on the right wing at Botafogo and for [Brazil](/nations/brazil), a succession that would have crushed most players; Jairzinho responded by winning the World Cup Garrincha could not win a third time.

## Early life and breakthrough

Jair Ventura Filho was born on Christmas Day 1944 in Caxias, in the Duque de Caxias suburb of Rio de Janeiro. He joined Botafogo as a 15-year-old in 1959 and was in a first team that already had Garrincha, Nílton Santos, Didi and Zagallo, the core of Brazil's 1958 and 1962 World Cup winners. He made his debut in 1961, playing initially on the left so that Garrincha kept the right, and won the Rio championship in 1961 and 1962.

He made his Brazil debut in 1964 and went to the 1966 World Cup in England, where he was used on the left in a disorganised squad that went out in the group stage. When Garrincha's knees finally gave out, Jairzinho took the right wing at Botafogo, winning the Rio title in 1967 and 1968 and the Taça Brasil in 1968, and became Brazil's first-choice number 7 under João Saldanha and then Zagallo.

## Peak years

Mexico 1970 was his summit. He scored twice against Czechoslovakia, once against [England](/nations/england) in the 1-0 win in Guadalajara, Pelé's cushioned pass and a thumping finish past Gordon Banks, once against Romania, once against Peru, once in the semi-final against Uruguay and once in the 4-1 win over [Italy](/nations/italy) in the final, a close-range finish from Pelé's headed knockdown. His 100-metre dash to set up the fourth goal, Carlos Alberto's, was a measure of the fitness that Brazil's preparation in altitude had given the squad. Seven goals in six matches, and Gerd Müller's ten were the only higher total.

At Botafogo he was the club's main striker in the early 1970s, and after a brief and unhappy spell at [Marseille](/clubs/marseille) in 1974-75, where he was suspended for hitting a referee, he returned to Brazil with Cruzeiro and won the 1976 Copa Libertadores, scoring in the final against River Plate. He played at the 1974 World Cup as a centre-forward and was recalled for the last time in 1982, aged 37.

## How he played

Jairzinho was a winger who played like a striker. His first instinct was to attack the full-back head-on, with the ball a yard in front and his body already leaning into the sprint. He did not have Garrincha's feints; he had a shoulder drop, acceleration, and a willingness to go past the man on the outside and shoot from the angle. His finish against England, taking the ball on the run in the box and hammering it high past Banks, was characteristic: power through the ball, no placement.

He also drifted inside as a second striker. Zagallo's 1970 system was nominally 4-2-4 but played as a 4-3-3 with Rivellino on the left of midfield, so the right side belonged to Jairzinho and the overlapping [Carlos Alberto](/players/carlos-alberto). Jairzinho's willingness to cut inside opened the touchline for the captain. He was a strong header of the ball for a winger and had a decent left foot for the cut-back.

His weaknesses were in close control at low speed and in temperament; he received several bans in Brazil and France. In the modern game he would be a right-sided forward in a front three, less inverted than today's fashion but similar in shape to the young Bukayo Saka or to Raheem Sterling at his most direct, a winger whose main product is goals.

## Evolution

Jairzinho began on the left as a teenager, moved to the right in 1967, and became a centre-forward in his thirties, playing there for Brazil in 1974 and for Cruzeiro, where he was more of a target man. The game around him changed from the 4-2-4 of 1958 to the 4-3-3 of 1970 and the pressing of the Dutch, who overran Brazil in 1974 at the World Cup; Jairzinho's power held up better than most Brazilians' finesse in that match. His later years were nomadic, through Portuguesa, Noroeste, Fast Clube in Manaus and finally Jorge Wilstermann in Bolivia, where he played until he was 37. He later coached, and ran the youth school that discovered a nine-year-old [Ronaldo Nazário](/players/ronaldo-nazario) and sent him to Cruzeiro.

## Legacy

Jairzinho's record of scoring in every match of a World Cup, from first to last, has never been equalled; Alcides Ghiggia in 1950 did it in four matches of a shorter format, which is why Jairzinho's seven in six is usually cited alone. He is the proof that the 1970 Brazil was not only an artists' collective but a team with pace and power in the right places, and his flank with [Carlos Alberto](/players/carlos-alberto) is still shown to young full-backs and wingers as the ideal of the overlapping relationship.

In Brazilian football he is the link between Garrincha and the direct wingers who followed, and his discovery of Ronaldo gave the game its next great striker. Botafogo consider him their second-greatest player, after Garrincha, and the [Brasileirão](/leagues/brasileirao) club's shirt number 7 carries both men's names. He was named in Pelé's FIFA 100 in 2004 and remains one of the most popular figures of the 1970 side, the one who, as Pelé put it, "could score the goals the rest of us only dreamed about".
