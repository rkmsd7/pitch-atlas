## Overview

Denis Law scored 237 goals in 404 games for [Manchester United](/clubs/manchester-united), won the Ballon d'Or in 1964 and remains the only Scot to have done so. With [Bobby Charlton](/players/bobby-charlton) and [George Best](/players/george-best) he formed the trinity that carried Matt Busby's rebuilt United from the ashes of Munich to the European Cup in 1968, though injury kept him out of the final itself. He was the quickest thinker in the penalty box of his generation, the most feared header of a ball for a man of his size, and the most combustible. Nobby Stiles said opponents feared him more than any other United player because "you never knew what Denis would do next".

## Early life and breakthrough

Law was born in Aberdeen in February 1940, the youngest of seven children of a trawlerman, and grew up in a council flat so poor that he played football in borrowed boots. He was a slight boy with a squint corrected by surgery at 15, which coincided with his transformation into a prodigy. Huddersfield Town's scout spotted him in Aberdeen, and he made his debut for the Yorkshire club in 1956 at 16 under Bill Shankly, who was then the manager there.

Shankly tried to take him to [Liverpool](/clubs/liverpool) when he moved in 1959; Huddersfield refused. Instead [Manchester City](/clubs/manchester-city) paid a British record £55,000 in 1960. He scored 21 league goals in a struggling side, and once scored six in an FA Cup tie at Luton that was abandoned in the mud and expunged from the records. Torino paid £110,000 for him in 1961, another British record, and he spent an unhappy year in Italy, where he found the [catenaccio](/tactics/catenaccio) defences suffocating and the club's control of his life intolerable. When Busby came for him in 1962 for £115,000, a third record fee, he jumped.

## Peak years

Law's first season at United ended with the winning performance in the 1963 FA Cup final, a 3-1 win over Leicester in which he scored the opener. In 1963-64 he scored 46 goals in all competitions, a club record that still stands, and won the Ballon d'Or ahead of Luis Suárez and Amancio. He led United to the league title in 1965 and 1967 and reached the European Cup semi-finals in 1966, where United beat [Benfica](/clubs/benfica) 5-1 in Lisbon.

For [Scotland](/nations/scotland), his finest hour was Wembley in April 1967, when he scored in a 3-2 win over the world champions that let Scots claim, half-seriously, to be the real champions of the world. By 1968 a chronic knee injury meant he watched the European Cup final from a hospital bed. He played on at United until 1973, was released, returned to City and scored the back-heel at Old Trafford in April 1974 that sealed United's relegation, a goal he refused to celebrate and said he never wanted to score. He played at the 1974 World Cup, Scotland's first since 1958, and retired that summer.

## How he played

Law was a striker who played like a boxer. He was quick off the mark, fearless in the air and constantly moving, and the key to his goals was reaction speed: he was first to loose balls, rebounds and half-clearances, and he could turn and shoot in one movement. His heading was extraordinary for a 1.75 m player, a result of a spring and a timing that let him hang above taller markers. His volleying was equally sharp, often with his back to goal and the ball dropping over his shoulder.

He played as the more advanced of United's two inside forwards, with Charlton deeper and Best on the right, which in modern terms was a 4-3-3 with Law as the number nine and Charlton as a creative number eight. Law dropped into pockets to link, but his instinct was always towards goal. He was also aggressive to the point of recklessness, with a temper that produced long suspensions, and his willingness to fight any centre-half earned him both the Stretford End's devotion and the referees' attention.

Weaknesses: he was not a hold-up striker, was easily drawn into confrontations, and from 1965 played on a knee that was never right. Translated to today he would be a mobile, aggressive second striker in the mould of a sharper, nastier Carlos Tevez or Luis Suárez, suited to a counter-attacking side with wingers who deliver early.

## Evolution

Law's career spanned the change from the 2-3-5 and W-M of his Huddersfield youth to the 4-4-2 and [4-3-3](/tactics/counter-attacking) of the late 1960s. He began as a classic inside forward, became in Italy briefly a deep-lying striker starved of service, and found at United a role that let him attack the box full-time. The trinity worked because its parts were different: Charlton's range, Best's dribbling and Law's finishing. After the knee operation of 1968 he lost some of his explosiveness and compensated with cleverness, winning fouls, finding the pocket, and still scoring 14 league goals in 1972-73 at 33.

## Legacy

Law is the first name in the story of the striker as a penalty-box predator in British football. His sleeve-clutching, one-armed celebration was copied by a generation; his aerial game for a small man anticipated [Kenny Dalglish](/players/kenny-dalglish) and later Alan Shearer's ability to win headers on timing rather than height; his 30 goals for [Scotland](/nations/scotland), a record he shares with Dalglish, stood unmatched for half a century. Sir Alex Ferguson, a fellow Aberdonian, called him the greatest Scottish player of all time. At [Manchester United](/clubs/manchester-united) he is one of the three figures on the United Trinity statue outside Old Trafford, and a separate statue of him alone stands in the Stretford End that gave him his nickname. He was diagnosed with dementia in 2021 and died in Manchester on 17 January 2025, aged 84.
