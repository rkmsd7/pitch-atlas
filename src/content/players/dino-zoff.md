## Overview

Dino Zoff is the standard by which Italian goalkeepers are measured, and Italy has produced more great goalkeepers than any nation. He was rejected as a teenager for being too small, did not play Serie A until he was 21, and then stayed at the top until he was 41. In between he set a record of 1,142 minutes without conceding for [Italy](/nations/italy), still unbroken, won six Scudetti with [Juventus](/clubs/juventus) in nine seasons, and captained the Azzurri to the 1982 [World Cup](/tournaments/world-cup) at the age of 40, the oldest winner in the tournament's history. He later managed Juventus, Lazio and Italy, taking the national side to the Euro 2000 final.

## Early life and breakthrough

Zoff was born in Mariano del Friuli, near Gorizia, in February 1942, into a farming family. Inter and Juventus both turned him down at 14 on account of his height; his grandmother prescribed eight eggs a day and he grew 33 centimetres in a few years. He signed for Udinese and made his Serie A debut in September 1961, conceding five against Fiorentina. Four years at Mantova, in Serie B and then A, built his reputation, and in 1967 Napoli bought him in a swap deal.

At Napoli he became an international, making his debut in April 1968 against Bulgaria in the European Championship quarter-final. Two months later he was Italy's goalkeeper for the Euro 1968 triumph in Rome, though Enrico Albertosi kept him out of the 1970 World Cup squad's starting line-up. It was a slow apprenticeship: Zoff was 30 before he became undisputed Italy number one.

## Peak years

Juventus signed him in 1972 and his career changed. Under Čestmír Vycpálek and then Giovanni Trapattoni, Juventus won the Scudetto in 1973, 1975, 1977, 1978, 1981 and 1982, plus the 1977 UEFA Cup against Athletic Bilbao, the club's first European trophy. Zoff played every minute of every league match for most of those seasons; across 11 years he missed almost nothing.

With Italy the clean-sheet run began against Yugoslavia in September 1972 and lasted until Haiti's Emmanuel Sanon scored at the 1974 World Cup, 1,142 minutes later. He was second to [Johan Cruyff](/players/johan-cruyff) in the 1973 Ballon d'Or. Enzo Bearzot made him captain in 1977 and Italy finished fourth at the 1978 World Cup and Euro 1980. In Spain in 1982, after a poor first round, Zoff was immense: the stoppage-time save on the line from Oscar's header in the 3-2 win over [Brazil](/nations/brazil), a clean sheet against Poland in the semi-final and the 3-1 final win over West [Germany](/nations/germany) that saw him lift the trophy in Madrid, 40 years and 133 days old.

## How he played

Zoff was the opposite of the showman goalkeeper. He studied angles, narrowed them early and stood up for as long as possible, so that shots that beat another keeper hit his chest. His positioning on crosses was so good that he rarely needed to punch; he caught, held, and distributed by hand or short pass to a full-back. Among the Italian school he was closest to the English model of calm authority, and furthest from the reflex gymnastics of [Lev Yashin](/players/lev-yashin) or later Gianluigi Buffon.

He was brave in one-on-ones, spreading himself at the striker's feet, and almost impossible to beat from distance because he was never off his line. His reading of the game let Trapattoni's Juventus and Bearzot's Italy defend with a sweeper, Gaetano Scirea, who could step into midfield, because Zoff would cover the space behind.

His weaknesses, such as they were, lay in distribution range, which the era did not demand, and a reluctance to come off his line to sweep, which a [high line](/tactics/high-line) today would require. In the modern game he would be a top goalkeeper in a mid-block side, and his temperament would translate anywhere.

## Evolution

Zoff's career spanned [catenaccio](/tactics/catenaccio) at its height and the first Italian moves away from it. At Mantova and Napoli he worked behind man-markers and a libero; at Juventus under Trapattoni the system became zona mista, a hybrid of man and zone marking, and Zoff's calm organisation was its foundation. Bearzot's Italy of 1982 pressed higher and attacked more than any Azzurri side before it, and Zoff, nearly 40, adjusted his starting position accordingly.

He was unusual in getting better with age. The 1982 tournament was statistically his finest, two goals conceded in the knockout rounds, and he retired in 1983 after a Coppa Italia win and a European Cup final defeat to Hamburg, having played 570 Serie A games. His longevity made the 35-year-old goalkeeper normal in Italy; Buffon's career to 45 followed the path he had cut.

## Legacy

Every Italian goalkeeper since has been compared with Zoff. Walter Zenga, Gianluca Pagliuca, Buffon and Gianluigi Donnarumma all grew up on his example of positional discipline, and Buffon in particular called him the model for his own long career. He showed that a goalkeeper could be a captain not through noise but through presence, a lesson [Paolo Maldini](/players/paolo-maldini) and [Franco Baresi](/players/franco-baresi) later applied in outfield positions.

As a coach he won the UEFA Cup with Juventus in 1990, took Lazio into Serie A's elite, and came within a golden goal of winning Euro 2000 with Italy before resigning after criticism from Silvio Berlusconi. He remains, with [Gianni Rivera](/players/gianni-rivera) and [Giuseppe Meazza](/players/giuseppe-meazza), one of the figures who define the [Serie A](/leagues/serie-a) century, and the 1982 team with Rossi, Scirea, Tardelli and Bruno Conti is still the benchmark against which Italian World Cup sides, including the 2006 winners, are judged. He was named the third-greatest goalkeeper of the 20th century by the IFFHS, behind Yashin and Gordon Banks.
