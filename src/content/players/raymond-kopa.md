## Overview

Raymond Kopa was the first French footballer to be recognised as the best in the world. He won the Ballon d'Or in 1958, the year he finished third at the World Cup with [France](/nations/france) and won the second of three straight European Cups with [Real Madrid](/clubs/real-madrid), where he played alongside [Alfredo Di Stéfano](/players/alfredo-di-stefano), [Ferenc Puskás](/players/ferenc-puskas) and Francisco Gento. Before and after Madrid he was the heart of the [Reims](/clubs/reims) team that reached the first European Cup final, and his partnership with [Just Fontaine](/players/just-fontaine) produced the most prolific tournament a striker has ever had. Small, quick and brave, he was the model for the French creative tradition that runs to [Michel Platini](/players/michel-platini) and Zinedine Zidane.

## Early life and breakthrough

Kopa was born Raymond Kopaszewski in Nœux-les-Mines, in the Pas-de-Calais coalfield, to Polish immigrant parents in October 1931. He went down the mine at 14, and lost part of a finger in an accident underground, an experience he said shaped his determination to escape. Football was the way out: Angers signed him from the local amateur club in 1949, and his dribbling in the second division caught the eye of Reims, who paid a record fee for a French player in 1951.

Under Albert Batteux, Reims played the most sophisticated football in France, a short-passing, movement-based game that became known as "champagne football". Kopa was its engine. He won the title in 1953 and 1955, and in 1956 took Reims to the inaugural European Cup final in Paris, where they led Real Madrid 2-0 and 3-2 before losing 4-3. Santiago Bernabéu had already agreed to sign him. He was 24.

## Peak years

At Madrid, Kopa was moved from the centre to the right wing to accommodate Di Stéfano, a change he resented and later said cost him goals. It did not cost him trophies. He won the European Cup in 1957 against Fiorentina, 1958 against [AC Milan](/clubs/ac-milan) and 1959 against his old club Reims, plus two Spanish titles, and in 1958 he was voted European Footballer of the Year ahead of Helmut Rahn and Fontaine.

That summer in Sweden he played his natural role again. France's coach Paul Nicolas and trainer Batteux let him roam behind Fontaine, and the pair carved up Paraguay, Northern Ireland and West Germany. Kopa scored three himself and made most of Fontaine's 13. Only [Pelé](/players/pele) and [Garrincha](/players/garrincha)'s Brazil, in the semi-final, were too good.

He returned to Reims in 1959, won two more titles in 1960 and 1962, and played on until 1967, by which time he had 45 caps and 18 goals for France.

## How he played

Kopa was 1.68 m and built low to the ground, which gave him a centre of gravity defenders could not shift. He carried the ball with very short touches, almost tapping it, and beat players with changes of pace rather than tricks, a feint of the shoulder and then a burst into the space he had already seen. Journalists called him "the Napoleon of football" for his size and his command of the field.

He was a creator first. At Reims he played as the deep-lying inside forward in a W-M that had already begun to look like a 4-2-4, dropping to receive, then driving at the defensive line and sliding the pass at the moment the striker's run broke the offside line. His timing in that final pass, rather than its weight or vision, was what separated him. He scored regularly too, usually by continuing his dribble into the box.

At Madrid, out wide, he adapted by taking the full-back on the outside and crossing early, and the discipline of playing for Di Stéfano's team made him a better defender of the ball. His weaknesses were physical: he could be muscled off the ball by the big half-backs of the era, and he was not a goalscorer on the scale of the men he served. Today he would be a right-sided number 10 or an inverted winger in a 4-2-3-1, a profile close to a smaller, more direct version of Bernardo Silva.

## Evolution

Kopa's career covers the birth of European club football and the decline of the old inside-forward role. He started as a classic W-M inside right, became a free-roaming playmaker under Batteux, learned the right wing at the most demanding club in the world, and finished at Reims as a deeper, more measured organiser as his legs went. Around him the sport professionalised: the European Cup he helped launch became the continent's defining competition, and the sweeper systems of the 1960s started to close the gaps between the lines he had exploited.

He was also a pioneer off the pitch. In 1963 he publicly argued that French players bound by lifetime contracts were "slaves", a campaign that led to the introduction of fixed-term contracts in France in 1969.

## Legacy

Kopa was the proof that a French player could lead the best club in the world, and the first of the line that runs through [Michel Platini](/players/michel-platini), Zinedine Zidane and Kylian Mbappé at [Real Madrid](/clubs/real-madrid). France's "champagne football" at [Reims](/clubs/reims), the movement-based passing game Albert Batteux built around him, was a forerunner of the fluid systems that [total football](/tactics/total-football) later formalised in the Netherlands. His campaign for players' contractual freedom changed French labour law for footballers decades before the Bosman ruling. He was made a Chevalier of the Légion d'honneur in 1970, the first footballer so honoured, and when he died in Angers on 3 March 2017, Real Madrid's players wore black armbands for a man who had played only three seasons there, which says something about how much those three seasons mattered.
