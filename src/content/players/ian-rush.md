## Overview

Ian Rush scored 346 goals for [Liverpool](/clubs/liverpool) across two spells, a club record that neither Roger Hunt before him nor Mohamed Salah after has reached, and 39 of them came in FA Cup ties, which was for years the competition record too. He was the finishing end of the partnership with [Kenny Dalglish](/players/kenny-dalglish) that made Liverpool the dominant team in Europe in the first half of the 1980s. He also, as Bob Paisley never tired of pointing out, was the team's first defender, a tall striker who pressed centre-backs so relentlessly that Liverpool's possession statistics started with him. For [Wales](/nations/wales) he scored 28 goals in 73 caps and never reached a major tournament.

## Early life and breakthrough

Rush was born in St Asaph, Flintshire, in October 1961, one of ten children of a steelworker, and grew up in Flint, close enough to Merseyside to support Everton. Chester City took him as an apprentice, gave him a debut at 17 and sold him to Liverpool in April 1980 for £300,000, a record for a teenager. He did not score in his first nine games, was unhappy in the reserves, and asked for a transfer. Paisley told him to be more selfish. In the 1981-82 season he was.

He scored 30 goals that year as Liverpool won the title and League Cup, and 31 the next. In 1983-84 he scored 47 in all competitions, including five in one match against Luton, won the European Golden Boot and both English player of the year awards, and scored in the League Cup, First Division and European Cup as Liverpool won a treble.

## Peak years

From 1981 to 1987 Rush was the most reliable goalscorer in Europe. His 1984-85 season was reduced by injury, but 1985-86 brought the double under player-manager Dalglish, with Rush scoring twice in the FA Cup final against Everton, and 1986-87 brought 40 more goals in a season he played knowing he had already been sold to [Juventus](/clubs/juventus) for £3.2 million, then a British record.

Turin did not suit him. He scored 14 goals in a side in transition, struggled with the defensive game of [Serie A](/leagues/serie-a) and with the isolation of being the only foreigner in a dressing room that spoke no English, and a year later Dalglish bought him back for £2.8 million. His second spell, from 1988 to 1996, yielded a fifth league title in 1990, two more FA Cups and a fifth League Cup in 1995, by which time he was captain and the last link with the great side of the previous decade. He left on a free in 1996 for Leeds, having scored 346 times in 660 games.

## How he played

Rush was the clearest example in British football of the striker as a finisher rather than a footballer. He touched the ball rarely, took it with his first touch into a shooting position and hit it low and early, usually across the goalkeeper into the far corner. His pace over 20 yards was deceptive because of his loose, long-legged stride, and he timed his runs onto Dalglish's passes so that the defender had no chance to recover. He scored almost nothing from outside the box and little from headers, despite his height, because that was not how Liverpool created chances.

The other half of his game was the pressing. Paisley and Joe Fagan built Liverpool's defensive shape from the front, and Rush, with his stamina and anticipation, chased every back-pass and cut off every passing lane. It is why the statistic that Liverpool never lost a match in which Rush scored held for 145 games until 1987. His weaknesses were a limited range of skills, a dependence on service from a creator of Dalglish's quality, and a game that, as his Italian year showed, could be neutralised by deep, man-marking defences that gave him no space to run into.

In the modern game he would be a prototype for the pressing centre forward, closer to Roberto Firmino's defensive role combined with Mohamed Salah's finishing, and his movement off a dropping striker would translate directly to a 4-4-2 or a 4-2-3-1 with a creative ten.

## Evolution

Rush's career mapped the end of the 4-4-2 era's great two-striker partnerships. In his first Liverpool spell he was the runner in a classic big-and-small pairing; in Italy he was asked to play as a lone target man against sweeper systems and found he lacked the back-to-goal game; in his second spell, as Dalglish aged and John Barnes and Peter Beardsley took over the creative work, he adapted by dropping deeper, linking play and scoring fewer but still regularly. By the mid-1990s, under Roy Evans, he was a mentor to Robbie Fowler, whom he taught the art of finishing with the first touch. After retiring he was briefly Chester manager, then Liverpool's ambassador and youth coach.

## Legacy

Rush is the reference point for every [Liverpool](/clubs/liverpool) striker since. Robbie Fowler and Michael Owen learned the early, low finish from him; Fernando Torres, Luis Suárez and Mohamed Salah have all been measured against his 346; and the pressing-from-the-front that Jürgen Klopp's [gegenpressing](/tactics/gegenpressing) Liverpool made famous has a direct ancestor in Rush chasing centre-halves for Paisley. His partnership with [Kenny Dalglish](/players/kenny-dalglish) remains the model for the dropping-creator-and-runner pairing in British football, echoed by [Eric Cantona](/players/eric-cantona) and Andy Cole at Manchester United and by Dennis Bergkamp and Thierry Henry at Arsenal. For [Wales](/nations/wales), his goals record stood until Gareth Bale broke it in 2018, and his inability to reach a tournament is one of the sadder facts of Welsh football before Euro 2016. He was voted into the English Football Hall of Fame in 2006.
