## Overview

Cafu is the most-capped male player in [Brazil](/nations/brazil)'s history with 142 appearances and the only footballer to start three [World Cup](/tournaments/world-cup) finals, in 1994, 1998 and 2002. He lifted the trophy as captain in Yokohama in 2002, the "Pendolino" (express train) having spent fifteen years tearing up and down the right touchline for São Paulo, [Roma](/clubs/roma) and [AC Milan](/clubs/ac-milan). Together with [Roberto Carlos](/players/roberto-carlos) on the opposite flank he changed what a full-back was expected to do.

## Early life and breakthrough

Marcos Evangelista de Morais was born in the Jardim Irene favela in São Paulo and nicknamed after Cafuringa, a 1970s winger. He was rejected by several clubs, including Corinthians and Santos, before São Paulo's youth system took him. Under Telê Santana he was converted from a winger into a right-sided midfielder and then a full-back, and he became a pillar of the São Paulo side that won consecutive [Copa Libertadores](/tournaments/copa-libertadores) and Intercontinental Cups in 1992 and 1993, beating [Barcelona](/clubs/barcelona) and Milan in Tokyo. He was named South American Footballer of the Year in 1994, the year he came on as a substitute in the World Cup final against [Italy](/nations/italy) after Jorginho was injured.

## Peak years

A brief, unhappy spell at Zaragoza and two years at Palmeiras preceded the move that defined his European career: Roma in 1997. Under Zdeněk Zeman and then Fabio Capello, Cafu became the engine of the right flank, and in 2000-01 he was a central figure in Roma's third Scudetto alongside [Francesco Totti](/players/francesco-totti), Gabriel Batistuta and Vincenzo Montella. In 2002, with Emerson injured, Luiz Felipe Scolari made him Brazil's captain. He played every minute of the tournament, climbed the podium with his wife's name scrawled on his shirt and ended a slightly chaotic qualifying campaign with the trophy in his hands.

At 33 Milan signed him on a free transfer in 2003 and he played five more seasons, forming part of [Carlo Ancelotti](/managers/carlo-ancelotti)'s great side with [Paolo Maldini](/players/paolo-maldini), [Alessandro Nesta](/players/alessandro-nesta), [Andrea Pirlo](/players/andrea-pirlo), [Clarence Seedorf](/players/clarence-seedorf) and [Kaká](/players/kaka). He won Serie A in 2004, lost the extraordinary 2005 Champions League final to [Liverpool](/clubs/liverpool) in Istanbul and then won the [Champions League](/leagues/champions-league) in 2007 against the same opponent.

## How he played

Cafu's game was built on repeat sprints. Tracking data was not yet standard in his era, but coaches estimated he made the most high-intensity runs in any team he played for, and he did it with no visible loss of quality late in matches. His positioning in possession was a template for the modern overlapping full-back: hugging the touchline when his winger came inside, cutting inside himself when the winger stayed wide, and timing the run so he received the ball at full speed.

Technically he was not a flawless crosser; many of his deliveries were low and hard rather than curled. His value was in the quality of the chances he created with combination play, quick one-twos and the pull-back from the byline. Defensively he relied on recovery pace, reading the pass to the winger and arriving at the second ball. He was rarely beaten one on one but he was not a crunching tackler, and against a side that pinned him back, such as the [France](/nations/france) of 1998, he could be neutralised. His discipline was poor early on, with a record for yellow cards in his first years at Roma.

## Evolution

Cafu was a winger who became a full-back, and in Italy he learned the defensive side of the job from Capello, who demanded he hold position until the ball was won. At Milan, in Ancelotti's 4-3-1-2 diamond with no natural wingers, he and Marek Jankulovski provided the only width, which gave him licence but also a heavier workload. He adapted by picking his moments, playing more in the half-space and leaning on Gennaro Gattuso's cover. He was still a regular starter at 37 and retired in 2008 after the Milan contract ended.

## Best plays and moments

- 1992, Intercontinental Cup, São Paulo 2-1 Barcelona: the right flank in a famous victory over Cruyff's Dream Team.
- 1993, Intercontinental Cup, São Paulo 3-2 AC Milan: a second world title in Tokyo.
- 1994, World Cup final v Italy: substitute for Jorginho, 90 minutes of concentration in the first penalty shoot-out final.
- 1997, Copa América final v Bolivia: a 3-1 win in La Paz in Brazil's first continental title since 1989.
- 2001, Serie A, Roma v Parma: the 3-1 win that sealed the Scudetto; Cafu's energy that spring was relentless ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/results?search_query=Cafu+Roma+2001+scudetto)).
- 2002, World Cup final, Brazil 2-0 Germany: captain, 90 minutes, and the trophy lift on the podium ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/results?search_query=Cafu+2002+World+Cup+captain+lifts+trophy)).
- 2004, Serie A, Milan: an immediate title in his first Milan season.
- 2005, Champions League final v Liverpool: the 3-3 heartbreak in Istanbul, starting on the right.
- 2006, World Cup, Brazil v Ghana: 142nd and final cap, aged 36, in a 3-0 win.
- 2007, Champions League final v Liverpool: a substitute's medal as Milan won 2-1 in Athens ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/results?search_query=Cafu+AC+Milan+2007+Champions+League)).

## Legacy

Cafu and Roberto Carlos made the full-back an attacking weapon, and every modern coach who asks a full-back to provide width, from [Pep Guardiola](/managers/pep-guardiola) to [Jürgen Klopp](/managers/jurgen-klopp), is working with a job description they wrote. He was named in the FIFA World Cup All-Star team twice and in the FIFPro World XI. He runs the Fundação Cafu, a social project in his old neighbourhood, and remains a regular Brazilian ambassador at FIFA events. His World Cup record, three finals and two winners' medals, may never be matched.
