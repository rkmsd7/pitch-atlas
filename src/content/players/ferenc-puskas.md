## Overview

Ferenc Puskás scored 84 goals in 85 games for [Hungary](/nations/hungary) and 242 in 262 for [Real Madrid](/clubs/real-madrid), and the second career began at 31 when he was overweight, exiled and supposedly finished. He captained the Mighty Magyars, the side that went unbeaten from 1950 to 1954, won Olympic gold in 1952 and destroyed [England](/nations/england) 6-3 at Wembley in 1953, a result that forced the English game to admit it had been overtaken. After the 1956 Soviet invasion he refused to return home, served a two-year ban, and then joined [Alfredo Di Stéfano](/players/alfredo-di-stefano) in Madrid to win three European Cups. FIFA's award for the best goal of the year carries his name, which is about right.

## Early life and breakthrough

Puskás was born on 1 April 1927 in Budapest and grew up in Kispest, where his father, Ferenc Purczeld, was a player and then coach at the local club. He debuted for Kispest at 16 in 1943 and for Hungary at 18 in August 1945, scoring against Austria. In 1949 the communist government turned Kispest into the army club Honvéd, gave Puskás the rank of major, hence the Galloping Major, and assembled around him the core of the national team: Sándor Kocsis, Zoltán Czibor, József Bozsik, Gyula Grosics.

Under Gusztáv Sebes the national side became the best in the world. They won the 1952 Olympic tournament in Helsinki, and in November 1953 became the first continental side to beat England at Wembley. Puskás scored twice in the 6-3, the first after a drag-back that left Billy Wright, in Geoffrey Green's phrase, like a fire engine going to the wrong fire. The return in Budapest in May 1954 finished 7-1.

## Peak years

Hungary went to the 1954 [World Cup](/tournaments/world-cup) as overwhelming favourites and beat West Germany 8-3 in the group, but Puskás was injured by a kick from Werner Liebrich in that match and missed the quarter-final and semi-final. He returned half-fit for the final in Bern, scored after six minutes, and Hungary led 2-0 after eight, but the Germans came back to win 3-2 in the rain; Puskás had a late equaliser ruled out for offside. It was Hungary's first defeat in 32 matches and the end of their chance at immortality.

In November 1956, while Honvéd were in Spain for a European Cup tie, Soviet tanks entered Budapest. Puskás, Kocsis and Czibor refused to go home and toured South America and Europe with the club before being banned by UEFA. Puskás was 31, heavy and considered done when Emil Östreicher persuaded Santiago Bernabéu to sign him in 1958. He lost the weight, won the Pichichi four times, scored four of Madrid's seven in the 1960 European Cup final against Eintracht Frankfurt and a hat-trick in the 1962 final lost 5-3 to [Benfica](/clubs/benfica). He won the European Cup again in 1966 as a 39-year-old squad player, and retired that year.

## How he played

Puskás played as an inside-left who was really a goalscorer. He used the left foot for everything, including corners from both sides and the occasional tackle, and the right was described by his team-mates as being for standing on. What made the left foot so dangerous was the preparation: a first touch that stopped the ball dead regardless of pace, and a drag-back or feint that created half a yard before a defender could close. From there he shot with little backlift and great accuracy, low into corners, or chipped the goalkeeper from the edge of the area.

He was not quick and did not run much, which is why English observers in 1953 dismissed him before the game as "a little fat chap". The Hungarian system, with Nándor Hidegkuti dropping from centre-forward into midfield, created the space in front of goal that let Puskás and Kocsis feed on it, an early model of what later became the [false nine](/tactics/false-nine). Weaknesses are obvious: pace, work-rate off the ball, right foot. In today's game he would be a penalty-box forward in a side that could bring the ball to him, something like a left-footed Harry Kane without the dropping, and his finishing would score in any era.

## Evolution

The young Puskás of the late 1940s scored 50 goals in a Hungarian season; the Honvéd and Hungary version of 1950 to 1956 was a complete inside-forward who also created for Kocsis. After the ban and the move to Madrid he became a pure finisher, happy to leave the running to Di Stéfano and Paco Gento. The 1960 final shows the division: Di Stéfano everywhere, Puskás waiting, and four goals. He became a Spanish citizen in 1961 and played four games for [Spain](/nations/spain) at the 1962 World Cup.

Around him the sport moved from the W-M, which Hungary's withdrawn centre-forward had broken, to the 4-2-4 of [Brazil](/nations/brazil) in 1958, a shape that borrowed directly from the Magyars. The Hungarian school scattered after 1956 and the country's football never recovered.

## Legacy

Puskás is the model of the goalscorer who makes physical limitations irrelevant, and the proof that finishing is a skill that ages well. The Hungarian side he captained is the origin of a line of thinking that runs through Brazil's 4-2-4, [total football](/tactics/total-football) and the modern [false nine](/tactics/false-nine): a centre-forward who drops to create space for the inside-forwards to score. His partnership with [Alfredo Di Stéfano](/players/alfredo-di-stefano) at [Real Madrid](/clubs/real-madrid) defined the club's first great era, and the forward line they shared with [Raymond Kopa](/players/raymond-kopa) and Gento is the standard against which Madrid's later attacks are judged.

He coached Panathinaikos to the 1971 European Cup final and was finally welcomed home to Hungary in 1981 after 25 years in exile. The national stadium in Budapest is named for him. He died there on 17 November 2006 and was given a state funeral. [Eusébio](/players/eusebio), [Pelé](/players/pele) and Di Stéfano all called him the finest finisher they had seen, and 84 in 85 for his country suggests they were being accurate.
