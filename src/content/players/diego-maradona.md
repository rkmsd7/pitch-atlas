## Overview

Diego Maradona was 1.65 m of defiance. He won the [World Cup](/tournaments/world-cup) almost single-handedly in 1986, the only time in the modern era that one player so plainly decided the tournament, and then took [Napoli](/clubs/napoli), a club that had never won Serie A, to two titles and a UEFA Cup against the richest teams in the world. He did it with the ball glued to a left foot that could pass, dribble, shoot and bend free-kicks, and with a personality that treated every match as a grievance to be settled. The life around the football, the cocaine, the Camorra, the suspensions, the ejection from the 1994 World Cup, is inseparable from the legend, and [Argentina](/nations/argentina) has never pretended otherwise. He is a saint in Naples and a god in Buenos Aires, and both cities mean it.

## Early life and breakthrough

Maradona was born on 30 October 1960 in Lanús and grew up in Villa Fiorito, a shanty town on the southern edge of Buenos Aires, the fifth of eight children. Scouted at eight for Argentinos Juniors' youth side, Los Cebollitas, he became a half-time entertainer juggling the ball for crowds. He made his first-division debut ten days before his 16th birthday, in October 1976, and nutmegged an opponent with his first touch. He was capped at 16 in February 1977, then left out of César Luis Menotti's 1978 World Cup squad, a rejection he carried for years. He answered by winning the 1979 World Youth Championship in Japan, scoring in the final against the Soviet Union, and by being the Argentine league's top scorer in five successive tournaments.

A move to [Boca Juniors](/clubs/boca-juniors) in 1981 brought the Metropolitano title and a goal against River Plate that he still rated among his favourites. The 1982 World Cup in Spain was a failure, ending with a red card for a kick at Brazil's Batista, but [Barcelona](/clubs/barcelona) paid a world-record £5 million for him that summer anyway.

## Peak years

Barcelona was a false start: hepatitis, then a broken ankle from Andoni Goikoetxea's tackle in September 1983, then a Copa del Rey final brawl with Athletic Bilbao in 1984 that ended his time in Spain. Napoli paid another world record, around £6.9 million, and 75,000 people came to the Stadio San Paolo to watch him kick a ball in the air. Over the next seven years he turned a relegation candidate into Italy's champions. The 1986-87 Scudetto, the first ever won by a club from the south, prompted a mock funeral for [Juventus](/clubs/juventus) and [AC Milan](/clubs/ac-milan) in the streets of Naples. The 1989 UEFA Cup, won over two legs against Stuttgart, and the 1989-90 title, won in a bitter race with Arrigo Sacchi's Milan, completed the set.

In between came Mexico 1986. Maradona scored five goals and made five more in seven matches, and the quarter-final against [England](/nations/england) contained both the Hand of God and the goal voted the best of the century, a 60-metre run past five players and Peter Shilton. He scored two more in the semi-final against [Belgium](/nations/belgium), the second another slalom, and in the final against West Germany he produced the pass for Jorge Burruchaga's winner from inside his own half. Carlos Bilardo's 3-5-2 was built entirely to give him the ball; the system is still the clearest demonstration of what a single player can be worth. At Italia 90, with a swollen ankle and a Napoli-Italy semi-final in his own adopted stadium, he took a limited Argentina to the final again.

## How he played

Maradona played as an attacking midfielder who was really a free agent, drifting between the lines, out to the left and back to collect from his defenders. Almost everything went through his left foot; defenders knew this and could not stop it, because the foot was good enough for every job. His dribbling relied on a very low centre of gravity, enormous strength through the thighs and hips, and a touch that kept the ball so close that a tackle had to go through him to reach it. He accelerated from standing in two strides and could change direction while at full speed, which is what the England goal shows at every frame.

He was a brilliant passer, not just in the obvious through-ball but in the disguised lay-off and the chipped ball over a line, the sort that set up Caniggia against Brazil in 1990. Free-kicks were struck with the inside of the foot and curled over walls from improbable angles. He was a competent header for his size and a willing tackler when provoked. Weaknesses: the game had to be organised around him, he did not track back as a habit, and his fitness fluctuated with his lifestyle, so his peak years were fewer than his talent deserved. In a modern side he would be the player a manager like [Pep Guardiola](/managers/pep-guardiola) builds a half-space around, a left-sided number 10 with license to roam and a team that compensates behind him. He would also be fouled far less, which would have added years.

## Evolution

The teenage Maradona at Argentinos Juniors was a goalscoring forward who dribbled past everyone because he could. Boca and Barcelona taught him that defenders in good leagues kick, and he developed the bulk and balance to absorb it. At Napoli, under Ottavio Bianchi and then Alberto Bigon, he became a complete playmaker, scoring fewer goals but running the team's attack through the Brazilian striker Careca. By 1990 he was playing more as a deep-lying creator, with his explosive pace reduced by the ankle injuries and the weight he had gained.

The game around him moved from the man-marking of [catenaccio](/tactics/catenaccio) Italy, where he was shadowed by a designated defender in every match, to Sacchi's zonal pressing at Milan, which was designed partly to take away the time he lived on. His last act with Argentina, a goal against Greece at USA 94 and a failed drugs test, closed the door. A brief Boca comeback ended in 1997.

## Legacy

Maradona is the reason the Argentine number 10 is a sacred office. [Lionel Messi](/players/lionel-messi) spent a career being compared to him, and when Messi finally won the World Cup in 2022 the first thing many Argentines did was invoke Diego, who had died two years earlier on 25 November 2020. Every small left-footed dribbler since, from Juan Román Riquelme to Pablo Aimar to Messi himself, has been assessed through his example. In Italy he showed that a provincial club could beat the north, and Napoli's stadium now carries his name; the club's 2023 Scudetto, their first since his, was celebrated with his face everywhere.

Tactically he forced coaches to think about how one player can be both the system and the exception to it. Bilardo's 1986 side and the Napoli teams that gave him the ball and let the rest adjust were the clearest examples of a team built around an individual, a model that [Pep Guardiola](/managers/pep-guardiola) later revisited with Messi at Barcelona and that rivals the collective idea of [total football](/tactics/total-football) as a path to greatness. As a coach he took Argentina to the 2010 World Cup quarter-finals and lost 4-0 to Germany. As a man he was generous, self-destructive, politically combative and never dull. He sits beside [Pelé](/players/pele) at the top of most lists, and ahead of him in the hearts of those who prefer their genius flawed.
