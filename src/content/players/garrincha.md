## Overview

Garrincha was born with a right leg that bent inwards and a left that bent outwards, and a spine that curved, and doctors said he would never be an athlete. He became the most beautiful dribbler in football history. [Brazil](/nations/brazil) never lost a match in which he and [Pelé](/players/pele) both played, and when Pelé was injured in 1962, Garrincha won the [World Cup](/tournaments/world-cup) more or less alone, scoring four goals from the right wing and being voted the tournament's best player. His life was chaotic, he drank himself to death at 49, and was buried in his home town under a plaque that calls him the Joy of the People.

## Early life and breakthrough

Manuel Francisco dos Santos was born on 28 October 1933 in Pau Grande, a textile-factory town in the hills above Rio de Janeiro. His sister called him Garrincha, after a small wren, because he was tiny. He played for the factory team and showed no ambition at all until a friend took him to a trial at Botafogo in 1953, where he nutmegged the Brazil left-back Nílton Santos, who told the club to sign him so that he would not have to face him. He scored a hat-trick on his debut.

Botafogo's coaches gave up trying to make him pass or track back and accepted him as he was. He was capped in 1955 but not a regular until the 1958 World Cup, where senior players reportedly petitioned coach Vicente Feola to pick him and Pelé for the third group match against the Soviet Union. The first three minutes of that game, Garrincha hitting the post and tormenting the left-back Kuznetsov, are among the most famous in Brazilian football.

## Peak years

In the 1958 final against Sweden, Brazil went 1-0 down and Garrincha created the next two goals with identical runs to the byline and cut-backs for Vavá. Brazil won 5-2 and he had made the right flank his property. Back home with Botafogo he won the Carioca championship in 1957, 1961 and 1962 and the Rio-São Paulo tournament in 1962 and 1964.

Then came Chile in 1962. Pelé was hurt in the second game and Garrincha took over. He scored twice against [England](/nations/england) in the quarter-final, a header and a shot from outside the box, and twice against the hosts Chile in the semi-final before being sent off for retaliation. He was allowed to play in the final against Czechoslovakia, despite the red card and a fever, and Brazil won 3-1. He was named player of the tournament and shared the Golden Boot on four goals. A knee that had been troubling him for years broke down after that, and the 1966 World Cup was his last tournament. The defeat to Hungary at Goodison Park was the only one of his 50 internationals he lost.

## How he played

Garrincha played on the right wing and almost never moved. Full-backs knew that he would show them the inside, drop a shoulder and go outside, and they still could not stop it, because the acceleration from standing was so sudden and the legs, with their strange angles, gave his body a lean that no defender could read. When he did go inside it was a shock, and several of his goals came from cutting onto his right foot and shooting from range, including the famous banana shot against Fiorentina in 1958 where he dribbled past four, rounded the keeper, waited on the line for a defender to arrive and beat him too before scoring.

His crossing was accurate and delivered early, which is why Vavá scored two in a World Cup final from his deliveries. He had no interest in tactics and asked before the 1958 final whether Brazil had already agreed the score with Sweden. Weaknesses: no defensive work, no positional discipline, and a short career of real quality from 1957 to 1962. In today's game he would be a pure one-v-one right winger who stays wide and waits, the role that Arjen Robben and Bukayo Saka played, and a coach would simply build a team that gave him the ball.

## Evolution

There was no evolution in the usual sense, which is part of the legend. Garrincha arrived at Botafogo doing one thing and left doing the same thing a little slower. What changed was the context: in 1958 he was the fourth or fifth most important player in a Brazil team with Didi, Pelé, Vavá and Zagallo; in 1962 he was the first. His scoring went up when the knee forced him to cut inside more often, and his famous goals from 1962 were shots rather than crosses.

After the knee surgeries he was a shadow, drifting through Corinthians, a single game in Colombia, [Flamengo](/clubs/flamengo) and Olaria, and he retired in 1972. The football around him changed completely: the 4-2-4 that gave him a winger's freedom became the 4-3-3 of 1970, where [Jairzinho](/players/jairzinho) played a similar role with far more running, and then the pressing systems of the 1970s that would have asked him to do things he never did.

## Legacy

Garrincha is Brazil's patron saint of the dribble, the player the country cites when it wants to explain that football is supposed to be fun. Every Brazilian winger since has been compared to him: [Jairzinho](/players/jairzinho), who inherited the shirt in 1970, Zico's Flamengo team-mate Tita, Robinho, Neymar and [Vinícius Júnior](/players/vinicius-junior), whose outside-foot feints on the left are Garrincha's run mirrored. Coaches who build sides around a wide one-v-one specialist are following the logic Botafogo settled on in 1953: do not fix him, feed him.

He also represents football's failure to protect the people who make it. He left the game with nothing and died of cirrhosis in Rio on 20 January 1983. The stadium at Brasília carries his name, and Botafogo's supporters still call themselves the club of Garrincha. For [Brazil](/nations/brazil), in a record of 50 games with only one defeat, he is the player who never lost when it mattered.
