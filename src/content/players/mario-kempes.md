## Overview

Mario Kempes won the 1978 [World Cup](/tournaments/world-cup) almost single-handedly in its last three matches. He had not scored in the first round; César Luis Menotti told him to shave his moustache for luck, and he scored twice against Poland, twice against Peru and twice in the final against the [Netherlands](/nations/netherlands), the second a surging, bouncing run through four defenders in extra time. Golden Boot, Golden Ball, [Argentina](/nations/argentina)'s first World Cup. He was also the only foreign-based player in Menotti's squad, a star at [Valencia](/clubs/valencia) who had twice won the Pichichi, and remains one of the most beloved figures in both countries.

## Early life and breakthrough

Kempes was born in Bell Ville, Córdoba province, in July 1954, the son of a footballer who taught him to play with his left foot. He joined Instituto de Córdoba's youth section and scored 11 goals in 13 games on his first-team debut season in 1973, earning a move to Rosario Central. There he scored 85 goals in 107 league matches in three seasons, top scorer in 1974 and 1976, and was taken to the 1974 World Cup at 19, playing three games as Argentina struggled in West Germany.

Valencia paid about 100 million pesetas for him in 1976. He was the first Argentine to play in Spain's top flight in a generation, and he scored 24 goals in his first [La Liga](/leagues/la-liga) season and 28 in his second, winning the Pichichi both times. By the summer of 1978 he was the best striker in Spain, and Menotti, who had kept a strict domestic-only policy, made an exception.

## Peak years

The 1978 tournament, played under the military junta and in a storm of ticker tape, was his. After the barren first round the second-round group began against Poland in Rosario, where he scored both goals in a 2-0 win and handled on the line to deny Grzegorz Lato. A 0-0 with [Brazil](/nations/brazil) followed, and then the 6-0 against Peru that Argentina needed by four goals, Kempes scoring twice. In the final at the Monumental he put Argentina ahead in the first half, Dick Nanninga equalised, Rob Rensenbrink hit the post in the last minute, and in extra time Kempes bundled through the Dutch defence for 2-1 before making Daniel Bertoni's third.

He went back to Valencia and won the 1979 Copa del Rey and the 1980 Cup Winners' Cup, beating [Arsenal](/clubs/arsenal) on penalties in Brussels, then the UEFA Super Cup against Nottingham Forest. A return to [River Plate](/clubs/river-plate) in 1981 brought the Nacional title, and he played in the 1982 World Cup alongside a young [Diego Maradona](/players/diego-maradona), his last international tournament.

## How he played

Kempes was a forward who began his runs from midfield. At 1.83 m with a long stride, he picked the ball up 40 yards from goal and simply ran, keeping it on his left foot, bouncing off challenges and finishing low and early. The extra-time goal in the 1978 final, which took three touches through three defenders and a goalkeeper and a rebound, is the signature: not elegant, but unstoppable. He was often described as a bulldozer with a pianist's left foot.

He was a clean striker of a moving ball, scored freely from outside the box and was good in the air for a player who preferred the ground. His stamina was exceptional; he was still running at full pace in the 120th minute in Buenos Aires. Menotti used him as the spearhead of a 4-3-3 with Leopoldo Luque beside him and Bertoni wide, and Valencia played him as a withdrawn striker behind a centre-forward, which is where his dribbling did the most damage.

His weaknesses were his right foot, which barely existed, and a tendency to drift out of matches when the service was poor, as in the first round of 1978. He was not a presser and not a playmaker, despite the number 10. In the modern game he would be a left-sided inside forward or a second striker in a 4-4-2, a less refined Thierry Henry or a more direct Kai Havertz.

## Evolution

Kempes went from a pure goalscorer at Rosario, where the game was fast and open, to the more complete forward of Valencia, who had to beat man-markers and sweepers in Spain. The 1978 World Cup saw him at his physical best, and the second Valencia spell and River Plate year were still productive. From 1984 he wound down at Hércules and then in Austria, where he won nothing but played until he was 36. The sport changed around him from the 4-3-3 of the 1970s to the pressing and offside traps of the 1980s, which gave a deep-starting runner less room; his style belonged to the era before midfields were compressed. He coached in Albania, Venezuela, Bolivia and Indonesia afterwards and became a television commentator in the United States.

## Legacy

Kempes was Argentina's first global superstar of the television age and the bridge between [Alfredo Di Stéfano](/players/alfredo-di-stefano)'s generation, who left for Europe and never played a World Cup, and [Diego Maradona](/players/diego-maradona)'s, who did both. His 1978 made the number 10 shirt a national symbol before Maradona wore it, and the sight of a long-haired striker running from midfield shaped how Argentine forwards saw themselves; Gabriel Batistuta and later Lautaro Martínez have both cited him. Menotti's attacking philosophy, the alternative to the Bilardo pragmatism that won 1986, is remembered through Kempes' goals more than any tactical diagram.

In Valencia he is the greatest player in the club's history, with the Mestalla's Kempes stand and a statue outside it, and he was named by [Pelé](/players/pele) in the FIFA 100. When Lionel Messi scored twice in the 2022 final in Qatar, the Argentine press reached for Kempes, the first number 10 to score two in a World Cup final for the country. He remains, in the words of the Argentine commentator José María Muñoz, "the man who gave us the first".
