## Overview

Alessandro Del Piero spent 19 seasons at [Juventus](/clubs/juventus), playing 705 games and scoring 290 goals, both club records, and captained the team through the Calciopoli relegation of 2006 and back to the top. He won the 1996 Champions League, six Serie A titles that still stand and the 2006 World Cup with [Italy](/nations/italy). He is the model of the Italian second striker, a player of elegance whose finishing from the left corner of the box became a geographic term.

## Early life and breakthrough

Born in Conegliano in the Veneto in November 1974, Del Piero was the son of an electrician and played youth football at San Vendemiano before Padova signed him at 13. He made his Serie B debut at 16 and Juventus paid around five billion lire for him in 1993. Giovanni Trapattoni gave him his Serie A debut in September 1993, and within weeks he had scored a hat-trick against Parma and a bicycle kick against Fiorentina. Under Marcello Lippi from 1994 he became the apprentice to Roberto Baggio, then his replacement.

## Peak years

Del Piero was the star of Lippi's Juventus between 1995 and 1998, winning the 1996 Champions League against [Ajax](/clubs/ajax) in Rome and three Serie A titles in four years, and reaching two more Champions League finals in 1997 and 1998 alongside [Zinedine Zidane](/players/zinedine-zidane). In 1997-98 he scored 32 goals in all competitions, including ten in the Champions League, and finished sixth in the Ballon d'Or.

A serious knee injury at Udine in November 1998 took a year and a half from his prime and changed his game. He returned to win two more titles under Lippi in 2002 and 2003, the latter alongside [Pavel Nedvěd](/players/pavel-nedved), and reached the 2003 Champions League final against [AC Milan](/clubs/ac-milan). At the 2006 World Cup he came off the bench in the semi-final against [Germany](/nations/germany) to score the second goal in the 120th minute and converted his penalty in the final shoot-out against [France](/nations/france).

## How he played

The Del Piero zone was the area between the left corner of the penalty box and the byline. He received there with his back half to goal, opened his body with the first touch, and curled the ball with the inside of the right foot into the far top corner, over or around the goalkeeper. He scored dozens of goals from that spot and defenders knew it was coming.

He was a complete finisher otherwise: free-kicks from 25 yards with a whipping, dipping technique, penalties under pressure, and intelligent runs onto through balls. He dribbled with a close, neat style, and in his early years had explosive acceleration. He was also a generous creator, dropping into pockets to combine with a striker, first Gianluca Vialli and Fabrizio Ravanelli, later David Trezeguet, with whom he formed one of Serie A's great partnerships.

Weaknesses: after the 1998 injury he lost a yard, and his relationship with Italy fans was fraught; he was often substituted and criticised at tournaments, especially Euro 2000 when he missed chances in the final.

## Evolution

He began as a quick, dribbling second striker and became a playmaking one after the knee injury, relying on positioning, technique and set pieces. Under Fabio Capello in 2005-06 he was a squad player; after Calciopoli, when Juventus were relegated and stripped of two titles, he stayed as captain and top scored in Serie B with 20 goals. He then had a late renaissance under Claudio Ranieri, winning the Serie A Capocannoniere at 33 in 2007-08 with 21 goals. He played a supporting role in Antonio Conte's unbeaten 2011-12 title season before the club chose not to renew his contract, and he finished in Australia and India.

## Legacy

Del Piero is the Juventus player of the modern era, the captain who stayed through Serie B and whose goal records are unlikely to be broken. He stands with [Francesco Totti](/players/francesco-totti) and Roberto Baggio as the great Italian number tens of the 1990s and 2000s, and his left-channel finish is still coached as a template. Since retiring in 2014 he has worked as a pundit for Sky Italia and CBS, owns a restaurant in Los Angeles, and has been talked about repeatedly for a Juventus boardroom role. He received a standing ovation from the Bernabéu in 2008; it is hard to think of a higher compliment for an Italian forward.
