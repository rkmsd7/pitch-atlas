## Overview

Paul Gascoigne was, for a few years either side of 1990, the best midfielder in England and one of the best in the world, a player whose combination of dribbling, vision, shooting and sheer joy had no equal in the English game since [George Best](/players/george-best). His tears in Turin after a booking in the 1990 [World Cup](/tournaments/world-cup) semi-final made him a national figure and, it is often argued, began the process by which English football became fashionable again after the hooligan decade. A reckless tackle in the 1991 FA Cup final wrecked his knee; alcoholism and the chaos of his private life did the rest. He still won the FA Cup, two Scottish titles and 57 caps, and scored one of the best goals in a European Championship.

## Early life and breakthrough

Gascoigne was born in Gateshead in May 1967 and grew up on the Dunston estate, where his talent was obvious from primary school and his anxiety, tics and compulsions were already present, aggravated by a childhood in which he witnessed the death of a friend's younger brother in a road accident. [Newcastle United](/clubs/newcastle-united) signed him at 13 and gave him a debut at 17 in 1985. By 1987-88 he was the best young player in the country, winning the PFA Young Player of the Year, and [Alex Ferguson](/managers/alex-ferguson), who thought he had agreed to sign him for Manchester United, found that Terry Venables and [Tottenham](/clubs/tottenham-hotspur) had got there first with a £2.2 million fee and a house for his parents.

## Peak years

At Tottenham he learned a professional's habits under Venables and found, in [Gary Lineker](/players/gary-lineker), the finisher who made his passes count. Bobby Robson took him to Italia 90 reluctantly, calling him "daft as a brush", and he was England's best player: a man-of-the-match performance against the Netherlands, the pass for David Platt's winner against Belgium, the cross for Lineker's penalty against Cameroon, and in the semi-final the booking that would have ruled him out of the final, the tears, and the finest half-hour he ever played as he tried to win the match anyway.

In 1990-91 he carried Tottenham to the FA Cup almost alone, scoring in every round, including the 35-yard free-kick against [Arsenal](/clubs/arsenal) in the semi-final. In the final his lunging tackle on Gary Charles ruptured his own cruciate ligament. Lazio waited a year and paid £5.5 million anyway. He scored against Roma in the derby, broke his leg again, and left for [Rangers](/clubs/rangers) in 1995, where under Walter Smith he had the best club season of his career, 19 goals and the player of the year awards in 1995-96. At Euro 96 he scored the goal of the tournament against [Scotland](/nations/scotland), and England lost another semi-final to Germany on penalties. Glenn Hoddle left him out of the 1998 World Cup squad, and his decline was steep thereafter.

## How he played

Gascoigne played as the attacking midfielder in a 4-4-2, nominally on the left of a diamond or centrally behind two strikers, but in practice wherever the ball was. His dribbling relied on strength as much as skill: he was squat and powerful, held off tackles with his arms and body, and used shifts of weight to send defenders the wrong way. He had the vision to see through-balls early and the technique to deliver them with either foot, outside of the boot included, and his shooting from distance, combined with a dead-ball technique learned on Tyneside, made him dangerous from 30 yards.

His flaws were as evident. He did not track back, lost concentration and discipline, and was prone to the rash challenge that ended up injuring himself more often than opponents. His fitness was a constant battle. Venables said he had never coached anyone so gifted or so hard to manage. Translated to today, he would be a number 10 or a free eight with the ball-carrying of a Jack Grealish and the passing of a prime Mesut Özil, a player a coach would build a side around and then worry about every day.

## Evolution

Gascoigne's game was reshaped by injury. Before 1991 he was a dribbler and runner who could cover ground; after the cruciate and the second leg break in 1994 he became a more static playmaker, his bursts shorter, his passing more central to his game. At Rangers he reinvented himself as a goalscoring midfielder, arriving late in the box rather than carrying the ball there. The sport around him changed just as fast: the Premier League's money and the Bosman ruling arrived while he was in Italy, and the attention his life attracted from the tabloids was unprecedented for an English footballer. His later years, in which he has spoken openly about addiction, were a warning about what the game does to those it does not protect.

## Legacy

Gascoigne's legacy is cultural as much as footballing. The tears of Turin and the Euro 96 summer made him the face of a decade in which the [Premier League](/leagues/premier-league) was born and [England](/nations/england) fell back in love with its national team. As a player, he was the last great English maverick in the line of [George Best](/players/george-best) and Glenn Hoddle, and the comparison with [Eric Cantona](/players/eric-cantona), his contemporary, is instructive: Cantona's talent found a manager who could manage him; Gascoigne's never quite did. Wayne Rooney and Jack Grealish have both been described as the closest things since to his combination of power and invention. At [Tottenham](/clubs/tottenham-hotspur) and [Rangers](/clubs/rangers) he is remembered for single seasons that were as good as any a midfielder has had at either club. His influence on coaches was less a tactical template than a case study in man-management, and the most honest tribute came from Bobby Robson, who said he would have been the best player in the world if only he had looked after himself.
