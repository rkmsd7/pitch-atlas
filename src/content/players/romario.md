## Overview

Romário was the best finisher of the 1990s and possibly of any decade. He scored 55 goals in 70 games for [Brazil](/nations/brazil), five of them at the 1994 [World Cup](/tournaments/world-cup) where he was Golden Ball winner and the reason Brazil ended a 24-year wait, and he was FIFA World Player of the Year the same year. At [Barcelona](/clubs/barcelona) he scored 30 La Liga goals in his first season for [Johan Cruyff](/managers/johan-cruyff-manager)'s Dream Team, including a hat-trick against [Real Madrid](/clubs/real-madrid). He counted his own thousandth goal in 2007, a figure FIFA does not recognise and Brazil does not question. Inside the box he was close to perfect.

## Early life and breakthrough

Romário grew up in Jacarezinho, one of Rio de Janeiro's largest favelas, and came through Olaria and then Vasco da Gama, where he debuted in 1985 and won two Carioca titles. His 1988 Olympics, where he was top scorer with seven goals as Brazil took silver, earned a move to [PSV Eindhoven](/clubs/psv-eindhoven), the newly crowned European champions. In five seasons at PSV he scored 128 goals in 167 games, won three Eredivisie titles and was top scorer three times.

He scored twice on his Brazil debut against Republic of Ireland in 1987 and won the 1989 Copa América with the winner in the final match against Uruguay. A dispute with coach Sebastião Lazaroni limited him to one substitute appearance at the 1990 World Cup.

## Peak years

Cruyff signed him for Barcelona in 1993 and he was instant: 30 goals in 33 league games, the Pichichi, a hat-trick in a 5-0 Clásico in January 1994, and a title won on the final day. Then came 1994. Carlos Alberto Parreira had left him out until a 2-0 win over Uruguay in the final qualifier in which Romário scored both goals and saved Brazil's World Cup. In the United States he scored against Russia, Cameroon, Sweden (a header, at 1.67 m), the quarter-final winner against [Netherlands](/nations/netherlands) was set up by him, and he scored in the semi-final against Sweden again. In the final against [Italy](/nations/italy) he converted his penalty in the shoot-out.

He fell out with Cruyff in early 1995 and returned to Brazil with [Flamengo](/clubs/flamengo), where he scored 155 goals in 189 games across four spells interrupted by a brief return to Spain with Valencia. He won the 1997 Copa América and Confederations Cup with [Ronaldo](/players/ronaldo-nazario) alongside him, a partnership nicknamed Ro-Ro, but a calf injury on the eve of the 1998 World Cup ruled him out and he was controversially omitted by Luiz Felipe Scolari in 2002 despite scoring freely for Vasco.

## How he played

Romário played almost entirely inside the penalty area and the ten metres outside it. His genius was in the first touch and the second: he could receive a pass with his back to goal, drop a shoulder so slightly that the defender barely shifted, and in the same motion open a lane for a shot. He scored many goals with the toe-poke, which he used not as a trick but because it needed no backlift and arrived before the goalkeeper was set.

He was two-footed in finishing, superb at the chip, deadly at the near post, and surprisingly good in the air because he timed runs to arrive unmarked. Weaknesses: no pressing, no tracking, no link play to speak of, an open contempt for training and for coaches who demanded it. Cruyff tolerated it for one season; Parreira built a cautious team to carry him. In today's game he would be a penalty-box forward in a possession side that creates many chances, with the understanding that the ten other players press. He is the model for [Sergio Agüero](/players/sergio-aguero), who shared his frame.

## Evolution

Romário barely changed his game in 20 years, which is itself remarkable. He was a penalty-box striker at 20 and at 40. What changed was his body, slowly, and the teams around him. At PSV he was a pure finisher in a Dutch possession system; at Barcelona Cruyff used him as the point of a [tiki-taka](/tactics/tiki-taka) prototype with [Hristo Stoichkov](/players/hristo-stoichkov) and Michael Laudrup feeding him; for Brazil in 1994 he was the one luxury in a disciplined 4-4-2 with Dunga and Mauro Silva doing the work. Back in Brazil he scored for Flamengo, Vasco and Fluminense into his late thirties, won the Brazilian championship with Vasco in 2000 at 34 and was its top scorer at 39 in 2005. He scored his self-declared 1000th goal in 2007, retired in 2009 and became a senator.

The game evolved towards athleticism and pressing while he stayed still, and the fact that he kept scoring regardless is the case against the idea that every forward must do everything.

## Legacy

Romário is the archetype of the Brazilian number 9 who wins games on his own in the box, the direct link between [Pelé](/players/pele)'s finishing and [Ronaldo](/players/ronaldo-nazario)'s, and the teacher of a thousand toe-pokes. [Neymar](/players/neymar) and [Ronaldinho](/players/ronaldinho) both named him as a childhood idol; Cruyff called him the best he coached in the box, an opinion he never withdrew even after their fall-out. His 1994 was the moment Brazil chose pragmatism, under [Carlos Alberto Parreira](/managers/carlos-alberto-parreira), over the romance of 1982, and Romário was the finisher that choice depended on.

Tactically his career is the counter-argument to total pressing: a side built to feed one static genius can still win the biggest prize. Managers from [Carlo Ancelotti](/managers/carlo-ancelotti) to [José Mourinho](/managers/jose-mourinho) have accepted that trade at times. On it, [watch the goals](https://www.youtube.com/results?search_query=Romario+best+goals); there are a thousand of them, give or take.
