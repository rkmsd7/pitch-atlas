## Overview

Pelé is the measuring stick. Before [Diego Maradona](/players/diego-maradona), before [Lionel Messi](/players/lionel-messi), the question "who is the greatest?" had one answer, and it was the boy from Três Corações who won the [World Cup](/tournaments/world-cup) at 17, lost most of 1962 and 1966 to injury and brutality, and then returned in 1970 as the senior statesman of a [Brazil](/nations/brazil) side that played the most joyful football ever captured in colour. He scored more than 1,200 goals if you count the friendlies and tours that made up much of a Santos player's calendar, and 77 in 92 for Brazil that nobody disputes.

## Early life and breakthrough

Edson Arantes do Nascimento was born on 23 October 1940 in Três Corações, Minas Gerais, the son of Dondinho, a Fluminense forward whose career was wrecked by a knee injury. The family moved to Bauru in São Paulo state, where Edson shined shoes and played with a sock stuffed with rags. Waldemar de Brito, a 1934 World Cup forward who coached him at Bauru's youth side, took the 15-year-old to Santos in 1956 and told the directors he would be the best player in the world.

He scored on his debut in September 1956, was the Paulista top scorer at 16 with 32 goals in 1957, and won his first cap against [Argentina](/nations/argentina) in July 1957, scoring in a 2-1 defeat. Brazil's coach Vicente Feola took him to Sweden in 1958 half-fit and did not use him until the third group game. Then came the winner against Wales in the quarter-final, a hat-trick against [France](/nations/france) in the semi-final, and two in the final against Sweden, including the one where he cushioned a high ball on his thigh, lobbed it over Bengt Gustavsson and volleyed in. He was 17 years and 249 days old. He wept on Gilmar's shoulder at the end, and the whole world saw him do it.

## Peak years

Statistically, Pelé's best years were at Santos between 1958 and 1965, when he scored 58, 127, 110, 111, 95, 76 and 101 goals in successive calendar years, counting every tour match. Santos under Lula built a team around him with Pepe, Coutinho, Mengálvio and Zito, and won the Copa Libertadores in 1962 and 1963 and the Intercontinental Cup both times, hammering [Benfica](/clubs/benfica) 5-2 in Lisbon in 1962 with a Pelé hat-trick so complete that [Eusébio](/players/eusebio) called it the night he knew what greatness looked like, and beating [AC Milan](/clubs/ac-milan) over a play-off in 1963.

The World Cups in between were cruel. In 1962 he scored against Mexico and then tore a thigh muscle against Czechoslovakia; [Garrincha](/players/garrincha) carried Brazil to the title without him. In 1966 Bulgaria and then Portugal kicked him off the pitch, Morais's double foul in the Goodison Park game becoming an emblem of how the tournament had failed to protect its stars. He said he would never play in a World Cup again.

He changed his mind, and 1970 was the vindication. Fit, 29 years old and operating slightly deeper than in 1958, he scored four goals in Mexico, headed the opener in the final against [Italy](/nations/italy), and laid on the fourth for [Carlos Alberto](/players/carlos-alberto) with a pass rolled sideways without looking, the final act of what many consider the greatest team goal ever scored. He was named the tournament's best player.
## How he played

Pelé played as a second striker in a line of five forwards in 1958, as a number 10 behind Coutinho at Santos, and as something close to a modern attacking midfielder by 1970, when [Jairzinho](/players/jairzinho) and Tostão occupied defenders ahead of him. Whatever the label, his game had the same ingredients. He was genuinely two-footed, which was rarer then than now; many of his best goals were struck left-footed. At 1.73 m he out-jumped much taller centre-backs by timing a run from behind them, and his heading was so reliable that Brazil's 1970 goal in the final came from exactly that.

His dribbling was built on balance rather than trickery: a low centre of gravity, a sudden change of pace, and a body feint that would send defenders the wrong way while the ball barely moved. The 1958 final goal and the dummy on the Uruguayan goalkeeper Mazurkiewicz in the 1970 semi-final, where he let the ball run one side of the keeper and went round the other without touching it, show the same mind at work: he saw the solution a beat earlier than anyone else. He was also a willing passer; the 1970 team's fluency owed much to his willingness to play a one-touch lay-off and run again.

Weaknesses are hard to find without reaching, but there are two honest ones. He was not a defender of any kind, and Brazil built sides that accepted that. And the physical attention he attracted, in an era before cards were handed out for persistent fouling, meant his career contains long injury gaps. If he were transplanted into today's game, with its protection for attackers and its emphasis on pressing from the front, you would probably see him as a free-roaming number 10 or a second striker in a 4-4-2 shape, somewhere between what [Harry Kane](/players/harry-kane) does when he drops and what Messi did when he ran at defences. His pace and leap would hold up; his decision-making would thrive in the extra space that modern pitches and rules allow.

## Evolution

The teenager of 1958 was a sprinter and a goal-hanger, played high up the pitch so that the explosive first step did the work. By the early 1960s at Santos he was the hub of everything, taking free-kicks and penalties, dropping to collect and carrying the ball 40 metres. After 1966 and the beatings he took, he adapted again: he kept the ball less, released it earlier, and learned to pick his moments to accelerate. The 1970 Pelé is a thinking player, almost a conductor, who saves his running for the final third and scores from set moves and headers.

The game changed around him too. The W-M and the 4-2-4 of his early years gave way to the 4-3-3 of 1970 and then to the [total football](/tactics/total-football) that [Johan Cruyff](/players/johan-cruyff) would make famous in 1974, by which time Pelé had retired from Brazil. His final chapter, with the New York Cosmos from 1975 to 1977, was as much evangelism as football: he filled Giants Stadium, won the Soccer Bowl in his last season, and planted the seed of the [United States](/nations/united-states) game that would grow into [MLS](/leagues/mls).

## Legacy

Pelé made the number 10 shirt a title rather than a position; everyone who has worn it since, from [Zico](/players/zico) and Maradona to Messi and Neymar, has been measured against him. He did more than anyone to turn Brazil's style, the jogo bonito of dribbling and improvisation, into a national brand, and the 1970 side he led remains the reference for every coach who believes attacking football can win the biggest prize. Managers from Mário Zagallo, who coached him in 1970, to [Carlo Ancelotti](/managers/carlo-ancelotti), who now coaches Brazil, speak of that team as the model of balance between freedom and structure.

He was the first global football celebrity, the face FIFA used to sell the game in Asia and North America, and the man whose move to New York made professional football plausible in the United States. He died on 29 December 2022, a week after Messi lifted the World Cup, which felt like a handover. For [Brazil](/nations/brazil) he remains simply O Rei, the King, and the debate about who comes after him has never produced a consensus about who came before.
