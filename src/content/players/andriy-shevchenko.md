## Overview

Andriy Shevchenko scored 175 goals for [AC Milan](/clubs/ac-milan), won the 2003 Champions League and the 2004 Ballon d'Or, and is the all-time leading scorer for Ukraine, which he later managed to the Euro 2020 quarter-finals. He was the first great forward to emerge from the post-Soviet game and the striker whose Champions League hat-trick at the Camp Nou in 1997 announced Dynamo Kyiv to the world.

## Early life and breakthrough

Born in Dvirkivshchyna near Kyiv in September 1976, Shevchenko was nine when the Chernobyl disaster forced his family to evacuate, and he was initially rejected by Dynamo Kyiv's youth school for failing a dribbling test. He was accepted on a second look and came through under Valeriy Lobanovskyi, the great systems coach, who returned to Dynamo in 1997 and built a counter-attacking machine around Shevchenko and Serhiy Rebrov.

Dynamo beat [Barcelona](/clubs/barcelona) 3-0 in Kyiv and 4-0 at the Camp Nou in the 1997-98 Champions League, Shevchenko scoring a first-half hat-trick in Spain. The next season he scored ten more and Dynamo reached the semi-finals, beating [Real Madrid](/clubs/real-madrid) on the way. He was 22 when Milan paid around $25 million in 1999.

## Peak years

Shevchenko won the Capocannoniere in his first Serie A season with 24 goals, the first foreign debutant to do so since 1950. Milan took time to build the team to match him. When [Carlo Ancelotti](/managers/carlo-ancelotti) took over in 2001 and installed [Andrea Pirlo](/players/andrea-pirlo) as a deep playmaker, with [Clarence Seedorf](/players/clarence-seedorf) and Rui Costa and then [Kaká](/players/kaka) behind the strikers, Shevchenko became the finishing point of a superb side.

In 2002-03 he scored the winner in the semi-final second leg against Inter and the decisive penalty in the final shoot-out against [Juventus](/clubs/juventus) at Old Trafford. In 2003-04 he scored 24 league goals as Milan won the Scudetto and won the Ballon d'Or, the third Ukrainian after Oleg Blokhin and Igor Belanov. He scored in the 2005 Champions League final against [Liverpool](/clubs/liverpool) in Istanbul but had his penalty saved by Jerzy Dudek in the shoot-out after the 3-3 collapse.

## How he played

Shevchenko was a runner first. He made diagonal runs from the left channel across the centre-backs, timed to the moment Pirlo or Seedorf looked up, and finished with either foot, often early and low across the goalkeeper. He struck the ball with a short, powerful backswing and was dangerous from 25 yards; many of his best goals were hit from outside the box.

He was composed one-on-one, with the dink and the placed finish both available, and was a reliable penalty taker until the two famous misses. He headed well for his height and worked hard without the ball in Lobanovskyi's tradition. He was not a creator, and when a team needed him to drop and link, as [José Mourinho](/managers/jose-mourinho)'s [Chelsea](/clubs/chelsea) did, he struggled.

## Evolution

At Dynamo he was a counter-attacking forward in a drilled collective, often starting wide. At Milan under Alberto Zaccheroni and Fatih Terim he was a goal machine in a 3-4-3, and under Ancelotti a centre-forward in a 4-3-1-2 with Filippo Inzaghi or Jon Dahl Tomasson beside him. The move to Chelsea in 2006 for £30 million, pushed by Roman Abramovich, came at 29 and never worked: 22 goals in three seasons, injuries, and a style mismatch with [Didier Drogba](/players/didier-drogba). A loan back to Milan in 2008-09 produced no league goals. He returned to Dynamo in 2009 and finished his career with Ukraine at Euro 2012 on home soil, scoring twice against Sweden at 35.

## Legacy

Shevchenko is Milan's second-highest scorer ever behind Gunnar Nordahl and the only Ukrainian to win the Ballon d'Or in the modern era. His 48 international goals are a national record and he led Ukraine as head coach from 2016 to 2021, reaching the quarter-finals of Euro 2020, before a short spell at Genoa. Since 2024 he has been president of the Ukrainian Football Association, a role made heavier by the war, and he has been a prominent voice for his country internationally. For a generation of eastern European forwards he was the proof that the route from Kyiv to the Ballon d'Or existed.
