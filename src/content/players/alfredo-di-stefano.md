## Overview

Alfredo Di Stéfano is the reason [Real Madrid](/clubs/real-madrid) think of themselves as the kings of Europe. When he arrived in 1953 the club had won two Spanish titles in its history. When he left in 1964 they had won eight more and the first five European Cups, and he had scored in every one of those five finals, including a hat-trick in the 7-3 win over Eintracht Frankfurt at Hampden Park that remains the most famous club match of the era. He was a centre-forward who played everywhere, a goalscorer who was also his team's best defender and playmaker, and the first footballer to be called "total" before the Dutch borrowed the word. [Johan Cruyff](/players/johan-cruyff) and [Pelé](/players/pele) both said he was the most complete player they had seen.

## Early life and breakthrough

Di Stéfano was born on 4 July 1926 in Barracas, Buenos Aires, to a family of Italian and French descent; his father had played for River Plate. He joined River's youth ranks at 17, debuted in 1945, and spent 1946 on loan at Huracán learning his trade before returning to lead River's famous forward line, La Máquina, in its final version. He scored 27 goals in 30 games as River won the 1947 title, and six in six for [Argentina](/nations/argentina) as they won the 1947 Copa América, his only international tournament for the country.

A players' strike in Argentina in 1949 pushed him to Colombia's outlaw league, the El Dorado of Bogotá's Millonarios, which operated outside FIFA and paid in dollars. He scored 90 goals in four seasons and the team, the Ballet Azul, toured Europe in 1952 to mark Real Madrid's 50th anniversary, beating the hosts 4-2 in Madrid. Both [Barcelona](/clubs/barcelona) and Madrid then tried to sign him, with River and Millonarios each claiming his registration. The Spanish federation's compromise, that he should alternate seasons between the clubs, collapsed when Barcelona withdrew, and he became a Madrid player in September 1953. Barcelona still regard the episode as a theft; Madrid regard it as the founding act of the modern club.

## Peak years

He scored four in his first Clásico, a 5-0, and Madrid won La Liga in 1954 for the first time in 21 years and again in 1955. Then the European Cup began, and Di Stéfano's team dominated it. Madrid beat Reims 4-3 in the 1956 final in Paris, with Di Stéfano scoring, and then Fiorentina in 1957 in Madrid, [AC Milan](/clubs/ac-milan) in 1958 after extra time, Reims again in 1959 and Frankfurt in 1960. Along the way the club added [Raymond Kopa](/players/raymond-kopa) in 1956 and [Ferenc Puskás](/players/ferenc-puskas) in 1958, and the forward line of Kopa, Rial, Di Stéfano, Puskás and Gento is the most celebrated of the 1950s.

He was Europe's top scorer or Pichichi five times, won the Ballon d'Or in 1957 and 1959 and was runner-up in 1956, the award's first year, behind [Stanley Matthews](/players/stanley-matthews). He also won the first Intercontinental Cup in 1960 against Peñarol. By then he was 34 and had acquired Spanish citizenship, playing 31 times for [Spain](/nations/spain) without ever appearing at a World Cup; injury kept him out of the 1962 squad. He played in the 1962 and 1964 European Cup finals, both lost, and after a fallout with Madrid's president Santiago Bernabéu he spent two seasons at Espanyol before retiring at 40.

## How he played

Di Stéfano wore the number 9 and finished like a centre-forward, but his team-mates described a player who would clear a corner from his own six-yard box, drive the ball 50 metres through midfield and then be on the end of the move. His running was his great weapon: he had the stamina to cover more ground than anyone else on the pitch and the discipline to use it in every phase. In possession he was a quick, accurate passer, constantly organising those around him, and he shot early and hard from range with either foot.

He was also a difficult, fierce competitor. He shouted at team-mates, argued with coaches and had a famous reluctance to share the spotlight; the story that he refused to let Puskás pass him for the Pichichi has been told both ways, with Puskás rolling the ball to him for the final goal of the 1959-60 season being the version Madrid prefer. Weaknesses: his willingness to drop deep meant Madrid sometimes lacked a presence in the box, which is where Puskás became essential, and his tactical control could crowd out younger players. He would translate to the modern game as something like a [false nine](/tactics/false-nine) with extraordinary engine, or a number 8 who scores 25 goals a season, a player in the mould of what a coach like [Luis Enrique](/managers/luis-enrique) asks of an all-action forward.

## Evolution

At River Plate he was a quick, direct striker in a five-man forward line. In Colombia he grew into a leader and started dropping deep to control matches that Millonarios dominated. At Madrid, especially after Puskás arrived to share the scoring, he became the team's playmaker as much as its finisher, operating from the centre circle forward and covering for a midfield that was not always strong. In his final Madrid seasons under Miguel Muñoz he played as a deep-lying forward in a side with Amancio and Gento, and at Espanyol he was close to a midfielder.

The football around him changed from the W-M of the 1940s to the 4-2-4 that [Brazil](/nations/brazil) used in 1958 and the 4-3-3 of the 1960s. Madrid's style under Di Stéfano, built on fast transition, individual quality and a star who could go anywhere, was the template for the counter-attacking European super-club, and its direct descendant is the Madrid of Cristiano Ronaldo.

## Legacy

Di Stéfano built the identity that [Real Madrid](/clubs/real-madrid) still sell: the European Cup as the club's property, the superstar signing as its method, the white shirt as a claim on history. Every Madrid forward since, from Amancio and Hugo Sánchez to Raúl, [Cristiano Ronaldo](/players/cristiano-ronaldo) and Kylian Mbappé, has been measured against his 308 goals, and the club's training ground stadium carries his name. He was voted the best European player of the half-century and awarded a Super Ballon d'Or in 1989 as the best of the award's first 30 years.

His style anticipated [total football](/tactics/total-football) by two decades: a forward who defended, a defender who attacked, positional freedom used in the service of the team. [Johan Cruyff](/players/johan-cruyff) called him the best he had seen, and Cruyff's own free role at Ajax owed something to the Argentine. He coached with less distinction but real success, winning La Liga with Valencia in 1971 and the Cup Winners' Cup with them in 1980, and Argentine titles with [Boca Juniors](/clubs/boca-juniors) and [River Plate](/clubs/river-plate). He became Madrid's honorary president and died in Madrid on 7 July 2014, three days after his 88th birthday. Alongside [Ferenc Puskás](/players/ferenc-puskas), the partner who stayed in the box, he remains the measure of the club's golden age.
