## Overview

Kenny Dalglish is the only player in these pages whom two of Britain's greatest clubs call their king. At [Celtic](/clubs/celtic) he won four Scottish titles and scored 167 goals; at [Liverpool](/clubs/liverpool), signed for a British record £440,000 to replace Kevin Keegan in 1977, he scored the winner in the 1978 European Cup final, won two more European Cups and six league titles, and in 1986 became the first player-manager to win the double. His partnership with [Ian Rush](/players/ian-rush) was the most productive in English football in the 1980s. His 102 caps for [Scotland](/nations/scotland) remain a record, and his 30 goals share the record with [Denis Law](/players/denis-law). And then, as a manager, he won the title with Liverpool three times and with Blackburn once.

## Early life and breakthrough

Dalglish was born in Dalmarnock in Glasgow's East End in March 1951 and grew up in Govan, a Rangers supporter with a view of Ibrox from his bedroom window. Rangers never asked. Celtic, under Jock Stein, signed him in 1967 and farmed him out to Cumbernauld United before giving him a debut in 1968. He played initially in midfield and was converted into a forward in 1971-72, when he scored 23 league goals in a championship season.

He won four league titles and four Scottish Cups with Celtic, played in the 1974 World Cup, and by 1977, when he had scored 167 goals in 322 games and was 26, had outgrown Scottish football. Bob Paisley, who had lost Keegan to Hamburg, paid a record fee and told the press that Liverpool had bought "the best player in Britain".

## Peak years

He scored seven minutes into his Liverpool league debut at Middlesbrough and finished the 1977-78 season by dinking the winning goal over Club Brugge's goalkeeper at Wembley to retain the European Cup. He was Footballer of the Year in 1979 and 1983, the latter season also bringing the PFA award. Rush arrived in 1980 and the two worked out a shorthand: Dalglish dropped off, turned, and played the ball into the space Rush was already running into.

Liverpool won the European Cup again in 1981 in Paris and 1984 in Rome, with Dalglish the creator rather than the scorer. He was present at Heysel in 1985, when 39 people died before the final against [Juventus](/clubs/juventus), and that summer, with Joe Fagan retiring, he became player-manager at 34. His first season produced the double, sealed by his own goal at Chelsea in the final league match. He played less thereafter, managing the club through the Hillsborough disaster of 1989, in which 97 Liverpool supporters died and after which he attended funeral after funeral, and resigned in February 1991, exhausted. He won the Premier League with Blackburn in 1995 and returned to manage Liverpool again in 2011-12, winning the League Cup.

## How he played

Dalglish was not quick, not tall and not a dribbler. What he had was a body built for shielding the ball, a first touch that put it exactly where his next action needed it, and a picture of the pitch that teammates said was uncanny. He played as a withdrawn striker in a 4-4-2, dropping between the opponent's defence and midfield to receive on the half-turn. With his backside out and arms wide, he was almost impossible to dispossess, and from that position he either turned and shot, early and placed, or slid passes into the channels for Rush.

Paisley said he was the best at "seeing a picture" he had ever had. Graeme Souness said he never saw him waste a pass. He scored 172 Liverpool goals largely from inside the box, with a notably quick release: rather than taking a touch to set himself, he hit the ball the moment it was playable, catching goalkeepers still moving.

Weaknesses: he lacked pace to beat a defender in a straight line and was at his best only when the team's movement around him was coordinated, which at Liverpool it always was. Today he would be a false nine or a second striker in a 4-4-2 or 4-2-3-1, with echoes in Roberto Firmino's hold-up play at the same club and Harry Kane's dropping deep.

## Evolution

Dalglish began as a midfielder and became a forward; began as a goalscorer at Celtic and became a creator at Liverpool; and ended as a player-manager who used himself sparingly as a substitute to control the tempo of matches. The game around him moved from the physical, direct British football of the early 1970s to Liverpool's pass-and-move possession game, which was itself a British adaptation of continental ideas, and he was the player who made the link between those two worlds. His later management emphasised the same qualities, with the 1988 Liverpool side of John Barnes and Peter Beardsley regarded as the most fluent in the club's history.

## Legacy

Dalglish is the bridge between [Liverpool](/clubs/liverpool)'s past and its present: the signing who proved Keegan could be replaced, the player-manager who built the best side the club had had, the man who held the city together after Hillsborough and who returned to steady the club in 2011. The Kenny Dalglish Stand at Anfield was named in 2017 and he was knighted in 2018. As a player he defined the deep-lying forward for British football decades before the [false nine](/tactics/false-nine) became a coaching term, and his partnership with [Ian Rush](/players/ian-rush) is the model every two-striker system is measured against. [Alex Ferguson](/managers/alex-ferguson), his great rival at Manchester United, called him the best British player of his lifetime. At [Celtic](/clubs/celtic) he remains the standard for home-grown forwards, and for [Scotland](/nations/scotland) he is simply the most-capped player and the joint top scorer, a status that half a century of successors have not threatened.
