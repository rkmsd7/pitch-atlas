## Overview

Duncan Edwards is the great unfinished story of English football. He made his [Manchester United](/clubs/manchester-united) debut at 16, was an [England](/nations/england) international at 18, had won two league titles by 20, and died at 21 from injuries suffered in the Munich air disaster of 6 February 1958. In 177 games for United he established himself, by the testimony of everyone who saw him, as the best young player the country had produced, and [Bobby Charlton](/players/bobby-charlton), who survived the crash and went on to win the [World Cup](/tournaments/world-cup) and the European Cup, insisted for the rest of his life that Edwards was the only player who had ever made him feel inferior. Matt Busby called him "the most complete footballer in Britain, possibly the world".

## Early life and breakthrough

Edwards was born in Dudley, in the Black Country, in October 1936, and was a schoolboy prodigy of a kind that attracted every club in England. He captained England Schoolboys and chose United, where Busby was building a team of teenagers, in 1952. Legend has it that Busby's assistant Jimmy Murphy was so anxious to beat Wolves to his signature that he drove through the night to get Edwards' signature at two in the morning.

He made his first-team debut against Cardiff in April 1953 at 16 years and 185 days, the youngest player in First Division history at the time. He was already 1.80 m tall and built like a heavyweight, and he played with such command that by the 1953-54 season he was a regular. He won three straight FA Youth Cups, and in April 1955, at 18 years and 183 days, he became England's youngest post-war international, against Scotland at Wembley. England won 7-2.

## Peak years

Edwards' two full seasons, 1955-56 and 1956-57, brought United consecutive league titles, the second by eight points with a team whose average age was 22. He played at left half in a 2-3-5 that was, in practice, a fluid attacking system in which he was the engine, defending the left side of midfield, launching attacks and arriving in the box. In 1956 he scored a famous goal against West Germany, the world champions, in Berlin, running 30 yards through the middle of the field and hitting a shot so hard that the German press named him "Boom Boom".

In 1957 United reached the FA Cup final, losing to Aston Villa after goalkeeper Ray Wood was injured, and the European Cup semi-final, losing to [Real Madrid](/clubs/real-madrid). The following season they were in the quarter-finals, drawn against Red Star Belgrade, and had drawn 3-3 in Belgrade when the chartered plane stopped to refuel at Munich. On the third take-off attempt in slush it crashed through a fence. Edwards suffered multiple fractures and severe kidney damage. He fought for fifteen days, asking at one point what time the kick-off was against Wolves because "I mustn't miss that match", and died on 21 February 1958.

## How he played

Edwards was, in modern terms, a complete box-to-box midfielder with the defensive capacity of a centre-back and the shooting of a striker. He was naturally two-footed, able to hit 40-yard passes off either, and his physical power let him break tackles rather than go round them. Jimmy Murphy said he had no weaknesses to coach. He headed the ball with the authority of a centre-half, filled in there when needed, and played centre forward for England Under-23s, scoring regularly.

What distinguished him from the many strong wing-halves of the era was intelligence. He read the game early, positioned himself so that the ball came to him, and chose the simple pass when the simple pass was right. He was reportedly serene in big matches to the point of appearing bored. His rare fault, if it was one, was a tendency to try to win matches alone when things went wrong, carrying the ball through four tackles instead of releasing it.

In today's game he would be a number 8 or a defensive-minded number 6 with the ball-carrying of a Steven Gerrard and the frame of a Yaya Touré. Analysts who have studied the surviving footage note how much ground he covered and how often he was the deepest and then the highest United player within the same attack, something that would now be called a "free eight".

## Evolution

Edwards did not live long enough to evolve. What can be said is how the game around him changed. The Busby Babes were themselves a revolution: a club that trusted its youth system rather than the transfer market, playing a fluid, attacking style while most English teams still played rigid W-M. Busby's decision to enter the European Cup against the wishes of the Football League in 1956 made United the first English club to take continental football seriously. The Munich disaster, which killed eight players and 23 people in all, altered the course of English football, and the rebuilt United that won the European Cup in 1968 under Busby was built in the memory of the team that died.

## Legacy

Edwards' legacy is partly a matter of testimony, because so little footage survives, and the testimony is unanimous. [Bobby Charlton](/players/bobby-charlton), [Bobby Moore](/players/bobby-moore) and Terry Venables all said he would have captained England at the 1966 World Cup; [Stanley Matthews](/players/stanley-matthews) called him "a rock in a raging sea". He remains the measure against which every [Manchester United](/clubs/manchester-united) youth product, from [George Best](/players/george-best) to Ryan Giggs and the Class of '92 to Marcus Rashford, is weighed. Two stained-glass windows in St Francis's Church in Dudley depict him, a statue stands in the town centre, and United's youth philosophy, the belief that a club can raise its own champions, is as much his memorial as anything the club has built in brick. He was 21.
