## Overview

Roberto Carlos spent eleven seasons at [Real Madrid](/clubs/real-madrid), played 527 matches, won four La Liga titles and three [Champions Leagues](/leagues/champions-league), and remains the club's most-capped foreign player of his era. He was a cornerstone of the Galácticos alongside [Zinedine Zidane](/players/zinedine-zidane), [Luis Figo](/players/luis-figo), [Raúl](/players/raul), [Ronaldo](/players/ronaldo-nazario) and [David Beckham](/players/david-beckham), and the left-back of [Brazil](/nations/brazil)'s 2002 [World Cup](/tournaments/world-cup) winners. For most of the public he is also one free kick: the swerving 35-yarder against [France](/nations/france) at Le Tournoi in June 1997 that physicists still write papers about.

## Early life and breakthrough

Born in Garça, São Paulo state, Roberto Carlos worked in a textile factory as a boy and started as a winger and then a left midfielder at União São João. Palmeiras signed him in 1993 and he won two Brasileirão titles, playing in a formidable side with [Rivaldo](/players/rivaldo) and [Cafu](/players/cafu). [Inter](/clubs/inter-milan) bought him in 1995, but Roy Hodgson played him in midfield and the relationship never clicked. Fabio Capello saw a left-back and took him to Madrid for a modest fee in 1996. He won La Liga in his first season.

## Peak years

From 1997 to 2003 Roberto Carlos was the best attacking left-back in the world and part of the best side. He scored a 25-yard screamer in the 1998 Champions League semi-final against [Borussia Dortmund](/clubs/borussia-dortmund), provided the cross from which Predrag Mijatović won the final against [Juventus](/clubs/juventus), and scored in the 2000 run that ended with a 3-0 win over [Valencia](/clubs/valencia) in Paris. In 2002 came the Glasgow final: it was his looping, mishit lob that Zidane volleyed into the top corner against [Bayer Leverkusen](/clubs/bayer-leverkusen). The same summer he started every game as Brazil won the World Cup, scoring a free kick against China and providing the attacking width in Scolari's 3-5-2 that let Ronaldinho, Rivaldo and Ronaldo play.

He finished second in the 2002 Ballon d'Or, won his fourth league title in 2007 and left that summer for Fenerbahçe after 11 seasons.

## How he played

Roberto Carlos was 1.68 m with the thighs of a sprinter. His free kicks were struck with the outside of the left foot, hit with so much pace and sidespin that the ball swerved late and viciously; the France goal started a yard outside the post and bent in. From open play he favoured the early shot, the chest-and-volley and the cross-field diagonal to the right wing. He had a quick first touch and an unusually good chest control for a defender.

Defensively he was fast enough to recover from almost any position, and his low centre of gravity made him hard to barge off the ball. He was a weak marker in his own box, prone to ball-watching, and his positioning was a running complaint: Vicente del Bosque built Madrid's shape partly around covering for him with Claude Makélélé and the centre-backs. He was not a tackler in the English sense and was outplayed by orthodox wingers who went outside him. Against [Portugal](/nations/portugal) and [Germany](/nations/germany) he had difficult nights.

## Evolution

Under Capello he was asked to defend first; under Jupp Heynckes, John Toshack, Del Bosque and later [Carlo Ancelotti](/managers/carlo-ancelotti) he had ever-greater licence to attack as midfielders like Makélélé, Fernando Redondo and Iván Helguera screened behind him. His decline at Madrid coincided with the post-Makélélé imbalance of 2003-06, when the team's defensive frailties were partly his own. In Turkey, Brazil and Russia he played deeper and sometimes as a centre-back in a back three, relying on reading rather than running. He retired in 2012 and became assistant and then briefly head coach at Anzhi, with later spells coaching in Turkey and ambassadorial work at Madrid.

## Best plays and moments

- 1997, Le Tournoi, France v Brazil: the free kick from 35 metres that bends around the wall and in off the post ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/results?search_query=Roberto+Carlos+free+kick+France+1997)).
- 1998, La Liga, Real Madrid v Tenerife: a near-impossible-angle strike from the left touchline ([watch](https://www.youtube.com/results?search_query=Roberto+Carlos+goal+Tenerife+1998)).
- 1998, Champions League semi-final v Borussia Dortmund: a 25-yard rocket at the Bernabéu.
- 1998, Champions League final v Juventus: the cross that led to Mijatović's winning goal, Madrid's first European Cup in 32 years.
- 2000, Champions League final v Valencia: a second European title in Paris.
- 2002, Champions League final v Bayer Leverkusen: the overhit cross that Zidane volleyed into history at Hampden Park.
- 2002, World Cup, Brazil v China: a swerving free kick in a 4-0 group win.
- 2002, World Cup final v Germany: 90 minutes at left wing-back, a second star for Brazil.
- 2003, Copa del Rey, Real Madrid v Barcelona: a long-range strike in a Clásico.
- 2006, World Cup quarter-final v France: the much-replayed moment, tying his bootlaces as Thierry Henry scored.

## Legacy

Roberto Carlos has a reasonable claim to be the most famous full-back who ever lived. The 1997 free kick has been studied in fluid dynamics papers and remains a reference point for anyone who strikes a dead ball. His influence is on the attacking expectations of the position: [Marcelo](/players/marcelo), his successor at Madrid, modelled himself on him, and the idea that a full-back should score from distance and create as many chances as a winger is his. Brazil built their 2002 World Cup win on his and Cafu's flanks. He was FIFPro World XI member in 2005 and is a fixture at Real Madrid legends events, regularly recalled in debates over the club's greatest XI.
