## Overview

Ruud Gullit was the most versatile great player of his generation: a libero at Feyenoord, a goalscoring midfielder at [PSV](/clubs/psv-eindhoven), a right-sided forward and then a central creator at [AC Milan](/clubs/ac-milan), and a sweeper again at [Chelsea](/clubs/chelsea) as player-manager. He captained the [Netherlands](/nations/netherlands) to Euro 88, their only major title, scoring the opening goal of the final, and won the 1987 Ballon d'Or, which he dedicated to Nelson Mandela. With [Marco van Basten](/players/marco-van-basten) and [Frank Rijkaard](/players/frank-rijkaard) he formed the Dutch trio at the heart of Arrigo Sacchi's Milan, the team that reshaped European football.

## Early life and breakthrough

Gullit was born in Amsterdam to a Surinamese father and Dutch mother and grew up playing street football in the Jordaan. He was rejected by [Ajax](/clubs/ajax) as a boy and signed for Haarlem, where he made his professional debut at 16 under Barry Hughes in 1979, initially as a centre-back and then as a forward. He joined Feyenoord in 1982, playing alongside [Johan Cruyff](/players/johan-cruyff) in his final season, and won the 1984 double as a libero who scored 10 league goals and drove forward from the back.

PSV signed him in 1985 and moved him into midfield, where he scored 46 goals in 68 games across two title-winning seasons. His Netherlands debut came in 1981 against Switzerland at 19. In 1987 Milan paid a world-record fee of roughly 18 million guilders for him.

## Peak years

His first Milan season, 1987-88, ended with the Scudetto, with Gullit scoring nine goals from the right of Sacchi's 4-4-2 while Van Basten was injured. Euro 88 followed: Gullit, as captain, headed in Van Basten's cross to open the 2-0 final win against the USSR and lifted the trophy in Munich. In 1988-89 Milan won the European Cup, beating [Real Madrid](/clubs/real-madrid) 5-0 in the semi-final second leg and Steaua Bucharest 4-0 in the final, where Gullit and Van Basten scored two each. He had returned from knee surgery barely a month earlier.

Milan retained the trophy in 1990, beating [Benfica](/clubs/benfica) 1-0 in Vienna, and won two Intercontinental Cups. Under Fabio Capello the club went unbeaten in 1991-92 and won the title again in 1993, though Gullit's knees now limited him. Loaned to Sampdoria in 1993-94 and given a free role by Sven-Göran Eriksson, he scored 15 goals, including the winner against Milan, and was recalled, before falling out with Capello and returning to Genoa. He also fell out with Dick Advocaat and walked out of the Dutch squad before the 1994 World Cup.

## How he played

Gullit was a total footballer in the literal Dutch sense: he had been trained in every position and could hold any of them at the highest level. His physical profile was remarkable, 1.91 m with the pace of a winger, and he used it to run through midfield in long diagonal surges, taking the ball with him. In the air he was almost unplayable; he scored in the Euro 88 final and in both European Cup finals with headers or aerial finishes.

In Sacchi's system he was a wide forward who tucked in to overload central areas and press, a prototype of the inverted wide player. His passing was precise over distance and he could play the final ball. Weaknesses: his knees, operated on three times, took away his explosive running after 1989, and he was never a natural dribbler in tight spaces, preferring to beat players with power. He could be temperamental with coaches. In today's game he would be a rangy box-to-box eight or a right-sided forward in a [gegenpressing](/tactics/gegenpressing) side, a bigger version of what [Jude Bellingham](/players/jude-bellingham) offers.

## Evolution

Gullit went from defender to midfielder to forward and back to defender over 20 years. The physical decline of his knees forced a tactical evolution: by Sampdoria and Chelsea he was a deep playmaker or sweeper who used positioning instead of pace. At Chelsea, signed by Glenn Hoddle in 1995 and made player-manager in 1996, he brought continental methods to [England](/nations/england), signed Gianfranco Zola, Roberto Di Matteo and Gianluca Vialli, and won the 1997 FA Cup, the first foreign manager to win a major English trophy. He was sacked in February 1998, later managed Newcastle, Feyenoord, LA Galaxy and Terek Grozny, and became a broadcaster.

The game changed around him from Dutch [total football](/tactics/total-football), which produced him, to Sacchi's pressing, which he enabled, to the Premier League's globalisation, which he helped start.

## Legacy

Gullit's Milan is the most influential club side between Cruyff's Ajax and Guardiola's Barcelona. [Arrigo Sacchi](/managers/arrigo-sacchi)'s zonal pressing, high line and compact 4-4-2 relied on athletes like Gullit who could cover ground and still create, and every pressing coach since, from [Jürgen Klopp](/managers/jurgen-klopp) to [Mikel Arteta](/managers/mikel-arteta), works in its shadow. The Dutch trio also broke the mould of foreign signings in Italy, proving three imports could define a club rather than decorate it.

He was the first black captain to lift a major international trophy for a European nation, and his Ballon d'Or dedication to Mandela made him a political figure at a time when few players spoke. At Chelsea he started the club's foreign revolution before Roman Abramovich, and the Premier League's taste for continental managers runs through him to [Xabi Alonso](/managers/xabi-alonso) today. For players, his multipositional profile is the one modern academies now try to produce.
