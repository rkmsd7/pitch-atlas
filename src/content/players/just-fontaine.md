## Overview

Just Fontaine's name is attached to a single number, 13, the goals he scored in six matches at the 1958 World Cup in Sweden. Nobody has come within two of it since, and in an era of 48-team tournaments and deep squad rotation the record looks less likely to fall with every edition. Yet Fontaine was far more than one summer. He scored 122 goals in 131 league games for the great [Reims](/clubs/reims) side of the late 1950s, won four French titles, reached a European Cup final against [Real Madrid](/clubs/real-madrid), and averaged more than a goal a game for [France](/nations/france) (30 in 21 caps). A broken leg ended his career at 28, which is the great "what if" of French football before [Michel Platini](/players/michel-platini).

## Early life and breakthrough

Fontaine was born in Marrakech in August 1933 to a French father and a Spanish mother, and grew up in Casablanca, where he played for USM Casablanca in the Moroccan league. His scoring there, more than 60 goals in three seasons, brought him to Nice in 1953. He won the Coupe de France in his first season and the league title in 1956, and his movement in and around the box was already drawing comparisons with the best in Europe.

The decisive move came in 1956. Reims had just lost Raymond Kopa to Real Madrid after the first European Cup final, and coach Albert Batteux needed a focal point. Fontaine arrived for a modest fee and scored 34 league goals in 1957-58 as Reims won the double. His partnership with the returning Kopa, who rejoined Reims in 1959, would become the defining attacking pairing of French football's first golden age.

## Peak years

The 1958 World Cup was the summit. Fontaine was not even France's first-choice striker when the squad was named; René Bliard's injury in a warm-up match opened the door. He scored a hat-trick against Paraguay in a 7-3 win, two against Yugoslavia, one against Scotland, two against Northern Ireland in the quarter-final, one against a Brazil side containing the 17-year-old [Pelé](/players/pele) in the semi-final, and four in the 6-3 third-place win over West Germany. He played the whole tournament in borrowed boots, having split his own on the eve of the opening match.

Back at Reims he kept scoring. He was the league's top scorer in 1958 and 1960, won the title in 1960 and 1962, and led the line in the 1959 European Cup final in Stuttgart, which Reims lost 2-0 to a Real Madrid side with Kopa and [Alfredo Di Stéfano](/players/alfredo-di-stefano). In 1958 he was third in the Ballon d'Or behind Kopa and Helmut Rahn. A double fracture of his leg in March 1960, and a second break on his return in 1961, finished him. He retired in 1962 at 28.

## How he played

Fontaine was a pure penalty-box striker, but of a more mobile kind than the target men of his day. He was short and powerfully built, with a sprinter's acceleration that let him attack the space behind defenders who, in the late 1950s, still largely marked zonally and dropped deep. His defining skill was finishing on the first touch with either foot. Eight of his 13 goals in Sweden came from inside the six-yard box or close to it, the product of arriving a half-second before the defender rather than beating him.

His relationship with Kopa was the tactical heart of both Reims and France. Kopa dropped off the front, drew the centre-halves out, and threaded passes into the channels for Fontaine's diagonal runs. Fontaine rarely dropped to link play himself; he trusted the playmaker to find him. That made him simple to describe and difficult to stop.

Weaknesses were the ones you would expect: he contributed little outside the box, offered no aerial dominance despite a decent leap, and his game depended on a creator of Kopa's quality. In the modern game he would be a classic number nine in a side built to feed one, in the mould of [Gerd Müller](/players/gerd-muller) or a sharper Filippo Inzaghi, and his two-footedness and willingness to run the channels would translate well into a pressing system.

## Evolution

Fontaine's career spanned the shift from the W-M formation to the 4-2-4 that Brazil popularised at the 1958 tournament he lit up. He was, in effect, the last great centre forward of the old system, benefiting from defences that had not yet learned to play a flat back four with a spare man. The 1960s brought [catenaccio](/tactics/catenaccio) and the sweeper, and strikers who relied purely on timing behind the line found life harder. Fontaine never had to adapt; injury took that chapter away.

After retiring he coached France briefly in 1967, then PSG in 1973-74 and the Moroccan national team at the 1980 Africa Cup of Nations, and was the first president of the French players' union.

## Legacy

Fontaine's record is the obvious monument, and every Golden Boot winner since, from [Gerd Müller](/players/gerd-muller) in 1970 to Kylian Mbappé's 10 in 2026, has been measured against it. But his wider influence is on how [France](/nations/france) thinks about its strikers. The Kopa-Fontaine axis is the template later repeated by [Michel Platini](/players/michel-platini) and Dominique Rocheteau, by Zinedine Zidane and Thierry Henry, and by Antoine Griezmann and Olivier Giroud: a creator who drops and a finisher who runs. He is remembered at [Reims](/clubs/reims) alongside [Raymond Kopa](/players/raymond-kopa) as the symbol of the club's great decade, and in Morocco as the first world-class footballer the country produced, decades before its own national team reached a [World Cup](/tournaments/world-cup) semi-final. He died in Toulouse on 1 March 2023, aged 89.
