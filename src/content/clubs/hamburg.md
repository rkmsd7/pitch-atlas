## History

Hamburger SV trace their origins to 1887, making them one of the oldest clubs in Germany, and took their modern form in a 1919 merger. They won German championships in 1923, 1928 and 1960, the last with [Uwe Seeler](/players/uwe-seeler), the Hamburg-born centre-forward who played 476 league games for the club and never left despite an offer from Inter. Founder members of the Bundesliga in 1963, they were the only club to play every season of the league's first 55 years, a record commemorated by a clock in the Volksparkstadion that counted the minutes since 1963.

The great era came between 1976 and 1983. Kuno Klötzer's side won the 1977 Cup Winners' Cup against Anderlecht; Kevin Keegan arrived from Liverpool in 1977 and won two Ballons d'Or in Hamburg; Branko Zebec won the 1979 title; and Ernst Happel, with Horst Hrubesch, Felix Magath, Manfred Kaltz and the young Thomas von Heesen, won the Bundesliga in 1982 and 1983 and the 1983 European Cup, Magath's early goal beating Juventus 1-0 in Athens. They lost the 1980 European Cup final to Nottingham Forest and the 1982 UEFA Cup final to Gothenburg. The 1987 DFB-Pokal was the last major trophy.

The 1990s and 2000s were respectable: Thomas Doll and Jörg Albertz in the early 1990s, Sergej Barbarez and Rafael van der Vaart in the 2000s, third place in 2006 and consecutive European semi-finals in 2009 (UEFA Cup, lost to Werder) and 2010 (Europa League, lost to Fulham), with Ruud van Nistelrooy in the side. Then came a decade of mismanagement: a dozen coaches, expensive flops, play-off survivals in 2014 and 2015 (Marcelo Díaz's 115th-minute free kick against Karlsruhe) and finally, on 12 May 2018, relegation for the first time, with the clock stopped at 54 years, 261 days.

## Trajectory

The fall was the story of the 2010s and the climb back the story of the 2020s. HSV finished fourth in the 2. Bundesliga four years running (2019-21) and third twice more, losing play-offs to Hertha in 2022 and Stuttgart in 2023, a sequence of near misses that became a running joke in German football. Merlin Polzin, promoted from assistant in November 2024, finally took them up as runners-up to Köln in 2024-25, and the promotion party on 10 May 2025 after a 6-1 win over Ulm spilled onto the pitch. The first season back, 2025-26, was a scrap for survival that ended successfully, and Polzin remains in charge for 2026-27.

The mismanagement of the 2010s deserves its own paragraph because it is the reason the clock stopped. Between 2008 and 2018 Hamburg employed 18 head coaches, spent heavily on players such as Rafael van der Vaart's return, Hakan Çalhanoğlu, Filip Kostić and Walace, and repeatedly changed sporting directors. The investor Klaus-Michael Kühne financed much of it and criticised most of it publicly. The result was two play-off survivals, a 17th-place finish in 2018 and seven years of second-division football, during which the club's turnover stayed higher than that of half the Bundesliga and its attendances above 50,000.

## Players the club produced

[Uwe Seeler](/players/uwe-seeler), the symbol of the club and of post-war German football, came through the youth system in the 1950s. The academy has since produced Manfred Kaltz, Thomas von Heesen, Jonathan Tah, who left for Leverkusen at 19 and later Bayern, Gideon Jung, Fiete Arp and Josha Vagnoman. Among the signings who became legends: Keegan, Hrubesch (the "Kopfball-Ungeheuer", the heading monster), Magath, van der Vaart in two spells, Barbarez and goalkeeper René Adler. Son Heung-min made his professional debut for Hamburg in 2010 and left for Leverkusen in 2013, the club's most lucrative recent academy product.

The promotion side was built around striker Robert Glatzel, Ransford Königsdörffer, goalkeeper Daniel Heuer Fernandes and the wingers Jean-Luc Dompé and Miro Muheim.

## Identity and playing style

HSV's historic identity is the Happel side: physically imposing, disciplined, built on Hrubesch's headers from Kaltz's "Bananenflanken" crosses, and the "Hamburg school" of hard-running, well-organised football. The modern club has searched for an identity through dozens of coaches. Merlin Polzin, at 35 among the youngest coaches in the league, plays a 4-2-3-1 with a direct, [counter-attacking](/tactics/counter-attacking) emphasis, [wing play](/tactics/wing-play) through Dompé and Muheim and a compact mid-block, the pragmatic approach that got the club promoted and kept it up.

## Rivalries

The **Nordderby** against [Werder Bremen](/clubs/werder-bremen) is the great northern rivalry, two Hanseatic cities 120 kilometres apart who have played each other more than 100 times in the Bundesliga; HSV's 2009 UEFA Cup semi-final defeat to Werder on away goals and Werder's 2021 relegation to join them in the second division are the modern chapters. The Hamburg city derby against St Pauli is the sharper local hatred, the establishment club against the left-wing, counter-cultural one, and the two were in the Bundesliga together in 2025-26 before St Pauli went down. [Bayern Munich](/clubs/bayern-munich) are the historic rival from the 1980s title duels and the 2001 last-day title decider.

## Current situation

As of 7 November 2026 Hamburg are eighth in the Bundesliga on 12 points from nine games, with four wins and five defeats and no draws, and a goal difference of minus ten that reflects some heavy defeats. After losing 1-0 at Paderborn on 24 October and 3-1 at Frankfurt on 1 November, they beat Gladbach 3-1 at the Volksparkstadion on 6 November. The next fixture is the Nordderby at Werder on 22 November, followed by Bayern at home on 28 November.

Polzin's side are a step more secure than a year ago, with Glatzel still the goalscorer and the squad strengthened over two summers. The objective is a comfortable mid-table season and a settled identity after a decade of upheaval; the Volksparkstadion, sold out every week, is enjoying being back in the big league.
